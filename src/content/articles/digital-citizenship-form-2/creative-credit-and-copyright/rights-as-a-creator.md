# Lesson 17 — Rights as a Creator

*You already own things you didn't pay for. You just haven't noticed yet.*

Think about the last thing you made — a story you wrote, a photo you took, a video you
edited, a Minecraft build, a drawing in your sketchbook, a song you hummed into your
phone. Every one of those is a *creation*. And in this lesson, we're going to build
an understanding of something that belongs to you the moment you make anything: your
**rights as a creator**.

This isn't a lecture about rules. It's a chance to think hard about a question that
matters to every person who makes things: **who owns a creation, and what does that
mean?**

---

## You're already a creator

Let's start by building the most important idea: **you are not waiting to become a
creator. You already are one.**

The word "creator" might sound like it belongs to famous authors or film studios. It
doesn't. Under copyright law, the moment you create an original work, you own it —
whether you're 12 or 42, whether your work is a class essay or a blockbuster movie.

> **A creator** is anyone who makes an original work. Under copyright law, the
> creator is almost always the first owner of the work.

Here's a small list of things that count as original works — you'll notice how many
you've already made:

- a story you wrote in English class
- a photo you took on a school trip
- a video you made for a project
- a slideshow you designed
- artwork you posted online
- a song you recorded
- a game you designed
- a mod you built for a game
- a drawing based on a character you love

One more thing about that last one: if you draw a character *inspired by* your
favourite game but you add your own details, the drawing is *your own original work*.
The idea came from somewhere else, but your expression of it is yours.

---

## What is copyright, and when does it start?

**Copyright** is legal protection for an original creative work. It gives the owner
the right to control copying, sharing, and adapting that work.

Here is the part that surprises almost everyone: **copyright is automatic.** It
exists the moment your work is written down, drawn, photographed, recorded, typed, or
saved in a file.

Let's build that idea piece by piece:

1. **You create something.** Maybe you write a paragraph.
2. **You fix it** — that's a fancy way of saying you put it in a permanent form other
   people can see or hear: written on paper, saved as a file, recorded. In copyright
   law this moment is called *fixation*.
3. **Copyright begins.** That's it. No forms, no fees, no © symbol needed.

So here's a reality check for a common trap: **if there's no © symbol, the work is
still protected.** Copyright has been automatic in the US since 1989. The symbol is a
friendly signpost, not a requirement.

And another one: **if it's on the internet, it is not free to use.** Being easy to
copy has nothing to do with being legal to copy.

---

## What makes a work "original"?

"Original" doesn't mean "completely new idea that nobody ever had." It means you
created it yourself, independently, and it shows at least a small amount of
creativity.

That last part is important. Copyright does *not* protect:

- facts (the height of a mountain, the year a country was founded)
- ideas (the idea of a story about dragons)
- titles and short phrases
- simple lists

Copyright protects the **expression** of an idea — the *way* someone expresses it,
not the idea itself. Everyone in the world can write a story about dragons. Nobody
can copy the exact story you wrote.

---

## The bundle of rights

When you own the copyright to a work, you hold a **bundle** of exclusive rights. That
word "exclusive" is the key: they belong to you, and other people need your
permission to use them. Your bundle includes the right to:

- **make copies** of the work
- **distribute or sell** copies
- **perform or display** the work publicly
- **create derivative works** — new works based on yours, like remixes, translations,
  and adaptations

You don't have to think about these as an owner every day — but it's powerful to know
you have them.

One trap to notice here: **buying a copy doesn't buy the rights.** If you own a game
disc or a book or a music file, you own that copy, not the copyright. You can enjoy
your copy, but you still can't freely copy it or share it with everyone.

---

## How long does copyright last?

A very, very long time.

In most cases, copyright lasts for **the life of the author plus 70 years**. For
anonymous or corporate works, it can last **95 to 120 years**. For a student your age,
that means *anything created recently is almost certainly still protected*.

For school purposes, the safe way to think is: **treat almost everything as
protected.** The day will come when a work's protection expires — but that day is a
long way off for almost everything you'll meet this year.

> **Public domain** is the word for works with no copyright protection — either the
> protection expired, or the work was never protected in the first place (like facts,
> ideas, and many government works). Anyone may use public-domain works freely.

---

## Plagiarism vs. piracy: two different wrongs

Here's the part where we untangle two words that people mix up all the time. They
sound similar, but they are different kinds of wrongs — one is about **credit**, and
the other is about **permission**.

### Plagiarism — the wrong of *credit*

**Plagiarism** is claiming someone else's words or ideas as your own, or using their
work without giving credit.

- It is an **ethical and academic** offense — it's dishonest.
- It's *against the author* — you're taking the credit that belongs to them.
- It is **not itself illegal** (a crime), but school policies treat it as a serious
  offense with real consequences. In some settings, like academic publishing, it can
  even lead to legal trouble.

**Example:** A classmate takes the poster design you finished, changes the name, and
hands it in as their own. That's plagiarism — it's about taking credit, not money.

**Example:** You put a copied image into your school project without noting where it
came from. That's a form of plagiarism too, even if you didn't mean to do it.

### Piracy — the wrong of *permission*

**Piracy** (formally called **copyright infringement**) is copying, sharing, or
downloading a copyrighted work without the owner's permission.

- It is **against the law** — it's illegal, not just rude.
- The word "piracy" is informal, and people use it especially for movies, music,
  games, and software.
- It's *against the owner's rights* — the creator isn't being paid for their work, or
  isn't getting to choose how it's used.

**Example:** Downloading a new movie from a shady site instead of paying for it, or
copying a whole album and sharing it with friends. The creator doesn't get to decide,
and doesn't get paid.

### The quick difference

| | Plagiarism | Piracy / infringement |
|---|---|---|
| What is it about? | **Credit** — who gets the credit | **Permission** — who decides and gets paid |
| What kind of wrong? | Ethical / academic (dishonesty) | Legal (against the law) |
| Against whom? | The author | The rights holder |
| Real-world word | "I took credit" | "I copied it without permission" |

One note to keep in mind: sources use different words for the same legal idea —
"copyright infringement," "copyright theft," and "piracy" all describe unauthorised
copying. Whatever the word, the core question is the same: *did the creator give
permission?*

---

## Why do creators' rights matter?

Let's build the *why*, because it's more interesting than the rules.

Copyright lets creators **control how their work is used** and **get paid for it**.
That control is what gives people a reason to keep making new work. Think about it:
if you spent twenty hours making a song and anyone could take it without asking,
would you make another? Probably fewer songs.

But there's a second half to the balance. Copyright doesn't exist to lock culture
away forever. It balances the creator's interests **with the public's ability to
build on and enjoy culture**. That's why protection eventually ends, why facts and
ideas stay free, and why (as you'll see in the next two lessons) there are legal
paths to using protected work.

Your rights as a creator and your respect for others' work are two sides of the same
thing. When you understand what you own, you understand what everyone else owns too.

## Worked example: Ameera's photo

Let's practise thinking like a creator. Follow the story, and see if you can
anticipate what's happening at each step.

**Story:** On the school trip, Ameera takes a great photo of her class at the
botanical garden. She uploads it to her slideshow that night.

- **Step 1 — Fixation.** The moment Ameera presses the shutter and saves the file,
  copyright begins. She doesn't need to apply, pay, or add a symbol. *What just
  happened?* She became the owner of an original work.

- **Step 2 — The bundle.** Ameera's rights now include making copies, sharing the
  photo, showing it publicly, and making new works from it. *Who needs her
  permission?* Anyone who wants to copy or share the photo.

- **Step 3 — A classmate asks.** Jordan asks if he can put Ameera's photo in *his*
  presentation. If he just takes it and doesn't mention her, that's plagiarism — he's
  taking credit. If he asks, and Ameera says yes, he can use it with credit. *What's
  the difference?* Permission.

- **Step 4 — Someone else's photo.** For the same project, Ameera wants a picture of
  a rare plant she didn't get to see. She finds one online. If she copies it into her
  slideshow without noting where it came from, that's plagiarism. If she downloads
  the whole photo from a site that doesn't have rights to share it, that's piracy.

**Now you:** what's one original work *you've* created this term? What are three
things you could now tell someone about why it belongs to you?

## Myth vs. reality — build the truth yourself

For each statement below, decide whether it's true or a trap *before* reading the
answer. Cover the right-hand column and think first.

| Statement | Reality |
|---|---|
| "No © symbol means it's not protected." | **Trap.** Copyright is automatic. |
| "It's on the internet, so it's free to use." | **Trap.** Easy to copy ≠ legal to copy. |
| "I'm a kid, so I can't own copyright." | **Trap.** Anyone who creates an original work owns it, whatever their age. |
| "I have to register or pay a fee to own copyright." | **Trap.** Registration is optional; it just adds extra enforcement benefits. |
| "Plagiarism and piracy are the same crime." | **Trap.** Plagiarism is about credit (ethical); piracy is about permission (illegal). |
| "Copyright protects ideas." | **Trap.** It protects the *expression* of an idea, not the idea. |
| "I own the book, so I own the rights." | **Trap.** You own the copy, not the copyright. |
| "The older a work is, the more protected it is." | **Trap.** Reversed — old works can enter the public domain; newer works are protected. |

Did you catch yourself holding any of these? Good — that's the friction doing its
job. Noticing the trap is the first step to thinking like a creator.

## A note on different countries

The big ideas in this lesson — copyright is automatic, the creator is the first
owner, plagiarism and piracy are different — are shared around the world. But the
*details* of copyright law are local. US law uses "fair use" and life-plus-70 years;
UK, Australian, and Singaporean law use **"fair dealing"** and can have different
terms and exceptions.

So if you move between countries or read sources from different places, don't be
confused if the details don't line up. The safe, universal takeaway for your age
group stands: **copyright lasts a very long time — treat almost everything as
protected.**

## Key words you've built

- **Copyright** — the law that protects original creative work and gives its owner
  the right to control copying, sharing, and adapting it.
- **Creator / author** — the person who makes an original work; usually the first
  owner of the copyright.
- **Original work of authorship** — a work you create independently that shows at
  least a small amount of creativity.
- **Fixation** — the moment a work exists in a permanent form others can see or hear;
  this is when copyright begins.
- **Copyright owner** — the person or company who holds the rights and decides how
  the work is used.
- **Derivative work** — a new work based on an existing one, such as a translation,
  remix, or adaptation.
- **Plagiarism** — using someone else's work or ideas without credit, as if they were
  yours.
- **Piracy / copyright infringement** — copying, sharing, or downloading a
  copyrighted work without permission; it's illegal.
- **Intellectual property (IP)** — creations of the mind (art, writing, music,
  inventions, brand names) that can be owned and protected by law, including
  copyright.
- **Public domain** — works with no copyright protection; anyone may use them freely.

---

## The big idea to take with you

Understanding your rights as a creator isn't a rule to memorise — it's a tool you
build and carry. Next time you make something, you'll know that it's yours, the
moment you make it. And next time you use someone else's work, you'll know the two
questions that matter: *who gets the credit?* and *did the creator give permission?*
