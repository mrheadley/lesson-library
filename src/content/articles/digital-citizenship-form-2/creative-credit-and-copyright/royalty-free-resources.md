# Lesson 19 — Royalty-Free Resources and Citing

*Finding media you're allowed to use — and showing who made it.*

In the last two lessons you built two big ideas: you own your original work, and some
reuse of others' work can be fair. Now comes the practical, hands-on part: **finding
media you're actually allowed to use, and crediting the people who made it.**

There's a wonderful tool for this, and it sits right between "all rights reserved"
copyright and the public domain. It's called **Creative Commons**, and once you
understand it, a huge world of music, photos, and other media opens up to you —
legally, and with a simple way to say thank you.

---

## The problem Creative Commons solves

Here's the friction: when you make something, copyright is automatic (Lesson 17).
That means "all rights reserved" is the default — nobody can copy, share, or remix
your work without asking.

But what if you *want* people to use your work? What if a student in another country
wants to use your photo in their project? There's no world government for
permissions. Asking everyone individually is impossible.

**Creative Commons (CC)** solves this. It's a set of **standardised, free licences**
that creators can put on their work to say, in advance: *"you may use this — here are
the conditions."*

> **Creative Commons (CC)** — a nonprofit organisation, and a set of standard licences
> creators use to allow reuse of their work under clear conditions.

A **licence** is permission granted by the copyright owner to use a work in specific
ways. CC licences standardise that permission so everyone can understand it.

---

## Four conditions, six licences

All CC licences are built from **four conditions**. Once you know the conditions, you
can read any CC licence at a glance.

| Symbol idea | Condition | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| **BY** — Attribution | Always | Others may use the work **if they credit the creator.** |
| **SA** — ShareAlike | Sometimes | If you build on the work, your new version must be shared under the same (or a compatible) licence. |
| **NC** — NonCommercial | Sometimes | Use and remix allowed **only for noncommercial purposes.** |
| **ND** — NoDerivatives | Sometimes | Others may copy and share the work **but may not change or remix it.** |

These combine into the **six main CC licences**, from most open to most restricted:

1. **CC BY** — the most open. Others may copy, remix, and even use the work
   commercially, as long as they credit the creator. *Wikipedia's articles are under
   CC BY-SA, a close cousin.*
2. **CC BY-SA** — like CC BY, but if you build on the work, your new version must be
   shared under the same or a compatible licence. (That's why Wikipedia's articles
   stay open — improvements have to stay open too.)
3. **CC BY-ND** — others may copy and share the work as-is, but may **not** change or
   remix it. No cropping, no adding your own text.
4. **CC BY-NC** — use and remix allowed for **noncommercial** purposes only, with
   credit.
5. **CC BY-NC-SA** — combines NC and SA.
6. **CC BY-NC-ND** — the most restrictive. Reuse is limited to sharing unaltered
   work, noncommercially, with credit.

Here's the most important fact about the whole system: **EVERY one of the six
requires attribution.** Giving credit is not optional. There is no CC licence without
BY. If a work is under a CC licence, you *must* credit the creator — always.

---

## Royalty-free — a word that lies a little

You'll see the term **"royalty-free"** all over media sites. It sounds like "free to
use, no rules." Here's what it actually means:

> **Royalty-free** — a work you can use **without paying ongoing royalties** per use.
> Often there's a one-time fee, or it's free with terms. The site's licence terms
> still apply.

It does **not** mean free of all restrictions. "Royalty-free" and "copyright-free"
get mixed up all the time, but they're different things. Always read each site's
exact licence terms before you use something.

And one more trap: **images from Google are not free to use.** Search engines *find*
images; they don't own them. You must check the source and its licence before using
any image you found through a search.

---

## The public domain and CC0

Two more ideas round out the picture.

**Public domain** means *no copyright at all*. A work can be in the public domain
because its protection expired, or because it was never protected (facts, ideas, and
many government works). Anyone may use these works in any way.

**CC0** is a special tool from Creative Commons that isn't a licence — it's a
**public-domain dedication**. The creator gives up their rights entirely. No
attribution is legally required for CC0 works — though crediting is still good
practice, and it's a kind way to say thanks.

**Careful:** CC0 and CC BY are not the same. CC0 removes all rights (no attribution
required). CC BY keeps copyright but allows reuse with credit.

## Finding media you can use

Now the fun part — where to *find* CC and royalty-free media. Try these:

- **Openverse** — a search engine built specifically for CC-licensed images and audio.
- **Wikimedia Commons** — the huge media library behind Wikipedia, full of
  CC-licensed and public-domain images.
- **ccSearch** — searches across multiple CC-friendly collections.
- **Flickr / Google Images** — use their **usage-rights filters** to narrow results
  to works you can reuse. Remember: the filter helps, but you still confirm the
  licence on the original source page.
- **Free Music Archive** and **ccMixter** — music you can legally use in projects.
- **Sites like NASA** — many NASA images are in the public domain.
- **Your library's "open media" guides** — librarians love curating these.

One honest note: search tools that aggregate licences (like Openverse or Google's
filter) are helpful but not perfect. **Always confirm the licence on the original
source page** before you commit to using something.

## TASL — the four-part way to give credit

All six CC licences require attribution, and Creative Commons recommends a simple,
memorable format. It's called **TASL**:

> **T**itle — the name of the work
> **A**uthor — who made it
> **S**ource — where you found it (a link)
> **L**icence — which CC licence it's under (including the version)

Put the credit **near the work**: a caption under an image, end credits in a video.

**Example — an image in your project:**

> Photo "Sunrise over the Rift Valley" by Li Wei, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons.

That's the whole thing: title, author, source, licence. Now anyone who reads your
project can find the original and know exactly what they're allowed to do with it.

**Example — a track in your school video:**

> Song "Sunny Day" by OpenTunes, CC BY 4.0, Free Music Archive.

In the end credits, it fits in one line.

**One more tip:** name the exact licence version (say "CC BY 4.0", not just "CC BY").
Licences have versions, and the version matters. And never write "Creative Commons"
as the author — CC is the *organisation* that made the licences, not the person who
made the work. Credit the actual creator.

## Worked examples: build the credit yourself

Cover the right-hand side and build the TASL credit from the information given.

### Example 1 — The Free Music Archive track

You found a song called "Road Trip" by the artist Blue Kite on the Free Music Archive.
It's marked CC BY 3.0.

- **Title:** "Road Trip"
- **Author:** Blue Kite
- **Source:** Free Music Archive
- **Licence:** CC BY 3.0

**Your credit line:** *Song "Road Trip" by Blue Kite, CC BY 3.0, Free Music Archive.*

### Example 2 — The Wikipedia Commons photo

You need a photo of a volcano for a geography project. You find one on Wikimedia
Commons by a photographer named Mei Tan, marked CC BY-SA 4.0.

**Your credit line (as a caption):** *Photo "Kīlauea Eruption" by Mei Tan, CC BY-SA
4.0, via Wikimedia Commons.*

*Bonus question:* CC BY-SA means if you build a new work from this photo, your new
work must also be shared under the same or a compatible licence. Good to know before
you build.

### Example 3 — The NASA image

You want a rocket photo for your slideshow. NASA images are in the public domain.

**What's the rule?** You can use it without asking — no licence needed. But you still
label where it came from, because good practice outlasts legal requirements. *Credit
line: Photo, NASA / public domain.*

## Real-world decisions: which work fits?

For each project, pick the right resource and explain why. This is where the 
conditions become useful, not just rules.

**Situation A — Your homework project.** You want a photo of a coral reef for a
science report. You find a photo marked **CC BY-NC**.

You can use it in your homework — that's noncommercial. But you *cannot* use it in
something your school club would **sell** to raise money. That would be commercial
use, which NC forbids.

**Situation B — Your club's fundraising video.** Your club is selling a video to
raise money for the library. You want background music.

Skip any track marked **NC** — the sale makes it commercial. Look for **CC BY** or
**CC BY-SA** tracks (and check that the SA condition works for you), and put the
credit in the end credits.

**Situation C — The poster you're remixing.** You found the perfect poster and want
to crop it and add your own text.

If it's **CC BY-ND**, stop. ND means *no derivatives* — you may share it as-is, but
you may not crop it or cover it with text. Find another poster, or use it unchanged.

**Situation D — The classmate's drawing.** A friend drew a great character for your
game design project and said you can use it.

Credit them just like you'd credit any CC work — title, author, source (their name),
and the permission they gave you. Crediting a classmate with their permission is
exactly the same respect a CC licence requires.

## Myths vs. reality — build the truth yourself

Cover the right-hand column and decide the truth before you look.

| Statement | Reality |
|---|---|
| "Creative Commons means free, so there are no rules." | **Trap.** All six licences require attribution, and some ban commercial use or changes. |
| "No copyright symbol means it's in the public domain." | **Trap.** Copyright is automatic; the missing symbol means nothing. |
| "Images from Google are free to use." | **Trap.** Search engines find images; you must check source and licence. |
| "Royalty-free = free." | **Usually wrong.** It means no ongoing royalties; the site's terms still apply (and you may pay once). |
| "CC0 and CC BY are the same." | **Trap.** CC0 removes all rights; CC BY keeps copyright but allows reuse with credit. |
| "If I'm not making money, I don't need to cite." | **Trap.** Attribution is required regardless of profit. |
| "Public domain is only about expired copyright." | **Partly wrong.** It also includes works never protected — facts, ideas, many government works. |
| "I can credit 'Creative Commons' as the author." | **Trap.** CC is the organisation, not the author. Credit the actual creator. |

## A note on versions and licences

Two small things worth knowing so you're never confused by what you find online:

- **Licence versions exist.** You'll see CC 2.0, 3.0, and 4.0 out there. Name the
  exact version in your credit (e.g., "CC BY 4.0").
- **"Noncommercial" is defined as "not primarily intended for commercial
  advantage."** That sounds precise, but people interpret it differently in practice.
  When in doubt about NC, ask your teacher or pick a more open licence.

And remember: sites like Unsplash and Pixabay use their **own licences** (similar to
CC BY but with no attribution required) rather than CC licences. Read each site's
terms — "royalty-free," "copyright-free," and "CC-licensed" are related but never
identical.

## Key words you've built

- **Creative Commons (CC)** — a nonprofit organisation and a set of standard licences
  creators use to allow reuse of their work under clear conditions.
- **Attribution / credit** — acknowledging who made a work and where it came from;
  legally required under all CC licences.
- **Licence** — permission granted by the copyright owner to use a work in specific
  ways.
- **TASL** — the recommended CC credit format: Title, Author, Source, Licence.
- **Public domain** — works with no copyright protection; anyone can use them in any
  way.
- **CC0** — a "no rights reserved" public-domain dedication tool from Creative
  Commons.
- **Royalty-free** — a work you can use without paying ongoing royalties per use; the
  site's terms still apply.
- **Remix** — combining or altering existing work to create something new.
- **NC (NonCommercial)** — reuse allowed only for purposes not primarily for
  commercial gain.
- **SA (ShareAlike)** — adaptations must be released under the same or a compatible
  licence.

---

## The big idea to take with you

You don't have to choose between "ask permission for everything" and "take whatever
you want." Creative Commons gives you a third path: **use work that's offered for
reuse, and show who made it.** The habit of crediting — of saying someone's name
aloud and pointing to their work — is the habit that keeps the whole creative
community working together. Build it, and you'll both give and receive well.
