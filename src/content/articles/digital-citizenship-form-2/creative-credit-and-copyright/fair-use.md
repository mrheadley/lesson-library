# Lesson 18 — Fair Use: The Four Factors

*The answer to "can I use this?" is almost never a simple yes or no.*

In Lesson 17 you built an understanding of your rights as a creator. Now comes the
interesting part: what happens when *you* want to use someone else's work? Do you
need to ask permission every single time?

Mostly, yes — but there's an exception designed to keep creativity flowing. It's
called **fair use**, and it's judged by **four factors** that you weigh together.

Here's the truth about fair use that surprises everyone: **there is no single test
that gives a definite "yes" or "no."** Courts weigh four factors together, case by
case, so the honest answer to "is this fair use?" is usually *"it depends."*

That's not frustrating — that's the point. This lesson gives you the tools to
*argue* each factor thoughtfully, the way a real decision-maker would.

---

## What is fair use for?

Fair use lets people use copyright-protected work without permission in certain
situations. The classic ones are:

- **criticism** — commenting on a work
- **comment** — adding your own thoughts about it
- **news reporting**
- **teaching**
- **scholarship**
- **research**

Why does this exist? To protect freedom of expression and keep creativity flowing.
If you could never mention, quote, or respond to other people's work, making new
things would be much harder. Fair use is the legal room for that.

> **Fair use** — the US legal right to use a copyrighted work without permission in
> certain limited circumstances, judged by four factors.

---

## The four factors

Here they are, one at a time. They're not a checklist where you need three out of
four — they're four weights you place on a balance together.

### Factor 1 — Purpose and character of the use

*Why are you using it, and what are you adding?*

Courts look at two things here:

- **Commercial or not?** A use that makes money or serves a business gets less
  leeway. A nonprofit, educational use leans *toward* fair use.
- **Transformative or not?** Is your use **transformative** — does it add something
  new, with a different purpose or character than the original? Or are you just
  *replacing* the original?

A transformative, noncommercial use leans toward fair use. A use that just substitutes
for the original leans against it.

> **Transformative** — a use that adds something new or serves a different purpose
> than the original, rather than just copying it.

### Factor 2 — Nature of the copyrighted work

*What kind of work is it?*

- **Highly creative works** — songs, novels, movies — get *less* fair-use leeway.
- **Factual works** — news articles, technical reports — get *more* leeway.
- Using an **unpublished** work weighs against fair use.

The logic: facts belong to everyone, so building on factual work is safer. Creative
expression is the very thing the creator made and owns, so it's more protected.

### Factor 3 — Amount and substantiality of the portion used

*How much did you take — and which part?*

The less you take, the safer it usually is. But here's the twist: **taking the
"heart" of a work can fail fair use even if it's only a tiny piece.**

> **"Heart of the work"** — the most distinctive or valuable part of a work. Using
> it can defeat fair use even when the quantity used is small.

A small piece that's just background is safer than a small piece that's the famous,
memorable moment.

### Factor 4 — Effect of the use on the potential market

*Does your use take away a sale the creator could have had?*

If your use **substitutes for buying the original**, or harms an existing or future
market for it, that weighs strongly against fair use. Courts pay close attention to
this factor — in practice it's the one that often carries the most weight.

---

## The rule you won't find in the law

Here's a trap worth naming loudly: **there are NO bright-line rules.** The law does
not say "10% is okay," or "30 seconds is okay," or "one page is okay."

You will see these numbers everywhere online. They are myths — outdated classroom
"guidelines" that were never law and that university sources explicitly reject. Treat
them as rough rules of thumb at *most*. Amount is only one factor, and even a tiny
amount can be the "heart" of a work.

---

## Three things people get wrong

**1. "It's for school, so it's automatically fair use."**
Trap. Educational purpose is only *one* factor. Copying a whole textbook, or posting
material publicly, can still be infringement.

**2. "If I don't make money from it, it's fair use."**
Trap. Not making money *helps*, but market harm is about substituting for the
original, not about profit. If your free use means the creator loses a sale, that's
still harm.

**3. "If I give credit, it's fair use."**
Trap. Credit is good practice and good manners — but it has no bearing on a
fair-use finding. Credit doesn't replace permission.

## Worked examples: weigh the factors yourself

Grab a piece of paper, or just use your thumb to cover the verdicts. For each
scenario, try to argue *all four factors* before you look. This is the skill — the
reasoning, not the "right answer."

### Scenario 1 — The book review

You quote a short passage from a book in your book-review project, to comment on what
the author said, and you cite it.

- **Factor 1:** Noncommercial, educational, and you're commenting on the work — it
  serves a *different purpose* than the book itself. Leans toward.
- **Factor 2:** It's a novel — a creative work. Leans against.
- **Factor 3:** A short passage, not the ending or the famous line. Leans toward.
- **Factor 4:** Nobody skips buying the book because of your review. Leans toward.

**Verdict:** leans toward fair use. *A small, credited quote used to comment on a
creative work, that doesn't replace the book.*

### Scenario 2 — The soundtrack

You want to use a few seconds of your favourite song in a school video to make a
point. Compare two endings:

- **Ending A — in class only:** you play the video in class to discuss it. Small
  amount, in-class purpose, not posted anywhere. Weighs more like fair use.
- **Ending B — posted publicly:** you upload the whole video to YouTube, song on the
  soundtrack. Now the music replaces the original (anyone can get your video
  *instead of* the song), and it's public — a different analysis entirely.

**Verdict:** the same starting idea becomes *much* riskier when the video is posted
publicly. **Fair use in class ≠ fair use online.**

### Scenario 3 — The parody

You make a comedy video that pokes fun at a TV show, using short clips of the show to
comment on it.

- **Factor 1:** This is the big one — your parody is **transformative**. It adds
  something new and comments on the original rather than replacing it.
- **Factor 2:** It's a creative work. Leans against.
- **Factor 3:** Short clips, used to make your point. Mixed — depends on which clips.
- **Factor 4:** This is special. A parody makes fun of the original in a way the
  original author would *never* license — so the market-harm test is judged
  differently.

**Verdict:** parody gets special consideration because it's transformative in a way
the original author would never permit. Courts treat it differently from ordinary
copying.

### Scenario 4 — The sale

You copy your favourite author's poems, staple them into booklets, and sell them to
your classmates for pocket money.

- **Factor 1:** Commercial — you're making money. Leans against.
- **Factor 2:** Poems — creative works. Leans against.
- **Factor 3:** You're copying *whole poems*. Each poem in full is the "heart" of the
  work. Leans against.
- **Factor 4:** You're directly displacing sales the author would have made. Leans
  strongly against.

**Verdict:** NOT fair use. Every factor leans against you.

### Scenario 5 — The news chart

For a report on climate change, you download a chart from a news site to include one
fact, and you credit the source.

- **Factor 1:** Educational, and you're using it for the *fact* inside it. Leans
  toward.
- **Factor 2:** News charts are factual works — more leeway. Leans toward.
- **Factor 3:** One chart, used for its data. Small and functional. Leans toward.
- **Factor 4:** Doesn't take a sale away from anyone. Leans toward.

**Verdict:** much safer than ripping a full music track to post online. Factual
works, small amounts, credited, no market harm.

## When "fair use" is actually something else

There's a separate rule that gets confused with fair use all the time: the
**classroom use exemption** (a US law known as §110(1)).

It allows teachers to show movies and images, and students to perform works, in
face-to-face classroom teaching — but only in *limited* situations: legitimate
copies, a live class audience, and no public posting or wide distribution.

Key point: it's a different provision with narrow conditions. It does **not** cover
posting material publicly or distributing it widely. Don't rely on it for anything
you plan to publish.

---

## Your job isn't to be a judge

Only a court can give a final ruling on fair use. But you're not studying to be a
judge — you're learning to *think* like one. For students and teachers, the practical
skill is the same: **make a reasoned judgment, factor by factor.**

When you're faced with a real decision, ask yourself:

1. What's my purpose — and what am I adding to the original?
2. What kind of work is it?
3. How much am I taking — and is it the "heart"?
4. Does my use take away a sale or a market?

Then weigh them all together, and you'll have an answer you can explain — not just
a guess.

---

## A note on other countries

Fair use is **specific to US law**. The UK, Australia, and Singapore use a different
framework called **fair dealing**, which covers a narrower, specific list of purposes
(research, review, reporting) and works differently.

If you're outside the US, treat the four factors as a US framework being used here
for teaching — a great way to practise *reasoned judgment about reuse* — not as a
universal rule. The habit transfers even when the law differs.

## Key words you've built

- **Fair use** — the US legal right to use a copyrighted work without permission in
  certain limited circumstances, judged by four factors.
- **Transformative** — a use that adds something new or serves a different purpose
  than the original.
- **Four factors** — purpose/character, nature of the work, amount/substantiality,
  and effect on the market.
- **Parody** — a work that imitates and comments on or makes fun of another work;
  courts treat parody differently under fair use.
- **"Heart of the work"** — the most distinctive or valuable part; using it can
  defeat fair use even in small quantity.
- **Commercial use** — a use intended to make money or gain business advantage.
- **Market / potential market** — the existing or future ways a creator could sell or
  license their work.
- **Classroom use exemption (§110(1))** — a separate, limited provision allowing
  performance/display of works in face-to-face teaching.

---

## The big idea to take with you

Fair use isn't a free pass, and it isn't a mystery. It's a balance you learn to weigh
for yourself. The next time you want to reuse something, don't ask "is this legal?"
and stop. Ask the better question: *how does this use weigh on each of the four
factors — and can I explain my reasoning?*
