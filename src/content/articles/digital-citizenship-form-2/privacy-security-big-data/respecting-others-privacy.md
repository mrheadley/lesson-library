# Lesson 14 — Respecting Others' Privacy: Ask Before You Post

## Opening the door

Picture this: you're at a sleepover. You're mid-bite, cheeks stuffed with pizza, hair a mess. Someone's phone comes out and the camera points at you. You laugh — then you say, "Hey, don't post that."

How would you feel if they posted it anyway? Embarrassed? A little betrayed? Probably like your say-so mattered and got ignored.

Now flip it. That exact feeling is what this lesson is about. Because privacy online isn't only about *you* protecting *your* info — it's also about respecting the privacy of everyone who shows up in *your* photos, videos, and stories. Your choices affect their privacy too. This isn't a rule you're handed; it's a skill you build by thinking about the other person's experience, the same way you'd want them to think about yours.

## Consent: the one-word habit

There's a simple word at the center of all of this: **consent** — asking for and receiving permission before you record, share, or post something about someone else.

Real consent has two ingredients, and both matter:

- **It's informed.** The person understands exactly what they're agreeing to — what photo, where it's going, who will see it. (Agreeing to a photo in a private group chat is not the same as agreeing to the same photo on your public feed.)
- **It's freely given.** They're not pressured into it, guilt-tripped, or worn down until they say yes.

The rule itself is beautifully simple: **ask before sharing a photo or video of someone.** And treat their answer as the final word. If they say no, respect it — keep it to yourself, or edit them out. If they later ask you to take something down, take it down without arguing. People are allowed to change their minds.

## Worked example: the birthday photo

You're at a friend's birthday party. You take a great group photo — everyone laughing, perfect lighting. You want to post it. But one friend in the photo says, "Please don't, I don't like how I look today."

What are your options?

- **Option A:** Post it anyway. It's a great photo! Everyone looks good except them, but still...
- **Option B:** Post it, but crop that friend out.
- **Option C:** Don't post it at all — or send it privately to just the friends in the picture.

Only one of those respects their "no." Here's the thinking: *you took the photo, but the people in it have rights over their own image.* Your photo isn't a licence to publish anyone. Their comfort beats your perfect photo. Option B is fine *if* they're genuinely out of the frame and the story you're telling doesn't depend on them — otherwise Option C, and honestly, Option C is often the kindest and easiest.

## Tagging is sharing

Here's a sneaky one. You post a group photo and tag your friends. That seems harmless — it's their own account, right? But here's what tagging actually does: it links their profile to your post and **shares the post with their connections too**. Everyone on their followers list can now see it.

So tagging counts as sharing. **Ask before tagging a friend**, before adding someone to a group chat or a story where their name or face appears, and before attaching their location to something. A friend's post can reveal your street in the background or your school uniform in the frame — that's *your* location being shared without your say-so, by someone else.

## Personal privacy vs. shared privacy

There's a real distinction here, and it's worth building a clear picture of it:

- **Personal privacy** is the choices you make about *your own* information — who can see your posts, what you share, what you keep to yourself. You're in charge of this one.
- **Shared (relational) privacy** is different. It's privacy that depends on *everyone involved*, because other people can share information about you even if you keep yours totally private.

Here's the uncomfortable truth that makes shared privacy matter: **your own privacy settings only control what YOU post.** They do not stop a friend from sharing a picture of you. You can have the tightest settings on earth, and a friend's screenshot of your story can still travel to people you never met.

And once something is posted, it is very hard to fully control its spread — screenshots exist, forwards happen, memes travel. That's exactly why the check has to happen *before* you hit post, not after.

## The empathy check: look before you share

Before you post anything about someone else, run this check. It's a habit you build, and it takes five seconds:

1. **Swap places.** How would I feel if this photo or this info were about me?
2. **Who might see it?** Not just the people I meant — the friends of friends, the forwards, the screenshot.
3. **How would they feel?** Is the person in it laughing, or are they the only one not laughing?
4. **How could it affect them later?** Could this embarrass them tomorrow, or in a year? A future job, a future school, a future you?

That last one matters because your **digital footprint** isn't only built by you — it includes what other people post and tag about you. By sharing without permission, you don't just risk your own footprint; you can damage someone else's, and they never asked for that.

## "It's just a joke" — when it isn't

Here's the line between funny and harmful, and it's one word: **the other person.** Sharing something embarrassing to make fun of someone, or to humiliate them, is online abuse — even if you meant it "as a joke." If the person isn't laughing, it's not a joke; it's embarrassment, and it can harm their mental health and their reputation.

That video of a classmate's embarrassing moment, forwarded around a group chat "for a laugh"? Even if it's funny to some of you, it's humiliation to the person in it. That meme made from a friend's photo? Memes travel fast and are very hard to remove.

And there's one form that's even more serious. **Sharing — or threatening to share — an intimate image of someone without consent is image-based abuse**, and it is illegal in many places (for example, Australia's eSafety Commissioner can order it taken down). Laws differ by country and reporting paths differ, so your teacher will point you to the rules where you live. But the underlying principle is the same everywhere: someone's most private images belong to them, and only them.

## Common misconceptions — check your thinking

- **"I took the photo, so it's mine to post."** The people in it also have rights over their own image. The photo is yours; their face isn't.
- **"My account is private, so it's fine."** Private doesn't stop screenshots, or your friends' friends, or forwards. Private shrinks the audience; it doesn't control it.
- **"They agreed once, so they're always OK with it."** Consent is for each specific photo and moment. People can change their minds, and they can withdraw consent — that's allowed, and it must be respected.
- **"We're friends, so I don't need to ask."** Friendship doesn't remove the need for consent. If anything, being close makes respecting boundaries *more* important, not less.
- **"If they say no, they're being silly."** Different people are comfortable with different things. Their "no" deserves respect regardless of how you'd feel in their place.

## One more piece of the puzzle

Two open questions worth thinking about, not just accepting:

- **What about the adults?** Whether parents and other adults should always ask before posting about a child — and how that even works for a child too young to give consent — is a genuinely open question. Many families are still figuring it out. You're allowed to have opinions about your own image; so are the kids in the adults' photos.
- **What about close friends and private groups?** Is consent still needed in a private story or a "close friends" group? Sources agree the rule is per-context — each photo, each place, each moment — but exactly where the line sits is a discussion worth having, not a fixed answer.

## Building the habit

None of this is about turning posting into a stressful minefield. It's about turning one small question into an automatic habit: **"Did they say it's okay?"** The sleepover photo, the tagged group shot, the forwarded video — the moment you make asking the default, you become the kind of person other people feel safe around. And that's the real point: respecting someone's privacy is one of the simplest ways to show you care about them. Build the habit before you post, and you'll never have to apologize after.
