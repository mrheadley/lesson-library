# Lesson 13 — Data Literacy: Who Knows What About You?

## Opening the door

Here's a strange little experiment you can try this week. On your phone, search for "football boots" once — just once. Then spend a few minutes scrolling through other apps. Notice anything? Those ads for football boots that start appearing *everywhere* — on the video app, on the social app, on the game's loading screen — those ads weren't random. Someone or something is watching.

This lesson isn't about being scared of that. It's about understanding the mechanism behind the curtain — because once you understand *how* something works, it stops being mysterious and becomes something you can think about and act on. That's the constructionist way: you don't get handed the truth, you build your understanding of it by noticing, questioning, and testing.

## The trail you leave behind

Everything you do online leaves a trace. Every search, every video you watch, every like, every click, every thing you buy, where you are, who you talk to, and what device you're using. Companies that run search engines, social media, video and shopping apps collect data on nearly every one of those interactions.

That permanent trail has a name: your **digital footprint**. And here's the part that surprises people — it's essentially permanent. A post you delete, a search you make "just this once," those can still exist somewhere. That's what makes a footprint a footprint: it's there even after you walk away.

## Cookies aren't crumbs

You've probably seen the word "cookie" on every website you've ever visited. Here's what they actually are: **small text files a website saves on your device to remember you.** That's it. They're not viruses, they're not spying robots — they're just notes.

Some cookies are genuinely useful. They keep you logged in, remember what's in your shopping cart, remember your preferences so you don't re-enter them every visit.

But here's the trick. **Tracking cookies** are a different kind of note — one that follows you across *many different websites*, reporting back on where you go. Think of it this way:

> A useful cookie is a name tag that says *"Welcome back, this person likes dark mode."*
> A tracking cookie is a private investigator who follows you from shop to shop and reports everything you looked at.

Deleting cookies reduces tracking — but here's the nuance: it doesn't erase the data that was already collected about you. That data has already been written down somewhere.

## From crumbs to a portrait

So what happens to all those traces? Companies combine them to build a **profile** of you — a picture of your habits, your interests, your preferences. Not "a file on you" like a school record; more like a collage, built from thousands of tiny observations about what you do.

And here's the part most people don't know: these profiles get **bought, sold, and combined** by companies called **data brokers**. A data broker is a company that doesn't make a product for you at all — its product *is* your data. It buys profiles, mixes them together, and sells them on.

Now let's talk about a number that should make you think. One 2017 study estimated that ad-tech companies could hold as many as **72 million data points on a child by age 13**. Other researchers think that's an underestimate. Treat numbers like this as a dramatic illustration, not a precise fact — the exact count doesn't matter. The point is: it's a lot. A lot more than you'd guess.

## "Free" doesn't mean free

Here's the big idea of this whole lesson, and it's a sentence worth writing down:

**If the product is free, you are the product.**

Think about it. A free app costs real money to build, run, and improve. Someone has to pay for that. If you're not paying with money, you're paying another way — with your data, your attention, or both. Free services don't charge you money because they make their money from you another way: selling your data or using it to sell you things.

Here's how that works in practice, step by step:

1. You use a free service. Every action gets collected.
2. Those actions build your profile — what you like, what you might buy, where you are.
3. Companies use that profile to show you **personalized** content and ads — content tailored to you.
4. Or they sell the profile to data brokers and advertisers.

So *personalization* — the videos "just for you," the ads that seem to read your mind — sounds friendly, but the motive behind it is commercial. The company isn't tailoring your experience out of kindness; it's tailoring it to keep you engaged longer (more watch time, more clicks) and to sell you things.

## Worked example: the ad auction

Have you ever opened a page and watched an ad load in a split second? That ad didn't just "happen" to be there. Behind the scenes, this happens:

- The page loads, and an automated system runs a tiny **auction** — in the time it takes the page to appear, advertisers bid for the chance to show you their ad.
- Your profile (past searches, pages visited, links clicked) helps advertisers decide how much you're worth to them.
- The highest bidder's ad appears on your screen.

**Targeted advertising** is exactly this: ads chosen for you based on what you've already done. That's why two friends can open the same free app and see completely different ads — each of them has a different profile.

## Testing two common rumors

**"The app is listening to my microphone."** You mention something in a group chat and — boom — an ad for it appears. It feels like the app must be listening, right? Here's what the evidence says: there is no solid evidence of apps using microphones to target ads. What's actually happening is usually far more boring (and far more impressive): your **searches, clicks, and viewing history** already told them what you're interested in. The chat mention was a coincidence on top of a profile that already knew. This is worth talking about in class, because believing the microphone rumor can make you think the world is creepier than it is — and can make you miss the real mechanism, which is behavioral tracking.

**"Incognito mode makes me invisible."** Private or incognito browsing hides your activity from *people who use the same device* — like hiding your browsing from a sibling on the shared family computer. It does **not** make you invisible to websites and companies. They can still track you.

## Is your data really protected because you're a kid?

Laws exist to protect children's data. In the US, **COPPA** (the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act) requires parental consent before companies collect data from children under 13. But here's the honest picture: research by Common Sense Media found that many apps still collect and share children's data, and that the rules are often not followed or not transparent. Laws differ by country too — COPPA in the US, GDPR in Europe, and different rules in Australia. So the legal protection isn't the same everywhere, and being cautious is smart no matter where you live.

## The open question: is targeted advertising worth it?

Here's a genuinely unsettled debate, and you get to think about it rather than be told the answer:

- **For personalization:** better recommendations, content you actually want, ads for things you're genuinely interested in. Some argue that's a real benefit.
- **Against it:** its purpose is to sell, it's built on data you may not realize you were giving away, and there's real debate about how well it even works. One 2019 study found targeted cookies raised publisher revenue by only about 4% on average — which makes you wonder whether all that data collection is even worth its privacy cost.

There's also what researchers call the **privacy paradox**: people — including teenagers — often say they care about privacy, yet keep sharing lots of data anyway. That's a real tension, not a solved problem. It's worth asking yourself honestly: what do I *say* about privacy, and what do my habits actually *do*?

## You're not powerless — the builder's toolkit

Here's the part that matters most: understanding how this works gives you power over it. You don't have to be a passive target. These are real levers you can pull:

- **Privacy settings.** Turn them on and check them regularly. They do make a real difference.
- **Review app permissions.** When an app asks for access to your contacts, camera, or location, ask: *does this app really need this to work?* A free game that needs your location to run is a red flag that it's collecting more than it needs.
- **Turn off location when you're not using it.** Your phone can know where you are even when you're not actively using an app — unless you switch location services off.
- **Be choosy about what you post.** Every post is a data point added to your footprint.
- **Think before "free."** Ask what the product is really costing you.

## Building your awareness

None of this is about becoming paranoid. It's about becoming *aware* — seeing the mechanism instead of being surprised by it. The companies are doing what companies do; the question is whether you understand what's happening, because that's where your choices live. The more you understand the trade, the more you can decide for yourself what's worth giving and what isn't. That understanding is something you build — one observation at a time.
