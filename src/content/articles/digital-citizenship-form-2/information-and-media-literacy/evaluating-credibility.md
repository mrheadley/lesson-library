# Lesson 15 — Can You Trust It? Building Credibility Checks

When you read something in a book, you usually know a whole team worked on it before it reached you — an editor read it, a fact-checker checked it, a publisher decided to print it. The internet doesn't work like that. Anyone — literally anyone — can put anything online, and no one checks it first.

That might sound a little scary. But it's actually the start of a superpower: it means *you* get to decide what deserves your trust. Reading online is not about receiving truth. It's about **building** your judgment, one question at a time. This article gives you the tools to build it.

## The big idea: anyone can publish

Here's the most important fact in this whole lesson:

> On the web, **being online says nothing about being true.**

Anyone with a phone can reach millions of people. There is no editor standing at the "door of the internet" approving what goes up. That means a blog post, a video, a photo, and a "news" site are all just *claims* until you check them. The person who made them might be an expert, a well-meaning kid, a joker, or someone trying to sell you something — and it's your job to find out which.

Researchers at Stanford tested students of all ages on exactly this. Many mistook advertisements for news stories, accepted posts from friends or famous people at face value, and judged websites by how they *looked* instead of who was *behind* them. Even adults struggle: in one survey, only about a quarter of adults could correctly sort all five factual statements they were given. So this isn't about age — it's a skill everyone needs to keep building.

## What does "credible" even mean?

**Credibility** is how much a source deserves your trust — whether you have good reason to believe it. It isn't a yes/no stamp a source is born with. You *earn* confidence in a source by checking it, the way a builder tests materials before adding them to a wall. Each check is another brick.

A source becomes more credible when you can answer these questions about it. You might know this set of checks as the **CRAAP test** — created in 2004 by a librarian named Sarah Blakeslee and her colleagues. The letters stand for:

| Letter | Question you ask | Why it matters |
|--------|------------------|----------------|
| **C** | **Currency** — How recent is it? | Some topics move fast. A page from 2006 will tell you Pluto is a planet; today's astronomy sites call it a dwarf planet. |
| **R** | **Relevance** — Does it actually fit your question? | A great article about the wrong thing doesn't help. |
| **A** | **Authority** — Who made it, and do they know this topic? | Ask for a name, an organization, and their connection to the subject. |
| **A** | **Accuracy** — Is it backed by evidence you can check? | Where are the facts, dates, quotes, and sources? Can you verify them yourself? |
| **P** | **Purpose** — Why was this made? | To inform, persuade, entertain, or sell? Every purpose shapes what's included. |

Don't rush through the letters as a mindless checklist. Treat them as a set of questions you build with — the more you can answer, the more trust you can place. And don't stop at the last letter. The best readers add one more habit after the test, and that's coming next.

## Fact vs. opinion — a two-second check that changes everything

Before you judge a source, judge the *kind* of statement it's making.

- A **fact** can be proven true or false by checking evidence. "Water freezes at 0°C." "Lions don't live in the Arctic."
- An **opinion** is a belief, judgment, or taste. "Chocolate is the best ice-cream flavour." "That movie was boring."

Two things trip people up here. First, an opinion wrapped in strong language or a chart is still an opinion — numbers and dramatic words don't turn a belief into a fact. Second, opinions aren't bad! "I think this is the best pizza place" is a perfectly good opinion. The problem is only when an opinion is *dressed up* as a fact so you can't tell them apart. Your job is to be able to tell which is which.

## One more habit: look outside the page (SIFT)

Here's a surprising finding from research: professional fact-checkers don't spend a long time reading a single page deeply. Instead, they do something called **lateral reading** — they open new tabs and check what *other* sources say about the site, the author, and the claims. They move sideways, not just down. (Checklist-style tests have limits too: a convincing fake can pass them, and studies disagree about how well they work for different ages — more on that at the end.)

That habit has a simple name: **SIFT**.

1. **S — Stop.** Before you share, quote, or believe, pause. Ask: do I actually know what this is?
2. **I — Investigate the source.** Who wrote it? Who runs the site? What are they known for?
3. **F — Find better coverage.** Look for a more trusted or more complete source on the same story.
4. **T — Trace claims, quotes, and media to the original.** Where did the photo, the number, or the quote actually come from?

SIFT is a habit, not a set of boxes. Like learning to ride a bike, you get faster the more you do it — until checking becomes automatic.

## Worked examples — watch the tools in action

### Example 1: "Lions have started living in the Arctic!"

A classmate forwards a photo of a lion in the snow, saying lions have moved to the Arctic.

- **Purpose?** Who posted it and why — a joke, a rumour, a science site?
- **SIFT it:** investigate the source, then *find better coverage* — do any real science sources report this? (They don't.)
- **Evidence?** Where's the study or the expert?
- **Fact or opinion?** "Lions live in the Arctic" is a fact-claim, so it *can* be checked — and it checks out as false.

You don't need to be a biologist to fact-check this. You need to be a builder: you test the claim before adding it to your wall of beliefs.

### Example 2: Mega-O's cereal — "makes you run faster!"

A YouTube ad (or cereal box) claims Mega-O's cereal will make you run faster.

- **Spot the ad.** Ads are paid content designed to persuade you to buy or click. They are not neutral, and they are *not* evidence.
- **Label it:** a lot of "reviews" online are secretly paid for. Look for the words **Ad** or **Sponsored**.
- **Ask for evidence:** who did the study? Can you find it? A claim without checkable evidence is just a claim.

### Example 3: Two sites, two different answers about your favourite footballer

One site says your favourite player has scored 45 goals; another says 51.

- **Cross-check:** look at a third, independent source — the club's official site, or a major sports stats site.
- **One good source is never enough.** Credibility is built by checking several independent sources that *agree*.

### Example 4: "Is Pluto a planet?"

A 2006 page says yes. A current astronomy site says it's a dwarf planet.

- **Currency matters:** space science changes quickly. For fast-moving topics, check the date *and* look for recent sources.
- Both pages are online. The difference is not "online vs. not" — it's **when** each was made.

## Watch out for these trapdoors

- **"It's on the internet, so it must be true."** Nope — anyone can publish anything.
- **".org and .edu are always good; .com is always bad."** Anyone can register a .org. Universities host student projects and opinion pages. A domain is one clue among many — never proof on its own.
- **"The top result (or first page) is the best."** Paid ads can sit at the top. Position is not reliability.
- **"It looks professional, so it's trustworthy."** Hoax and parody sites are often designed to look very convincing. A polished design is easy to fake.
- **"My friends shared it, or a famous person said it, so it's true."** Popularity and trustworthiness are different things. Researchers found students trusted sources mainly because others shared them.
- **"Opinions with statistics are facts."** Wrapping an opinion in numbers doesn't change what it is.

## Build your own mini-checklist

You can use this whenever you meet a new source. It's a starter kit — add your own questions as you build your skills.

1. What kind of statement is this — fact or opinion?
2. Who made it, and do they know this topic? (authority)
3. Why was it made — to inform, persuade, entertain, or sell? (purpose)
4. What evidence backs it, and can I check it? (accuracy)
5. How recent does it need to be for this topic? (currency)
6. What do *other*, independent sources say? (SIFT / lateral reading)

## A note on the debate (for teachers)

This is a live curriculum question: should a 35-minute lesson centre a checklist like CRAAP or a habit like SIFT? The research genuinely disagrees. Stanford's researchers and Mike Caulfield (who created SIFT) argue checklist-style evaluation has real limits — students can rate a convincing fake as credible based on in-page markers, and professional fact-checkers prefer lateral reading. But other studies (for example, one with Grade 10 students published in the *International Journal of Technology in Education*, 2021) found CRAAP measurably improved students' evaluation skills.

This lesson deliberately teaches *both* — the CRAAP questions give students a shared vocabulary, and SIFT gives them the habit of stepping outside the page. Neither is claimed as the single right way. The teaching move is to let students build both, then see which serves them in the moment. The same applies to the .org question: some guides treat .org as a positive signal, while others stress anyone can register one. In the classroom, treat a domain as a clue students investigate, not a stamp of approval.

## Key ideas to take away

- Anyone can publish on the web — so you must judge for yourself.
- Credibility is **built**, question by question, not received.
- Ask: author, purpose, evidence, currency, and bias.
- Know the difference between facts (provable) and opinions (beliefs and tastes).
- Check *outside* the page, too: SIFT (Stop, Investigate, Find better coverage, Trace).
- Cross-check several independent sources — one good source is not enough.
