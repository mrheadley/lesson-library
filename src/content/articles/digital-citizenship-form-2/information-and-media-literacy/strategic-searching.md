# Lesson 16 — Search Like a Strategist

Typing a question into a search box feels like just one step, but you're actually doing something pretty clever — you're a detective gathering clues, a builder choosing which materials to inspect. A great search isn't luck. It's a **strategy**: a plan you build before you ever press Enter. This article shows you the plan, the tools, and the habits that separate a lucky find from a reliable one.

## First, know your tool: how a search engine works

Search engines like Google, Bing, and DuckDuckGo don't "know" everything on the internet. They work in three stages:

1. **Crawling.** Automated programs (often called bots, crawlers, or spiders) visit pages and follow the links on them, discovering new pages along the way.
2. **Indexing.** Each discovered page is read, analysed, and stored in a giant database called the **index**.
3. **Ranking.** When you type a search, the engine matches your words against its index and orders the results using hundreds of different signals.

That middle step matters more than you'd think. **A search engine can only show you pages it has crawled and indexed.** If a page was never discovered, no search in the world will find it — no matter how good your strategy is.

## The first strategy: keywords, not whole questions

Here's a classic mistake: typing your whole question word for word.

❌ "How many planets are in the solar system?"

Engines match on key terms, not full sentences. Turning a question into a few strong keywords usually works far better:

✅ `solar system planets list`

This is called **extracting keywords** — pulling out the important words and short phrases that capture what you're really looking for. (Your teacher might call the words you type a **query**.)

**Keyword is the name for the important word or phrase that captures what you're looking for.** The better your keywords, the better the engine can help you.

## The next tools: search operators

**Operators** are special symbols and words that change *how* the engine searches. They're not secret hacker tricks — Google officially documents them, and they're simple enough for everyday school searches.

| Operator | What it does | Example |
|----------|--------------|---------|
| `"..."` | Find an **exact phrase**, in that word order | `"we don't talk about Bruno"` |
| `site:` | Search **only one website or domain** | `site:kids.britannica.com dinosaurs` |
| `-` | **Exclude** a word (no space after the minus) | `jaguar -car` |
| `OR` | Find results with **either** word (must be uppercase) | `football OR soccer` |
| `filetype:` | Find a **specific file type**, like a factsheet | `filetype:pdf space facts kids` |

The rules that trip people up:

- The minus sign goes **directly before** the word you want to exclude — `jaguar -car`, not `jaguar - car`.
- `OR` must be written in **capital letters**.
- There's **no space after the colon** — `site:kids.britannica.com`, not `site: kids.britannica.com`.
- Quotes force the exact order of the words inside them.

### Watch one in action

Try searching just `jaguar`. You'll get cars *and* a big cat mixed together. Now try `jaguar -car` — the car results drop away. Or search `"we don't talk about Bruno"` and watch the exact song appear at the top, instead of thousands of pages that happen to use those words separately. One small symbol, one much better result.

## The golden rule: the smallest effective constraint

Here's the catch with operators: **adding more doesn't always make a search better.** The smallest constraint that gets you what you need is the goal. Stack too many operators on one query and you can remove useful results along with the useless ones. Build your search like a craftsman — add a tool only when it earns its place.

## After the results load: judge what you see

A good strategy doesn't end at the results page. Before you click *anything*, read the results like a detective:

- **Scan the title** — does it match what you asked?
- **Read the snippet** — the short description under each title tells you whether the page is worth opening.
- **Spot the ads** — paid listings are labelled **Ad** and can appear *above* the real results. Being first does not mean being best. If the top listing says "Ad", scroll to the first non-ad result for a more balanced answer.

Researchers who study this call it **click restraint**: taking a moment to decide what's worth opening instead of clicking the first shiny thing. Paired with **lateral reading** (checking what other sources say about a page), it's the difference between an OK search and a strategic one.

## Worked example: from muddle to masterpiece

**Your task:** a school project on jaguars.

1. **Start with a question.** "What do jaguars eat and where do they live?"
2. **Extract keywords.** `jaguar habitat diet`
3. **Search.** You get car ads and cat sites mixed up. Narrow it: `jaguar animal facts` or `jaguar -car`.
4. **Constrain the site.** You want a trusted source only: `site:kids.britannica.com jaguar`.
5. **Judging results.** Read the titles and snippets. Skip the ads. Compare what two different sources say.
6. **Try another engine.** Google and DuckDuckGo often return different pages for the same query — a second engine is a second opinion.

That's a full **search plan** in action: think about your question, extract keywords, apply a strategy, run the search, and judge what comes back. You'll notice the plan is mostly *thinking* — the typing is the smallest part.

## What about those AI answer boxes?

Since 2024, some search engines have started placing AI-generated answer summaries above the usual links. Here's the thing to remember: **an AI summary is content, not a fact.** It's still something to evaluate — check it against other sources just like you'd check a website. Treat the blue links as the evidence; treat the summary box as a helpful but unaudited guess.

## Common misconceptions — don't fall for these

- **"The first result is the best answer."** Top results can be ads or sites that are simply good at ranking. Use click restraint.
- **"Typing the whole question is the best way to search."** Engines match key terms, not sentences. Extract keywords.
- **"Search operators are only for computer experts."** Quotes, minus, and `site:` are simple everyday tools.
- **"Search engines show every page on the internet."** Only pages they've crawled and indexed — some pages are never indexed.
- **"I need perfect capitals and punctuation."** Engines ignore capitalisation and most punctuation, but *spelling* still matters.
- **"`site:` shows the exact number of pages on a site."** Those counts are estimates, not precise figures.
- **"More operators always means a better search."** The smallest effective constraint wins.
- **"All search engines work the same way."** Different engines use different indexes and algorithms, so results vary.
- **"Old tricks like `cache:` and `link:` still work."** Some operators Google once supported have been retired. Rely on the officially documented ones.

## Notes for teachers — what's genuinely up for debate

A few things about search teaching are still being worked out, and this lesson makes deliberate choices:

- **Boolean vocabulary.** Some resources (citing the National School Library Standards) advise against using the label "Boolean operators" before high school, as long as students can actually use `AND`, `OR`, `NOT`. Others use the vocabulary directly. This lesson teaches the *behaviour* — using `OR`, quotes, minus — without overloading Form 2 students with the label.
- **Operators change over time.** Google's documented operators evolve; older guides recycle outdated ones (`cache:`, `link:`) that no longer work. Prefer Google's current official Search Help and Search Central pages when planning.
- **`site:` counts.** Guides disagree on whether `site:` shows an exact or approximate number of results; Google's own position is that counts are estimates. Present it as approximate.
- **AI summaries.** How AI Overviews change results pages (and how much attention students pay to traditional blue links) is still evolving guidance. Treat AI answers as content to evaluate, not as a definitive source.
- **Engine differences.** Operator support isn't identical across Google, Bing, and DuckDuckGo (for example, `before:`/`after:` or wildcards). A strategy that works on one engine may not on another — another reason to try more than one.

## Key ideas to take away

- Search engines **crawl**, **index**, and **rank** — they can only show pages they've indexed.
- Turn whole questions into **keywords**, not full sentences.
- Use the small toolbox: `"exact phrase"`, `-exclude`, `site:`, `OR`, `filetype:`.
- Use the **smallest effective constraint** — don't overload the query.
- Read titles and **snippets**, skip the ads, and practise **click restraint**.
- Build a **search plan** before you search, and try more than one engine.
- AI answer boxes are content to evaluate, not facts to copy.
