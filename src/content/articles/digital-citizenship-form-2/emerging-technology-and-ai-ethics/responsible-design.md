# Lesson 20 — Building With Care: Responsible Design and AI

Every time you open an app, you're meeting someone's decision. The autoplay video that plays next, the chatbot that answers your homework question, the filter that smooths your photo — each of these was *designed* by people. And behind each one sits a quieter question that this lesson is all about: **who does this tool help, who might it hurt, and who chose that to happen?**

Good news: that person with the power to choose can be you.

---

## What is AI, really?

**Artificial intelligence (AI)** sounds mysterious, but it's just this: computer programs made by people that can learn from data and then make predictions, suggestions, or decisions — like suggesting a video or recognising a face.

Two words matter in that sentence: *made by people* and *learn from data*. AI doesn't arrive from nowhere, and it doesn't make choices in a vacuum. It learns from the examples people give it, and it follows rules people wrote. So the choices designers make decide what the tool does, who it helps, and who it might hurt.

> **Think of it like this:** An AI is not a wizard. It's more like a very fast student who has only read the books you handed them. If the books are one-sided, so is their "knowledge."

---

## Algorithms: instructions with an opinion

An **algorithm** is just a set of step-by-step instructions that turns an input into an output — like a recipe.

Here's a classic experiment from the MIT Media Lab. Write the recipe for the "best" peanut-butter sandwich. Now compare your recipe with a classmate's.

- Did you use crunchy or smooth peanut butter? White bread or brown? Cut in triangles or squares? Toast or no toast?
- Everyone wrote a *perfectly reasonable* recipe — and everyone's "best" was different.

That's the key insight: **every algorithm hides an opinion about what matters.** A recipe's "best" reflects the cook's values. An app's rules reflect the designer's values. There's no such thing as a neutral set of instructions — the person who writes them always chooses what to optimise for.

### Worked example: the YouTube autoplay question

YouTube's algorithm decides what plays next after each video. Seems technical and neutral, right? Let's look closer.

| Person affected | What the algorithm does for them |
|-----------------|----------------------------------|
| The viewer | Shows "just one more" video they'll probably enjoy — but maybe keeps them watching long past when they meant to stop |
| The video creator | Recommends their channel, which can grow their audience |
| The company | Keeps the viewer's attention longer, which keeps advertising money flowing |

Now the design question: **whose time is being spent, and who benefits from that spending?** Endless watching helps the company and the creators — and quietly uses up the viewer's time. The algorithm isn't evil; it's an *opinion* about what "best" means, and that opinion was built in by people.

A stakeholder is **anyone who is affected by a product** — not just the person using it, but also families, creators, viewers, communities, and the company itself. Learning to list the stakeholders of a tool is one of the most powerful skills you can build, because it lets you *see* the invisible people a design touches.

## Building a picture of harm and benefit

AI can do wonderful things:

- **Accessibility tools** that help people with disabilities do things they couldn't before.
- **Translation tools** that let you talk with a pen pal in another country.
- **Learning help** that explains a tricky idea in a new way.
- **Health-data tools** that spot patterns in medical information that humans might miss.

And AI can do harmful things:

- **Bias** — treating some groups of people unfairly (the whole next lesson digs into this).
- **Loss of privacy** — collecting and using data about people without them really understanding.
- **Fake content** — deepfakes and misinformation that pretend to be real.
- **Over-reliance** — trusting an answer that's confidently wrong.

Notice something: the same tool can be on both lists. A homework chatbot is a brilliant study buddy *if* you use it to help you think. But if you copy its answer without checking, and the answer is wrong, you've learned nothing — and worse, you've stopped practising thinking. **Responsible use means using AI to help you think, not to replace thinking.**

### Worked example: the photo filter

A photo app adds a filter that changes people's skin tones. It looks like a tiny technical choice. But who does that filter work well for, and who does it subtly tell "you don't look right"? A small design decision can change how people see themselves and others — that's *societal impact* in miniature. The same goes for voice assistants that understand some accents better than others: a design choice about who the tool works for.

## Common misconceptions (let's bust these)

1. **"AI is neutral and can't be biased or wrong."** — Nope. AI learns from data chosen by people and follows rules chosen by people. It can reflect — and even amplify — the mistakes and values in that data.
2. **"AI is basically a person who can think and feel."** — It's a tool. No feelings, no common sense, and it can be confidently wrong.
3. **"Ethical use of AI just means not cheating on homework."** — It's so much more: privacy, fairness, who the tool helps or harms, and what designers choose to build.
4. **"Designers are the only people responsible for technology."** — Users make choices too. And you can grow up to be a conscientious designer *and* a conscientious consumer.
5. **"If it's convenient and popular, it must be harmless."** — Harms are often indirect: privacy, attention, misinformation, unfairness. A tool can delight its user and still cause harm to people who never even open it.
6. **"Ethics can be added after the product is finished."** — Research (Williams et al., 2022) shows ethics works best when it's embedded *throughout* design, not tacked on at the end.

## Responsible design: your builder's checklist

**Responsible (or ethical) design** means designing technology while thinking about its effects on people and society, and making choices that respect users' fairness, safety, privacy, and well-being.

A good designer asks these questions **before, during, and after** building:

- **Who is affected?** List every stakeholder — direct and indirect.
- **What is my "opinion"?** What does my algorithm optimise for, and is that the right thing to optimise for?
- **Who is left out?** Whose needs did I not design for?
- **What data am I collecting, and do I need it?** Deciding what to collect and what to do with it is a responsible-design question, not just a technical one.
- **What could go wrong?** And what am I doing to catch it?

When you build your own projects, you're not just writing code — you're *making choices that affect people*. That's exactly what AI literacy is: **the knowledge and skills to understand what AI is, use it responsibly, and question its effects.**

## What the experts are still figuring out

Different schools and researchers disagree on some big questions, and it's honest to say so:

- **Should AI tools be in schools at all?** Some districts block tools like ChatGPT; others actively teach with them. Curricula disagree on whether to emphasise avoiding AI or embracing it.
- **Is AI's overall effect on society good or harmful?** AI4K12 stresses that AI affects society in both positive and negative ways — most experts land on "both," while some commentaries weigh the risks more heavily.
- **Who causes the harm — designers, the data, or society?** Different sources assign responsibility differently. Some point at technical design, others at wider societal systems. The truth likely involves all three.
- **Build with AI or critique AI?** Some curricula are creation-heavy (students build with AI), others critique-heavy (students analyse risks). Both matter.

Don't worry about "the one right answer" here — these are open questions being worked out by thoughtful people. Your job as a young digital citizen is to keep asking them.

## Your challenge

Design (or redesign) one small digital thing for a stakeholder group of your choosing. It could be the "best next video" choice, an app for your class, or a filter — anything. Then:

1. List **every stakeholder**.
2. State **the opinion your design builds in**.
3. Name **one benefit and one harm** your design could create.
4. Change one thing to make it fairer, kinder, or safer.

You're not just learning about responsible design — you're *practising it*. And that's how understanding gets built: one decision, one question, one redesign at a time.
