# Lesson 11 — Digital Etiquette

*Reference article for Form 2 (ages 11–13) · Digital Citizenship*

Imagine wearing your football boots to a wedding. Or your school uniform to the beach. Nothing about the clothes is *bad* — they're just wrong for the room. The same thing happens online all the time: perfectly fine words land in the wrong space and cause drama nobody intended.

That's what **digital etiquette** — or **netiquette** ("network" + "etiquette") — is about: matching how you communicate to where you are, and to who's on the other end.

---

## The room you're in changes the rules

The most important idea in this whole lesson: **what's fine in one space can be rude in another.**

| Space | The vibe | What's fine | What's not |
|---|---|---|---|
| Messaging a friend | Informal, casual | "lol k", emojis, short replies | All-caps screaming, ignoring them |
| Email to a teacher | Formal | Full sentences, "Dear/Hello Ms. ____", a sign-off | "k", no greeting, ALL CAPS, no subject line |
| Comment section | Public, on-topic | Thoughtful comments on the post | Spam, drama, tagging friends to start fights |
| Group project chat | Collaborative | Replying to the right message, "I" statements | Assuming bad intent, public blame |

The message *"k"* is perfectly friendly when a friend asks if you're coming to practice. The same *"k"* sent to a teacher in reply to feedback is rude. Same two letters. Different room.

That's the skill — and it's a real skill — of **reading the room**: noticing the tone and expectations of a space before you post.

---

## The problem with text

Here's the core difficulty: writing has no tone, no body language, no facial expression.

In person, you can say "wow, great job" and your face makes it clear you're teasing. Online, the same words are a blank face. The reader guesses — and guesses wrong a lot. Most online "drama" isn't anyone being mean on purpose. It's a joke landing badly, or sarcasm being read as an attack.

A few famous traps:

- **ALL CAPS** is widely read as shouting. Even if you "didn't mean it," the reader hears a shout.
- **Sarcasm and jokes** often don't translate. Many netiquette guides say: either skip them, or explain them ("that's a joke, by the way 🙂").
- **Emojis don't fix everything.** Different people read them differently — an emoji that looks cheeky to you can look rude to someone else. The clearest communication uses words.

The golden rule follows directly: **would I say this in person?** If you wouldn't say it face-to-face, don't post it. There's a real person behind every message — and every message leaves a record that's hard to fully retract.

---

## Worked example: the joke that landed badly

Let's watch a small thing go wrong, and get fixed.

**The setup.** In a group project chat, Jomo finishes his part. Kira types: *"wow, took you long enough, genius."* She means it as a joke — they're friends, they tease.

**The misread.** Jomo doesn't see her face. He reads "genius" as sarcasm. He fires back: *"at least I did something, unlike you."*

**The fire.** Now it's an argument, in front of the whole project group. Nobody wanted this. The group project is the casualty.

Now — the de-escalation skill (you learned the vocabulary in Lesson 10; here's the etiquette version):

**Option 1: Kira repairs it.** She messages him privately: *"hey, that came across differently than I meant, I think. I was joking — your part was actually great."* That's **extending grace** — assuming good intent and giving the other person a way out. Drama dissolves.

**Option 2: Jomo pauses.** Instead of firing back publicly, he asks for clarification: *"wait, was that a joke or are you annoyed?"* Asking instead of assuming turns a fire into a conversation.

The rule underneath both options: **disagreeing is fine; it's the delivery that matters.** And the delivery that works online is clear, kind, and assumes the other person isn't a villain.

## Email to a teacher: the most formal room

Emailing a teacher is the dress-up room of digital communication. Here's the checklist that makes you look like someone who's got it together:

- **Subject line** — a few words saying what it's about: "Question about the project due Friday."
- **A greeting** — "Dear Ms. Awe," or "Hello Mr. Bello," (not "hey").
- **Full sentences** — not chat-speak, not "u", not "lol".
- **Clear message** — state what you need politely.
- **A sign-off** — "Thank you," followed by your name.

And the all-caps rule applies here more than anywhere: **no ALL CAPS.**

This isn't about being stiff. It's about respect — you're showing the reader, in the first two lines, that you see them as someone worth a full sentence.

---

## Group chats and comment sections: the crowded rooms

### In a group chat about a class project:
- **Reply to the message you're replying to.** Nothing confuses a project like a response to the wrong message.
- **Use "I" statements when disagreeing.** "I think we should do X" lands far better than "You're wrong." One attacks the idea; the other attacks the person.
- **Ask for clarification instead of assuming bad intent.** "Did you mean the part from page two?" is a sentence that prevents a war.

### In a comment section:
- **Stay on topic.** The post is about the topic; your comment should be too.
- **No spam.** Don't post the same thing everywhere.
- **No all-caps yelling.**
- **Don't tag friends to start drama.** Tagging people into an argument is how a comment section becomes a battlefield.

---

## More myths, smashed

- **"Netiquette only matters for school."** — No. The same norms apply in games, forums, and social media, and they affect your reputation and digital footprint everywhere.
- **"Emojis make sarcasm obvious."** — Not reliably. Different people read them differently, and emojis don't replace tone. Write clearly in words.
- **"Anonymity means I can say anything."** — Your posts still leave a record, platforms can trace accounts, and your behavior still affects real people.
- **"Formal writing is only for teachers."** — Forums, public comments, and group projects often expect clear, complete, respectful writing too. "u," "lol," and all-caps are context-dependent.
- **"Disagreeing online is rude."** — Disagreement is fine and even valuable; it's the delivery that matters. Attack the idea, not the person.
- **"The 'would I say this in person?' test means never joking."** — It means checking whether the joke would land kindly in person. If it would hurt someone face-to-face, it will hurt online too.

---

## Who enforces the rules?

Mostly, **you and your community do.** Etiquette is enforced informally — by norms, by people deciding what's acceptable, by the group gently pushing back on what isn't.

But posting spaces also have rules and moderators. Break them and your post can be removed, your account can be suspended, and your reputation takes the hit. There are consequences even without a teacher in the room.

And remember the deeper truth from Lesson 8 and Lesson 10: behavior that's repeated, intentional, and aimed at someone from a position of power isn't just "bad manners." It's bullying. Etiquette is the everyday floor; the lessons before this one are what happens when someone punches through the floor.

---

## A note from the research

Honest caveat: how formal online communication *should* be isn't agreed upon. University guides describe quite formal expectations for email and discussion boards. Everyday social media and gaming are much more casual. There is no single universal standard — context decides.

Same with all-caps: it's *widely* read as shouting, but not everyone reads it that way, especially across age groups and languages. So the safe, useful rule is to teach it as "often read as yelling, so avoid it" — rather than as an absolute law of nature.

And one genuinely important finding that experts do agree on: **your intent (what you meant) and your impact (how it was received) can differ.** And the receiver's experience matters. You can *mean* a joke and still *cause* a wound. The grown-up move isn't to defend your intent — it's to repair the impact.

---

## Hold on to this

Digital etiquette is not a list of boring rules. It's a **skill of reading rooms and caring about the people in them.** Ask three questions before you post:

1. **What room am I in?** (Friend? Teacher? Public? Project?)
2. **Who's on the other end?** (A real person, with real feelings.)
3. **Would I say this in person?**

You already use these skills everywhere else — you know not to joke with your headteacher the way you joke with your best friend. You're a master of *reading rooms* in real life. Digital etiquette is just taking that skill you already have and carrying it online, where there's no face to help you. Now you're the one who helps with the words instead.
