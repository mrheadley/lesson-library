# Lesson 6 — Thinking Traps

## Why this matters to you

Imagine reading a message from a friend that just says "ok." Your brain supplies the rest: *she's annoyed with me.* Or you post a photo and watch the likes trickle in slowly, and you think: *nobody actually likes me.*

These thoughts feel true in the moment. That's the catch — and it's the whole point of this lesson: **feelings are not facts.** Thoughts can be examined, tested, and rebuilt. When you learn to spot the automatic patterns behind them, they start to lose their power. That's a skill you can practice, and it gets stronger the more you use it.

## What is a thinking trap?

Psychologists call them **cognitive distortions**: automatic, biased thought patterns that feel true but don't match the evidence. Everyone has them — adults too. They are a normal brain shortcut, not a personal failing and not a sign that something is wrong with you. Simply *knowing and naming* the trap is already an intervention: when you can say "oh, that's mind reading," the thought has less grip on you.

Tech can make traps worse. Online, we lose the facial expressions and tone of voice we rely on face-to-face, so messages get misread easily. And curated, filtered feeds give comparison an endless supply of "evidence." But the traps themselves are human — they existed long before phones.

## Four traps worth knowing well

There are more than four (researchers list anywhere from seven to fifteen), but these four show up again and again in online life. Master these and you'll recognise the family of traps more generally.

### 1. Mind reading

Assuming you know what someone else is thinking or feeling, without evidence.

> **Worked example:** "My friend didn't reply to my message for an hour — she must be furious with me." More likely: she's in class, or her phone died, or she read it and forgot.

Online we can't see the person, so our brain fills in the blank — and it usually fills in the worst. Remember that mind reading works *both ways*: the person who hurt you is probably not thinking about you all the time, either. We usually overestimate how much others notice us.

### 2. All-or-nothing thinking

Seeing things in two extremes — perfect or failure, nothing in between — and leaning on words like *always, never, everyone, nobody*.

> **Worked example:** "I got a B on the test, so I'm a total failure." A B is a good result that leaves room to grow — but the trap sees only two boxes: perfect or useless.

> **Online version:** "Everyone I follow is happier than me." Everyone? Really? One feed, one hundred people — the trap rounds them all up into "everyone."

### 3. Catastrophizing

Taking a small setback and inflating it into a disaster. One bad moment becomes "my life is ruined."

> **Worked example:** "If I trip or stumble in my presentation, everyone will think I'm hopeless and I'll never live it down." Notice the ladder: a stumble → everyone thinks I'm hopeless → I'll never recover. Each step is the trap adding more weight.

A quick note on naming: some sources treat catastrophizing as its own trap, and others fold it under **fortune-telling** (assuming you know what will happen, and that it will be bad). Both names point at the same behaviour — predicting the worst — so don't worry if you hear either. They're the same family.

### 4. Comparison

Measuring yourself against other people's posted lives — which are usually their best moments only.

> **Worked example:** "Everyone's holiday photos look amazing — my life is boring." Remember: nobody posts their boring Tuesday. You're comparing your ordinary moments to everyone else's highlight reel, and the trap gives the reel all the power.

Research shows a rise in teen depressive symptoms alongside the smartphone era, especially among girls — but here's the honest nuance: social media is generally seen as *fuel*, not the sole cause. It pours fuel on comparison; it doesn't start the fire alone.

## More traps in the same family

If these four feel familiar, here are the other members of the family, so you can name them when you meet them:

- **Negative filter** — noticing only the negatives. "I got nine nice comments and one mean one — I can only think about the mean one."
- **Personalizing** — blaming yourself or assuming things are about you. "Two friends made a group chat without me, so they must all hate me." (More likely: they made it for a specific school project.)
- **Labeling** — attaching a negative label to yourself. "I'm a failure."
- **Shoulds** — telling yourself what you *should* or *shouldn't* be.

## Reframing: the skill that fights back

**Reframing** is stopping a distorted thought and replacing it with a more accurate, balanced one. It's important to get this right: reframing is **not** forcing yourself to be positive and ignoring your feelings. It's about *accuracy* — finding a thought that fits the actual evidence, which usually turns out calmer than the automatic version.

The approach comes from Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT), first described by psychologist Aaron Beck in the 1960s. It has four steps:

1. **Catch and name the trap.** "That's catastrophizing."
2. **Examine the evidence.** What's actually true? What's the evidence *for* and *against* this thought?
3. **Ask what you'd tell a friend.** If your best friend had this exact thought, what would you say to them? Be as kind to yourself as you'd be to them.
4. **Generate a more balanced thought.** Build a new sentence that fits the evidence.

### Worked example: the full reframe

**Step 1 — Catch it.** "My post didn't get many likes. Nobody likes me."

**Step 2 — Name the trap.** That's *mind reading* (assuming what "few likes" means about what people feel) mixed with *all-or-nothing thinking* (nobody vs. everyone).

**Step 3 — Evidence.**
- For: the post got few likes.
- Against: I can't see inside anyone's head. Some friends saw it and didn't like it but still like me. The algorithm decides who even sees a post. I have friends who talk to me in person — likes aren't how I know they care.

**Step 4 — Rebuild.** "Likes don't measure my worth. My real friends are the ones who talk to me in person."

That rebuilt sentence is not fake positivity. It's just true — and true happens to be kinder.

### Your own reframe in four steps

Take a real thought you've had recently — something online or something at school. Run it through the four steps. Write the trap's name, the evidence for and against, what you'd tell a friend, and your more balanced thought. Notice that the balanced version usually isn't dramatic. It's just... accurate.

## Things people get wrong about thinking traps

- **"It means something is wrong with me / I'm weak."** No. These are universal — adults have them too — and naming them is already an intervention.
- **"Reframing means ignoring my feelings."** No. Feelings are real and worth respecting. Reframing questions whether the *thought* matches the facts — and it usually doesn't.
- **"My thoughts are facts."** They are not. Thoughts can be tested against evidence, the way you'd test a claim in science class.
- **"Only people with mental-health problems get these."** Distortions appear in everyone under ordinary stress; frequency and intensity are what differ.
- **"More likes or followers would fix how I feel."** External validation doesn't fix a distorted interpretation — noticing and questioning the thought does. Researchers have even found these patterns (like discounting the positive) in teens' social-media posts as a signal of low mood. That's how real these patterns are.

## Quick check — can you do this?

1. Name the trap in this thought: "My friend didn't reply, so she must be mad at me."
2. Name the trap in this thought: "I got a B, so I'm a total failure."
3. Take one thought you've had this week and run it through the four-step reframe.

The traps are quick. They've had practice being quick. But the skill — catching, naming, testing, rebuilding — is yours, and the more you build it, the quieter the traps get.
