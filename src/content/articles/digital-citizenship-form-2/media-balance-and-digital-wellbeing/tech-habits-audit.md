# Lesson 7 — Tech Habits and Values

## Why this matters to you

You reach for your phone without thinking. A hundred times a day, your hand decides before your brain does — at a red light, during a lull, the second a video ends. These automatic behaviours are *habits*, and here's the good news: habits run on autopilot, and autopilot can be changed. But change sticks best when it points somewhere. That's where values come in.

This lesson puts the two together: understand how your tech habits work, decide what you actually care about, and then line the two up so your technology supports your values instead of quietly working against them. The goal is **balance and intention, not avoidance.** Nobody is asking you to throw away your phone.

## How habits work: cue → routine → reward

A **habit** is an automatic routine you do without really thinking about it. Psychologists describe every habit as a three-part loop:

- **Cue** — the trigger that starts the habit: a time of day, a place, a feeling like boredom, or even just the sight of your phone.
- **Routine** — the behaviour itself.
- **Reward** — what your brain gets out of it.

The loop was popularised by Charles Duhigg in *The Power of Habit*, and it gives us a reliable method for change: **keep the same cue and the same reward, but swap in a different routine.** You don't have to fight the trigger or the payoff — you just change the middle step.

### Worked example: the bedtime loop

- **Cue:** getting into bed.
- **Routine:** grabbing the phone and scrolling.
- **Reward:** a few minutes of distraction — but it steals sleep.

Same cue (bedtime), same reward (something calm before sleep). New routine: leaving the phone to charge outside the bedroom, or reading a few pages instead. The cue still fires; the payoff still comes; only the middle step changed. That's why this method beats raw willpower — willpower alone usually fails because habits run on autopilot in the brain. Change the cue and the environment, and the brain changes with you.

### Worked example: the boredom cue

- **Cue:** standing in a queue, or waiting for a bus.
- **Routine:** hand reaches for the phone automatically.
- **Reward:** the moment fills.

New routine for the same cue: pause and ask, *"What do I actually want here?"* Sometimes the honest answer is "nothing — I just got trained to fill every empty second." A deliberate break can be better than a grab.

## Mindless vs intentional use

There are two very different ways to use technology:

- **Mindless scrolling** — swiping through content automatically, with no purpose, usually to fill a moment. It's triggered by boredom, stress, or a familiar cue.
- **Intentional use** — using tech on purpose, toward a goal that matters to you. It starts from a question: *why am I opening this?*

Here's what the research points to: **passive, mindless scrolling is linked to more anxiety and worse mood than purposeful use.** It's not the tool, and it's not the minutes — it's the *how*. A focused hour learning can beat a scattered three hours scrolling. Pediatric experts agree: how tech fits into your sleep, movement, and relationships matters more than a single number of minutes.

> **One honest note:** guidance sometimes talks about time *limits* and sometimes about *quality over quantity*. Both views appear in the research. For you, the strongest frame is intentionality and values rather than a strict minute count — and worth remembering that the evidence on whether screen time "causes" harm is largely correlational, so the balanced view is: use that supports your values tends to be the healthiest.

## Start with values, not tech

The most productive conversations about tech and well-being **start with who you are, not with the device.** Values are the principles or personal rules that are important to you — shaped by your experiences, your identity, and your culture. They're the answer to the question *"What kind of person do I want to be?"*

Technology can **support** your values:

- **Kindness** — using the group chat to check on a friend who seemed down, instead of forwarding an embarrassing photo of someone.
- **Connection** — a video call with a grandparent in another country, or using tutorials to learn a skill to share with a friend.
- **Balance** — swapping an hour of late-night gaming for an earlier night's sleep so you're sharper in class; setting an app timer that gently reminds you.

And the same technology can **undercut** your values:

- **Balance** — late-night scrolling quietly eats the sleep you need for tomorrow.
- **Kindness to yourself** — a comparison feed makes you feel small.

Notice the key idea: **the same tool can support one value and undercut another at the same time.** The question is never "is this app good or bad?" The question is *alignment*: does this use move you toward what you care about, or away from it?

### Worked example: values alignment check

Your value: *connection*. A video call with a faraway grandparent → aligned, supports it. An hour of staring at a stranger's highlight reel → not aligned, and it doesn't even feel connected. Same device, same hour, completely different direction.

## Your own habit challenge

Here's the challenge model — small, specific, and doable, because that's what actually sticks:

1. **Interview a partner** about their tech habits. Ask: *"When do you reach for your phone without thinking?"* Most people discover cues they never noticed.
2. **Reflect on your own.** Pick one routine and map it: what's the cue? the routine? the reward? Which value does it support, and which does it undercut?
3. **Set one small challenge.** Make the old habit *harder* and the new habit *easier* — that's the cue-and-environment trick at work.

### Sample challenges

- "For three days, no phone during family dinner."
- "Phone stays in the bag during homework."
- "For one evening, autoplay off and a digital sunset — screens winding down at a set time before bed."

### Protective habits worth keeping

- **Tech-free zones** — places like the dinner table or the bedroom where devices are deliberately left out.
- **Turn off non-essential notifications** — fewer cues, fewer loops.
- **Pause before unlocking** — the "why am I opening this?" question again.
- **Curate the feed** — follow people and accounts that support your values, not ones that shrink you.
- **Screen-time awareness tools** — they put a stopping cue back in.
- **A digital sunset** — wind down screens at a set time before bed to protect sleep.

## What people get wrong about tech habits

- **"Healthy tech use means no tech."** No — the whole frame is balance, not avoidance. The goal is intentional, values-aligned use, not unplugging forever.
- **"Changing a habit is just willpower; if I fail, I'm weak."** Habits run on autopilot, so willpower alone usually fails. Changing the *cue and environment* (phone out of reach, autoplay off) works far better.
- **"More screen time automatically means more harm."** The evidence points to *how* you use tech mattering more than the raw minutes.
- **"Tech is either all good or all bad."** The same tool can support one value and undercut another at the same time. The question is alignment, not the tool.
- **"A habit change has to be big to count."** The opposite is true: small, specific, doable challenges are more likely to stick.

**One last honest note:** you may have heard that habits form in exactly 21 days. The research doesn't support a magic number — it varies wildly from person to person, and consistency matters far more than any day-count. Don't chase the number. Chase the direction.

## Quick check — can you do this?

1. Map one of your own routines into cue → routine → reward.
2. Name one value you care about, and one way tech supports it and one way tech undercuts it.
3. Choose one small, doable habit challenge to try this week.

The phone is a tool, and tools work for the person who knows what they want. You're that person now.
