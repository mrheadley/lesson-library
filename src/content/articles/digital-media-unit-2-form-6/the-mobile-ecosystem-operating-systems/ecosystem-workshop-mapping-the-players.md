# Mapping the Mobile Ecosystem: Players, Flows, and Control Points

## What an Ecosystem Map Is (and Isn't)

Your phone only feels like a single object. In reality it is the end of a long chain — every time you send a voice note, a dozen companies touched that message before it arrived. The **mobile ecosystem** is the web of players, relationships, and flows that make a phone useful: not just the phone and the operator, but the network equipment, the app store, the app developer, the regulator, the cable under the sea, the advertiser, and the money moving between them.

An **ecosystem map** is a snapshot of that web: it lists the players, then draws the relationships — who pays whom, who sends data to whom, who depends on whom. GSMA, the mobile industry body, sorts the players into three buckets:

- **Mobile operators** — the companies licensed to run radio networks and sell service (Digicel, FLOW).
- **Infrastructure and equipment** — tower companies, network gear vendors, device makers, and IoT hardware.
- **Content and services** — app developers, content providers, retailers, and cloud platforms.

A good map is not one picture but **three graphs laid over the same actors**: a player graph (who connects to whom), a money graph (who pays whom), and a data graph (who sends whose information where). Mapping the people is only the first third of the task.

## The Anatomy of a Complete Map

Students who produce shallow maps draw the players and stop. A complete map answers four further questions:

1. **Relationships** — who is linked to whom, and in which direction?
2. **Money flows** — which way revenue actually moves: a top-up, a subscription, an ad, a commission.
3. **Data flows** — where your usage, location, and contacts travel, and who reads them.
4. **Control points** — where one actor's permission is *required*, so that actor can dictate terms and capture value.

A **control point** is the most analytical part of the map. Think of the gatekeeper with the keys: to reach customers you must pass through them, and they can tax the passage. The app store is the classic example — Apple's App Store and Google's Play Store together controlled **over 90% of native app downloads** in the UK per a 2022 Competition & Markets Authority study, with Apple's store running at an estimated **75–100% gross margin**. A national map should make clear that the OS and app store sit above any single country.

The map should also **tell a story**. The test of a finished map: can it narrate one complete journey — how a call is made, or how an app purchase is paid for — from start to finish?

## Structuring a Country's Mobile Landscape

To avoid drawing one unreadable spiderweb, separate levels and fix a boundary. **Bound the map**: choose one country or scenario and decide what is inside scope versus what is global. For a small Caribbean island, the OS, app store, cloud, and biggest apps are global actors that still reach into every phone — they must appear on the map, labelled as external.

A workable structure for any national map:

- **Regulation layer** — spectrum licence, regulator, consumer protection. Spectrum is scarce and state-owned; a regulator decides who may use it.
- **Network layer** — operators, tower companies, and backhaul. For a Caribbean island this extends to **submarine cables** (Americas-II, ECFS, SSCS, ARCOS), which carry almost all international traffic and are a resilience risk in hurricane season.
- **Distribution layer** — app stores, the OS platform, carrier billing. This is where gatekeeping lives.
- **Content and services layer** — apps, local creators, media, e-commerce, payment platforms.
- **Money layer** — who pays whom, drawn as labelled arrows.

### A Worked Mini-Map: A Typical Duopoly Island

Take a composite Caribbean country somewhere between Jamaica and Barbados. Population in the low millions, two operators, one national regulator. Here is the skeleton:

| Player | Category | Role on the map |
|--------|----------|-----------------|
| Digicel | Mobile operator | Licensed network; sells prepaid bundles |
| FLOW (Liberty) | Mobile operator | Second licensed network; duopoly competitor |
| National regulator (e.g. OUR/TATT) | Regulator | Licences operators, sets interconnection and consumer rules |
| Spectrum authority (e.g. SMA) | Regulator | Assigns radio frequencies |
| Submarine-cable consortium | Infrastructure | Carries international traffic between the island and the world |
| Apple / Google | Global platform | App stores and OS; control point over apps and payments |
| Local content creators | Content & services | YouTube channels, radio/podcasts, news sites riding over the operator |
| WhatsApp / TikTok | Content & services (OTT) | Free-to-user apps that carry calls and data over both networks |
| Device makers (imported) | Infrastructure & equipment | Phones imported into the country; no local manufacturing |
| Advertisers / payment platforms | Content & services | Fund "free" apps and collect transaction data |

Follow one text message with a picture: it travels **device → Digicel tower → backhaul → submarine cable → peer operator overseas → recipient**. Reverse direction, and the money for terminating that traffic flows back — interconnection and peering agreements between networks are themselves a money flow. Now follow a purchase: a student buys a Digicel top-up to cover an app subscription; money moves **consumer → operator → app store (commission of roughly 15–30%) → developer**, while data flows back for personalisation and ads. The "free" app was never free — someone paid at every hop.

Pull back to the sector scale. The Caribbean mobile market earned roughly **USD 5.9 billion in revenue in 2025** (GSMA Mobile Economy Caribbean 2026), while mobile technologies support around **5.8% of global GDP and ~50 million jobs** (GSMA Mobile Economy 2025/2026). Most markets are duopolies — two operators (Jamaica: Digicel and FLOW; Trinidad: Digicel and FLOW-owned TSTT). The Dominican Republic runs three (Claro, Altice, Digicel); Puerto Rico has T-Mobile, Claro, and AT&T. Watch the difference a third entrant makes: more players shift money flows and consumer choice, but even with three, regulators still watch prices.

## Finding the Data

You do not have to invent the map; the pieces are public:

- **GSMA** — *Mobile Economy Caribbean*, coverage and revenue datasets (gsma.com).
- **Regulators** — annual market reports from OUR (Jamaica), TATT (Trinidad & Tobago), ECTEL (Dominica, Grenada, St. Kitts & Nevis, St. Lucia, St. Vincent — a regional regulator created by treaty in May 2000), INDOTEL (DR), CONATEL (Haiti), PUC (Guyana).
- **Hope Research Group** — Caribbean operator and penetration tables.
- **UK CMA / GSMA value-chain work** — the global app-store and value-chain layers (content rights, online services, enabling technology, access/connectivity, user interface).

Cross-check two sources when a figure matters. Regulators know who is licensed; GSMA knows what is sold; app-store statistics sit with the platform owners.

## Common Misconceptions

The errors that most often sink a student map:

- **"The ecosystem is just the phone and the operator."** Incomplete — include app stores, OS owners, regulators, spectrum, cables, content providers, payment platforms, advertisers, and users.
- **"More operators always means a better market."** Even with two or three players, market power persists; regulators still intervene on prices and interconnection.
- **"The apps I use are free, so no money moves."** Ads, data, and app-store commissions move money invisibly. Someone always pays.
- **"My operator handles my call end to end."** International calls and data cross other operators, cable owners, and peering points — multiple companies, multiple fee flows.
- **"Everything is local."** The OS, app store, cloud, and key apps are controlled by global firms outside the country; a national map must show external actors.
- **"Digitise by drawing one giant web."** Strong maps split into layers (players, money, data, regulation) to stay readable.

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| Mobile Network Operator (MNO) | Company licensed to run its own radio network and sell service |
| Mobile Virtual Network Operator (MVNO) | Sells service on another operator's network without owning infrastructure |
| Spectrum Licence | Regulatory right to use specific radio frequencies; spectrum is scarce and state-owned |
| Duopoly | A market served by only two competing operators |
| Regulator | State body that licences operators, sets rules, and watches prices |
| Control Point | A spot where one actor's permission is required, letting that actor dictate terms |
| Money / Data Flow | Movement of payment between players / of usage information between players |
| Interconnection / Peering | Arrangements under which different networks exchange calls and data |
| OTT (Over-the-Top) | Apps riding on a network without operator involvement in delivery or revenue |

## Summary

An ecosystem map lists the players and then draws the relationships, money flows, and data flows among them — three graphs on one set of actors. The most valuable thing to hunt for is the control point: the app store, the OS, the regulator, the carrier that gates access and therefore captures value. Bound the map to a country, split it into layers so it stays readable, include the global actors that reach into every local phone, and make it tell one end-to-end story. On a Caribbean duopoly island the map spans two operators, one national regulator, submarine cables, imported devices, local creators, and the international platforms above them all — and every "free" app in it is financed by a chain of money moves you can trace with arrows.
