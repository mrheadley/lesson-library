# Enabling Policy & Regulation

## The Rules Beneath Everything

Every player in the mobile ecosystem — content, apps, infrastructure, devices, carriers, users — operates inside a set of conditions set by **policy and regulation**. This is the enabling layer: who may operate, on what spectrum, under what obligations, and with what user protections.

Think of regulation not as red tape but as the **rules of a game everyone must play**. Without them, there would be chaos: two operators broadcasting on the same frequencies, apps handling your data with no accountability, and no one obliged to connect rural communities. Policy is what makes competition, choice, and new entrants possible at all.

Four pillars define this layer: **spectrum allocation, net neutrality, data protection/privacy law, and regional regulation**.

## Spectrum Allocation: The Scarce Public Resource

Radio frequencies are a **scarce public resource**. The airwaves are not owned by phone companies — they are assigned by regulators. How those frequencies are planned, licensed (by auction, tender, or fixed assignment), and capped shapes how many operators can serve a market.

In Trinidad and Tobago, **TATT** (the Telecommunications Authority) plans and manages spectrum under the Telecommunications Act. Its Spectrum Plan allocates bands for public mobile services (700 MHz, 850 MHz, 1900 MHz, AWS 1.7/2.1 GHz, 2.5 GHz, and lower 3.5 GHz) — with **spectrum caps** so that up to **three cellular operators** can be accommodated. Trinidad and Tobago follows **ITU-R Region 2** allocations, and TATT updates its national Frequency Allocation Table (TTFAT) to track World Radiocommunication Conferences like WRC-23.

**Why does this affect your plan price?** Competition requires spectrum. If one operator hoards the airwaves, fewer players compete, and prices tend to rise. Spectrum caps are the regulators' lever to keep the market open. Whether your town has a true third-operator choice often comes down to how spectrum was planned and licensed.

Caribbean 5G rollout shows how spectrum becomes real-world service: commercial 5G reached **Jamaica, Trinidad, the Cayman Islands, and Puerto Rico by late 2025**, with the Dominican Republic targeting nationwide 5G by end-2026.

## Net Neutrality: Equal Treatment of Traffic

**Net neutrality** is the principle that carriers treat all lawful internet traffic equally — no blocking, no throttling, no paid "fast lanes" for companies that pay more.

Its legal status is the most contested rule in this lesson, and it has **flipped repeatedly**:

- **2015**: The US FCC applies "Title II" rules treating broadband as a utility.
- **2017–2018**: The "Restoring Internet Freedom" order repeals those rules (adopted December 2017, effective in 2018 — sources differ on which year to cite).
- **April–July 2024**: The FCC reinstates Title II rules.
- **2 January 2025**: The Sixth Circuit strikes the rules down (*Ohio Telecom v. FCC*), ruling the FCC lacked authority — following the Supreme Court's 2024 *Loper Bright* decision ending Chevron deference.

As of **2026 there are no binding federal net neutrality rules in the US**. The current FCC formally removed them (Federal Register, August 2025). This is genuinely unsettled — sources diverge on exact dates and on which year to call the repeal, and on the precise count of US states (some say the number of states with active measures is roughly 8; others list 5 comprehensive plus 3 contracting states). What's clear: protection is now **uneven and state-dependent**, with states like California (SB-822), Washington, Oregon, Colorado, and Vermont enforcing their own rules.

The broader lesson stands regardless of jurisdiction: **who controls the network controls what you can reach**, and neutrality rules decide whether that power is constrained.

## Data Protection & Privacy Law

Mobile devices capture a staggering amount of personal data — every app install, every message, every location. Data protection law governs how that data may be **collected, used, stored, shared, and transferred**.

The Caribbean picture is honest but **uneven**. There is no single regional law; each country has progressed differently.

| Jurisdiction | Law | Status (as of 2025) |
|--------------|-----|---------------------|
| Jamaica | Data Protection Act, 2020 | **In force** — Information Commissioner, 72-hour breach-notification duty, GDPR-style principles |
| Barbados | Data Protection Act, 2019 | **In force** — Data Protection Commissioner, rights incl. erasure and portability, restricted cross-border transfers |
| Trinidad & Tobago | Data Protection Act, 2011 | **Only partially proclaimed** — core obligations and penalties not yet fully in force as of Feb 2025; a 2018–19 review flagged gaps vs. GDPR |
| The Bahamas | Data Protection Act, 2025 | **New Act passed** (replacing the older Privacy of Personal Information Act), setting up a Data Protection Commissioner |

Note the honest caveat: some sources imply T&T's regime is fully enforceable, but the more precise position is that the Act is only partially proclaimed with key obligations unenforced. The practical takeaway for a student: **when you sign up for an app, the rules about your data depend on where you (and the service) are based** — those rules are not the same everywhere in the Caribbean. Jamaica's 72-hour breach-notification duty is a concrete, GDPR-style feature worth remembering.

## Regional Regulation & the Single ICT Space

The Caribbean pairs **national regulators** with **regional bodies** to harmonise policy.

| Body | Role |
|------|------|
| TATT (Trinidad & Tobago) | Licenses, plans spectrum, protects consumers/competition |
| URCA (The Bahamas) | Regulator; licensing satellite operators like Starlink, Viasat, SES |
| OUR (Jamaica) | National regulator |
| CARICOM / CTU | Regional telecom and ICT coordination |
| CANTO | Regional telecom association (40th anniversary in 2025) |
| ECTEL | Eastern Caribbean regional regulator |

The flagship regional project is the **CARICOM Single ICT Space** — a vision of a "borderless ICT space" as the digital layer of the Caribbean Single Market and Economy (CSME). Mandated at the 2014 CHOG and road-mapped through the CTU, it builds on the CARICOM Digital Agenda to harmonise how the region connects.

The region faces a real connectivity gap: **CANTO** noted that roughly **15 million Caribbean citizens still lack mobile broadband** and **~4 million households lack fibre**, and proposed that digital infrastructure be treated as a shared responsibility. CARICOM heads agreed in February 2025 to **review the regional telecom environment and platform economics** — including how much revenue global platforms (Netflix, WhatsApp/Meta) earn from Caribbean users and how little they contribute to regional networks.

Liberalisation is the other big Caribbean policy story. **Guyana opened its telecom sector on 5 October 2020**, enabling new operators like ENet — a clean case of policy enabling ecosystem growth, followed by new cables and data-centre ambitions.

Policymaking also adapts in emergencies: after Hurricane Melissa in 2025, regulators and operators **quickly permitted satellite Direct-to-Cell (Starlink) emergency SMS** in Jamaica and The Bahamas — a real demonstration of policy flexing for disaster resilience.

## Common Misconceptions

### "Phone companies choose their own frequencies freely"
No. Spectrum is a scarce public resource allocated and licensed by regulators, often via auction or tender with caps.

### "Data protection law is the same everywhere in the Caribbean"
No. Jamaica and Barbados have in-force GDPR-style acts, T&T's 2011 Act remains only partially proclaimed, and The Bahamas passed a new Act in 2025.

### "Net neutrality is a settled, permanent rule"
No. It has flipped repeatedly — 2015, repealed in 2018, restored in 2024, struck down in January 2025. As of 2026 the US has no binding federal rule, but several states enforce their own.

### "Regulation is just red tape that slows progress"
No. Enabling policy — licensing, liberalisation, spectrum planning, privacy — is what allows competition, choice, and new entrants (and digital safety) to exist at all.

## Summary

Enabling policy and regulation set the conditions every other ecosystem player operates under. Spectrum is a scarce public resource allocated by regulators with caps to keep markets competitive; net neutrality, though unsettled and currently absent as a US federal rule, governs whether carriers can shape what you reach; data protection law varies sharply across the Caribbean, with some acts fully in force and others partly so; and regional bodies like CARICOM, CTU, and CANTO work toward a harmonised Single ICT Space. For the digital media student, these rules are not abstract — they determine what you can build, what it costs to reach an audience, and what anyone may do with a user's data.
