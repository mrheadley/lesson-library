# Web-Friendly Writing Criteria

## Introduction

You've probably experienced this: you click on a search result, land on a page, and within three seconds you've decided whether to stay or hit the back button. The page has a massive wall of text, no headings, tiny font, and no clear point. You leave. You never read a word.

Now think about the pages you actually stay on. They have clear headings that tell you what each section covers. Short paragraphs that don't overwhelm your eyes. Bullet points that make key information easy to find. Images and white space that give your brain room to breathe. That's not an accident — it's web writing, and it's a specific, learnable skill.

Writing for the web is different from writing an essay, a letter, or even a social media caption. Web readers don't read — they scan. Studies show only about 20% of the text on any given web page actually gets read in detail. If your content isn't structured for scanning, most of it will never be consumed. This article teaches you how to write content that works on screens.

*Analogy:* Writing for the web is like designing a highway sign. Drivers have seconds to read it at speed. The sign needs to be clear, large, and direct — no long paragraphs, no fancy language, no ambiguity. "EXIT 14 — OCHO RIOS 2 KM" works. A poetic description of the Ocho Rios exit doesn't. Web readers are driving past your content at highway speed. Write for them accordingly.

## Part 1: The Rules of Web Writing

### Write Like a Human

The fundamental rule of web writing is deceptively simple: write like a human. Content should be concise, readable, and scannable while maintaining a conversational, accessible tone appropriate for the audience. This means no jargon without explanation, no unnecessarily complex vocabulary, and no academic formality unless your audience expects it.

### Scannable Content Is Non-Negotiable

Web readers scan rather than read linearly. Effective web writing accommodates this behaviour through specific structural choices:

| Element | Purpose | Best Practice |
|---------|---------|---------------|
| **Headings** | Signposts that help readers decide whether to read a section | Descriptive and specific — "How to Reduce Your Carbon Footprint" beats "Going Green" |
| **Subheadings** | Break content into digestible chunks | Use them every 200-300 words in blog posts |
| **Short paragraphs** | Prevent overwhelming text walls | 2-4 sentences maximum |
| **Bullet points** | Make lists scannable | Use for any collection of 3+ related items |
| **Numbered lists** | Show sequence or priority | Use for step-by-step instructions |
| **Bold text** | Highlight key terms and ideas | Bold the most important phrase in each section |
| **White space** | Give eyes and brain room to breathe | Don't fill every pixel — empty space is functional |

### Headings as Signposts

Headings do more than break up text — they function as a table of contents that readers use to decide what to read. A reader should be able to scan just the headings and understand the main points of your article.

**Good headings:** Specific, descriptive, action-oriented
- "How to Write a Creative Brief in 5 Steps"
- "Why Caribbean Tourism Needs Localised Content"
- "Common Phishing Tactics Targeting Students"

**Bad headings:** Vague, generic, uninformative
- "Introduction"
- "Some Thoughts"
- "More About This"

### Short Paragraphs Save Attention

On screens, especially mobile devices, long text blocks appear overwhelming. A paragraph that looks fine on a printed page looks like a wall on a phone screen. Visual breathing room encourages readers to continue.

**Rule of thumb:** If a paragraph exceeds four sentences, break it up. If a sentence exceeds 25 words, consider splitting it.

## Part 2: SEO Writing — Getting Found

### What SEO Actually Is

**SEO** (Search Engine Optimisation) is the practice of writing and structuring content so that search engines like Google can find it and show it to people searching for relevant topics. SEO is not about tricking search engines — it's about creating content that is discoverable, accessible, and valuable to human readers.

### Keywords — The Foundation

Keywords are the words and phrases that people type into search engines. Effective SEO writing incorporates relevant keywords naturally into titles, headings, opening paragraphs, and throughout the content — without overusing them.

**Keyword stuffing** (repeating the same keyword so many times that the content reads unnaturally) is penalised by search engines and creates a terrible reader experience. Modern SEO rewards natural, helpful content over keyword manipulation.

### Long-Tail Keywords

Long-tail keywords are specific phrases (like "handmade leather wallet with RFID blocking") rather than broad terms (like "wallet"). They're less competitive and often more effective, attracting more qualified traffic — people who know exactly what they're looking for.

| Keyword Type | Example | Competition | Intent |
|-------------|---------|-------------|--------|
| **Broad** | "shoes" | Very high | Vague — could be anything |
| **Medium** | "running shoes Trinidad" | Moderate | Clearer — looking for running shoes in a specific place |
| **Long-tail** | "best running shoes for flat feet in Trinidad and Tobago" | Low | Very specific — ready to buy |

For Caribbean content creators, long-tail keywords that include island names, local terms, or regional references can be powerful. "Best roti shop in Port of Spain" is easier to rank for than "best roti shop."

### Meta Descriptions

Meta descriptions are short summaries (150-160 characters) that appear below page titles in search results. They don't directly affect search rankings, but they heavily influence whether someone clicks on your result. Include the primary keyword and give readers a compelling reason to click.

**Good meta description:** "Learn how to write web content that Caribbean audiences actually read. Practical tips on SEO, readability, and scannable writing for CAPE Digital Media students."

**Bad meta description:** "This article is about writing for the web and covers various topics related to digital media."

### Alt Text — Accessibility First

Alt text (alternative text) describes images for screen readers used by visually impaired people and for search engines. Good alt text is descriptive, concise, and includes relevant keywords where appropriate.

**Good alt text:** "CAPE Digital Media student filming a promotional video with a smartphone on a tripod at a school in Jamaica."

**Bad alt text:** "image" or "photo123.jpg"

Alt text is primarily an accessibility feature. The SEO benefit is secondary but real — search engines use alt text to understand what images show.

## Part 3: Readability — Writing People Can Actually Understand

### Measuring Readability

Readability scores measure how easy a piece of text is to read. They're not just academic exercises — they directly correlate with how many people actually read and understand your content, affecting engagement, conversions, and search rankings.

The most commonly used tool is the **Flesch Reading Ease** score:

| Score Range | Reading Level | Best For |
|-------------|--------------|----------|
| **90-100** | Very easy (5th grade) | Children's content, very simple instructions |
| **60-70** | Standard (8th-9th grade) | General web content — **target this range** |
| **30-50** | Fairly difficult (college level) | Academic, technical, or professional content |
| **0-30** | Very difficult (graduate level) | Legal documents, dense academic papers |

Most web content should target a Flesch Reading Ease score of 60-70. This doesn't mean "dumbing down" — it means writing clearly enough that a broad audience can understand your message.

### The Hemingway Editor

The Hemingway Editor is a free online tool that highlights complex sentences, passive voice, adverbs, and difficult words. It encourages shorter sentences, active voice, and plain language. It's a practical tool you can use to check any piece of writing before publishing.

*Note:* The Hemingway Editor may flag Caribbean dialect or Creole as "complex" language. This is a limitation of the tool, which is calibrated for Standard American English. Use it as a guide, not a rulebook.

### Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level

The Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level estimates the US school grade level needed to understand the text. Most web content should target grade 6-9 for general audiences. Technical, legal, or medical content can be higher when the audience expects specialised terminology.

### Why Readability Matters for SEO

Readability is now considered an indirect ranking factor for SEO because clear writing improves engagement metrics — longer session durations, lower bounce rates, and better user satisfaction signals to search engines that content is valuable. In plain terms: if people stay on your page and read it, Google assumes it's good content and shows it to more people.

### Common Readability Mistakes

| Mistake | Why It's a Problem | Fix |
|---------|-------------------|-----|
| Complex vocabulary without explanation | Loses readers who don't know the terms | Use plain language; explain technical terms |
| Passive voice throughout | Sounds bureaucratic and indirect | Switch to active voice: "The teacher graded the essays" not "The essays were graded by the teacher" |
| Walls of text without headings | Impossible to scan | Add headings every 200-300 words |
| Optimising for search engines only | Content might rank but nobody reads it | Write for humans first, search engines second |

## Part 4: Writing for Different Web Formats

### Blog Posts

Blog posts typically range from 500-2,500 words, use a conversational tone, include headings every 200-300 words, feature internal and external links, and end with a call to action or discussion question. They should provide value through education, entertainment, or inspiration.

**Blog post structure:**

1. **Hook** — Opening paragraph that grabs attention (question, statistic, relatable scenario)
2. **Context** — Why this matters to the reader
3. **Main content** — Organised under clear headings, with examples and practical tips
4. **Summary** — Key takeaways or a brief recap
5. **Call to action** — What should the reader do next? (Comment, share, try something)

### Landing Pages

Landing pages are standalone pages designed for a single conversion goal. They use concise, persuasive copy, a clear value proposition, social proof (testimonials), trust signals, and a prominent call to action. Simplicity is key — the most effective landing pages are often the simplest.

**Landing page structure:**

1. **Headline** — Clear value proposition (What will the visitor get?)
2. **Supporting text** — Brief explanation (2-3 sentences)
3. **Social proof** — Testimonials, reviews, numbers ("Join 500 students who...")
4. **Call to action** — One clear button or link ("Register Now," "Download Free Guide")
5. **Trust signals** — Credentials, awards, security badges

### Product Descriptions

Product descriptions should focus on benefits rather than just features, tell a story about how the product solves a problem, include relevant keywords for search visibility, and use bullet points for scannability. Each product needs a unique description to avoid duplicate content penalties.

**Worked Example — Feature vs. Benefit:**

| Feature | Benefit |
|---------|---------|
| "Waterproof canvas" | "Your bag stays dry even in a tropical downpour" |
| "128GB storage" | "Store all your CAPE coursework, photos, and music without running out of space" |
| "Adjustable straps" | "Comfortable for every body type — no one-size-fits-all discomfort" |

### All Formats Share These Requirements

Regardless of format, all web content benefits from:
- Mobile-responsive formatting (looks good on phones and tablets)
- Fast loading considerations (avoid unnecessarily heavy files)
- Accessibility features (alt text, heading hierarchy, sufficient colour contrast)
- Clear calls to action (tell the reader what to do next)

## Part 5: The Caribbean Web Writing Perspective

### Multilingual Audiences

Caribbean web writers must consider multilingual audiences — content may need to work for English, French, Spanish, and Creole-speaking readers across the region. Code-switching (mixing languages in a single piece of content) and culturally specific references can create connection but may limit broader reach.

The key is balance: authentic Caribbean voice that is also globally readable. You don't have to sound American to succeed on the web. But you do need to be clear.

### Bandwidth Considerations

Internet infrastructure varies across the Caribbean. Web writing should consider bandwidth constraints: compressed images, efficient code, and avoiding unnecessarily heavy multimedia can improve user experience on slower connections. This isn't just a technical consideration — it affects writing choices too. A page that loads slowly because of heavy images will lose readers before they see your words.

### Localised Content

Caribbean digital marketers emphasise that localised content — tailored to specific island markets rather than treating "the Caribbean" as monolithic — significantly improves engagement and conversion rates. What resonates in Kingston doesn't necessarily resonate in Bridgetown. Content about " Caribbean culture" should be specific: mention the actual island, the actual community, the actual traditions.

Caribbean businesses increasingly need web content in multiple languages. Spanish-speaking markets (Dominican Republic, Puerto Rico) and French/Creole markets (Haiti, Martinique, Guadeloupe) require localised content strategies that go beyond simple translation.

### Tourism Content

Tourism is a major industry across the Caribbean, making travel and hospitality content a significant area of web writing opportunity. Effective tourism content combines practical information with evocative storytelling — not just "our hotel has 200 rooms" but "wake up to the sound of waves on a beach that's been called the most beautiful in the Caribbean."

## Part 6: Putting It All Together

### Worked Example: Before and After

**Before (poor web writing):**

"Welcome to our website. We are a school that has been around for many years and we provide quality education to students in the Caribbean. Our school has many facilities and programmes that are designed to help students succeed in their academic careers and prepare them for the future. We invite you to learn more about what we have to offer by reading through the information on this page."

**Problems:** No headings, no structure, generic language, tells rather than shows, no call to action, reads like an essay.

**After (effective web writing):**

## Why Choose [School Name]?

**For over 30 years**, we've helped Caribbean students succeed — not just in exams, but in life.

### What Makes Us Different

- **Small class sizes** — Your teachers know your name, not just your student number
- **Strong CAPE results** — Consistently above regional averages
- **Real-world projects** — Students build portfolios, not just take exams

### Our Programmes

| Programme | Duration | Key Features |
|-----------|----------|--------------|
| CAPE Digital Media | 2 years | Industry-standard software, portfolio development |
| CAPE Environmental Science | 2 years | Fieldwork component, university pathway |
| CAPE Spanish | 2 years | Immersion exchange programme |

### See for Yourself

**[Register for Open Day →]**

**Improvements:** Clear headings, scannable structure, specific details, bullet points, table, call to action, conversational tone, Caribbean context.

### Worked Example: Caribbean Blog Post Opening

**Weak opening:**

"This article discusses the importance of web writing in the Caribbean context. It will cover several topics related to digital content creation and best practices for online communication."

**Strong opening:**

"Last week, a local bakery in Kingston launched a website. Beautiful photos, great menu, perfect location. One problem: nobody could find it on Google. Why? The content was written like a brochure, not like a web page. No headings, no keywords, no structure. If you're creating content for the Caribbean web, this guide will make sure your work gets found — and actually read."

The strong opening uses a specific, relatable scenario. It names a place (Kingston). It identifies the problem concretely. It promises a benefit. It hooks the reader emotionally.

## Common Misconceptions

| Misconception | Reality |
|---------------|---------|
| "Longer content is always better for SEO" | Quality and relevance matter more than length. A concise, well-structured 800-word post often outperforms a rambling 3,000-word post. |
| "SEO means keyword stuffing" | Modern SEO rewards natural, helpful content. Keyword stuffing is penalised by search engines and creates poor user experience. |
| "Readability scores are just academic exercises" | These scores directly correlate with how many people actually read and understand your content, affecting engagement, conversions, and search rankings. |
| "Web writing and print writing are the same" | Web readers scan, skip, and have shorter attention spans. Content must be structured differently for the web than for print. |
| "Alt text is only for SEO" | Alt text is primarily an accessibility feature for visually impaired users. SEO benefit is secondary. |
| "Caribbean content needs to sound American to succeed" | Authentic Caribbean voice can be a competitive advantage, especially for regional audiences, as long as the content remains clear and accessible. |
| "If it reads well aloud, it works on the web" | Web reading is visual and non-linear. Content that reads well aloud may still fail as web content if it lacks scannable structure. |
| "Writing for the web is easy — just type it up" | Effective web writing requires understanding of audience, structure, SEO, readability, and accessibility. It is a professional skill that improves with practice. |

## Key Takeaways

1. **Web readers scan, not read.** Only about 20% of page content gets read in detail. Structure everything for scanning.
2. **Headings are signposts.** Make them descriptive and specific — readers use them to decide what to read.
3. **Short paragraphs are critical.** 2-4 sentences maximum. Long text blocks drive readers away, especially on mobile.
4. **SEO is about being helpful**, not about keyword tricks. Write naturally, use relevant keywords, and focus on quality.
5. **Target a Flesch Reading Ease score of 60-70** for general web content. Use tools like the Hemingway Editor to check your work.
6. **Different formats have different rules** — blog posts, landing pages, and product descriptions each have specific structures and best practices.
7. **Caribbean web writers** need to consider multilingual audiences, bandwidth constraints, localised content, and the balance between authentic voice and global readability.
8. **Alt text is accessibility first**, SEO second. Every image should have descriptive alt text.
9. **Web writing is a professional skill** in demand across the Caribbean digital economy — from tourism marketing to e-commerce to government communications.
