# Marketing Campaign Workshop & Case Studies: Learning by Doing and by Studying

## Two Ways to Learn a Campaign

This workshop teaches the campaign method two ways at once: by **doing** and by **studying**.

- **Doing:** you design a marketing campaign for a real product brief, in the room, applying the campaign-plan structure from the previous lesson.
- **Studying:** you study real successful and failed campaigns and extract *lessons learned* — reusable, transferable takeaways.

Both matter. Building a plan shows you can *apply* the method; studying real campaigns (with their wins and losses) shows you can *reason from evidence*. Success and failure are equally instructive — a post-mortem of a failed campaign often teaches more than a winning story, because it shows you what was missing.

## The Workshop: Design a Campaign

The workshop uses the campaign-plan structure you already know, applied to a brief. The brief is a product scenario plus explicit constraints (budget, target, region). Your one-page plan covers:

1. **Goal** (SMART) — what measurable outcome?
2. **Audience** — who exactly?
3. **Message** — the single value claim.
4. **Channels** — guerrilla / owned-email / influencer (from recent lessons).
5. **Timing** — pre/launch/post calendar.
6. **Budget** — spread across channels.
7. **KPIs** — the numbers.
8. **Review** — when and how you'll check and adjust.

**The budget is deliberately small** — often the point of the exercise. Making you solve the problem with targeting, message, and channel logic (rather than "spending your way out") is what forces you to actually think about audience and priorities. It also mirrors the real small-budget reality of a school or local app.

## A Better Brief: Make It Local

Using a **local scenario** — a school, community, or news app for one island — makes the work authentic and harder in a useful way. Your decisions have to reflect the regional reality (data cost, WhatsApp dominance, smaller reach) rather than copying a generic global launch. When the brief is local, a "global template" answer visibly fails — which is the lesson.

## Studying Cases: Success and Failure in Pairs

The most effective way to read campaigns is as **success/failure pairs**: a campaign that won on a tight audience and a clear goal, side by side with one that failed on fuzzy targeting and no measurement. The contrast isolates *exactly what made the difference*. To study them properly:

- **Read the evidence, don't assume.** A campaign that "grew after an influencer post" is a claim to interrogate — was it the influencer, the timing, the store listing, or the pre-launch buzz? (the correlation-vs-causation discipline from earlier lessons)
- **Name the specific lesson.** Don't stop at "it went well/bad." Extract a *reusable, actionable takeaway*: "target one clear persona" or "set measurement up first."

### Typical lesson patterns

**Success patterns** (2022–26 synthesis): pre-launch community building, strong store listing/ASO, targeted paid/influencer, and data-driven iteration — measurable in installs, retention, and cost-per-acquisition.

**Failure patterns:** unfocused audience ("marketing to everyone"), no measurement set up, vague goals with no numbers, message mismatch between channels, and over-reliance on one channel or persona.

These are the inverse of the planning discipline — which is exactly why reading them teaches you the discipline.

## The One Example That Keeps Coming Up

The **ALS Ice Bucket Challenge** remains a teaching-favourite success: a dead-simple mechanic, essentially zero media spend, ~$115M raised in 8 weeks. Its opposite — a launch that misses the audience or the timing — shows the cost of fuzzy targeting. Putting the two together is a ready-made success/failure pair for discussion.

## Worked Example: Reading a Pair

**Campaign A (success):** targets one clear persona (local students), a SMART goal (500 installs / 35% activation), diversifies budget across a campus stunt and one relevant micro-influencer, and sets up UTM tracking before launch. Result: hits its numbers, and can even say *why*.

**Campaign B (failure):** "market to everyone," no numeric goal, no tracking, money dumped on one channel. Result: clicks but no accountable installs — the team cannot explain what worked.

**Lesson learned:** "a tight audience and set-up tracking predict results; fuzzy targeting and no measurement guarantee a post-mortem you can't do."

## Common Misconceptions

### "A workshop plan just needs a media list."
The plan's value is the *logic* — goals, audience, message, channels, budget, KPIs, review — not merely listing where to post.

### "A successful campaign proves the tactic worked."
Correlation isn't causation. Success could come from timing, the store listing, or pre-launch buzz — not the glamorous tactic. Interrogate before crediting (read the pair).

### "Failed campaigns teach nothing."
Post-mortems of failures reveal the missing pieces (fuzzy audience, no measurement, message mismatch) — the most reusable lessons of all.

### "More budget is the fix for a weak idea."
Workshops cap budget on purpose so you must solve the problem with targeting, message, and channel logic. Spending more would hide a bad plan.

### "Lessons are just 'it went well/bad'."
A lesson is a *reusable, actionable takeaway* ("target one persona", "measure first"), not a verdict on a single campaign.

### "A global template works for a local app."
The regional brief forces decisions that fit Caribbean data/platform reality, so the plan is specific, not a copy-paste global launch.

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Campaign brief | The product scenario + constraints (budget, target, region) you design against |
| Post-mortem (campaign) | A structured review, after the campaign, of what worked/failed and why |
| Lesson learned | A reusable, actionable takeaway distilled from a result ("measure first") |
| Success/failure pair | Two contrasted cases used to isolate the specific factors that made the difference |
| Correlation vs causation | Not crediting a tactic merely because the outcome followed it |

## Summary

This workshop teaches campaigns by doing *and* by studying. In the doing half, you build a one-page plan from a brief — solving the problem with targeting, message, and channel logic under a deliberately small budget, ideally for a local scenario. In the studying half, you read success/failure pairs and extract reusable lessons, always watching for the correlation-vs-causation trap and the "note the specific lesson" habit. Success shows you the winning patterns; failure shows you the missing pieces. Together they turn the campaign-plan discipline from theory into a habit you can defend.
