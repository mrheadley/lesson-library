# App Creation Workshop: Building a Prototype That Works

## The Workshop's Product: A Prototype, Not a Finished Product

This lesson is a build-it lesson, and it is where the whole unit comes home. You assemble a working **mobile app prototype** using no-code or low-code tools, applying everything from the mobile-UX arc — conceptualisation, wireframing, design, testing and store thinking — to one concrete idea. Be precise about what you are producing: a **prototype** is a working, simplified version of an app built to test its core idea, not the finished product. One core idea, a couple of screens and a single genuinely working feature is enough — as long as it is **finished**. That is the whole bar.

This workshop is also where **Marked Assignment 8** is assigned: the prototype you start here is the assignment, due at Lesson 142. The workshop is not preparation for the assignment — it *is* the assignment's opening, and its output extends out of class through the milestone checklist.

## The 35-Minute Kickoff Protocol

A build period stays productive only with a **protocol**. Follow it in order; it is sized to a single 35-minute period:

**Step 1 — Pick a "fix something local" idea (fast).** Choose a real pain in the school or community — a canteen menu, a bus-stop tracker, emergency numbers, a hurricane-shelter map. Name it in one sentence. Draw from the local-apps and regional-needs lessons; solving a local problem on the region's cheap Android phones is a documented rubric strength.

**Step 2 — Agree the idea and kill the scope.** One core idea, not three. If the app needs more than a couple of screens to make sense, cut it now.

**Step 3 — Wireframe the screens.** Three screens, on paper, from the wireframing lesson: home, the feature, and one more screen that makes the flow complete.

**Step 4 — Build the first working screen.** In the no-code builder, assemble one genuine, functioning screen before the bell — blocks that do something, not static pictures.

**Step 5 — Write the milestone checklist.** Small, dated deliverables that keep out-of-class work on schedule: idea, wireframes, first screen, working feature, tested build. The 35-minute period gets you to "first screen"; everything after runs against the checklist.

## The Tool: MIT App Inventor and Friends

**MIT App Inventor** is the classroom-free baseline: **free forever**, open-source, **block-based programming** (Snap-together logic in the Scratch style), and browser-based using the school's existing Google accounts — no code, no fees, no Mac, no Xcode. It is Android-first (iOS support is limited and beta), but its **companion app** live-previews a project on a real phone as you build, which is ideal for schools where students own Android devices — the emulator-free echo of the native tool-chain's instant-run magic. Its scale is real, not toy-like: the platform reports **25.1 million all-time users, 121.2 million apps built and roughly 937,000 active users in a recent month**, has added AI and image extensions, and runs a yearly Global Appathon that drew **2,176 participants from 141 countries** in 2026.

Two cousins round out the toolkit. **Thunkable** uses the same block model for web + iOS + Android with live browser preview and AI-assisted generation — but its store-publishing tier sits behind roughly **US$189/month** per 2026 sources, so flag that cost before any student plans on releasing through it. **Kodular** is a second free, Android-only, block-based builder that publishes to Google Play — an escape hatch if App Inventor's limits bite.

Each row is dated and checkable, which is the point — scope discipline under a deadline. A finished row ("works and is usable") counts under the rubric; a beautiful unfinished row does not.

## Scoring Is Rubric-Not-Aesthetics

Fair scoring needs a **rubric** announced up front, and it is not aesthetics. The class app is marked on four honest axes — **does it work, is it usable, is it relevant to the community, and does it demonstrate the unit's concepts** (the bandwidth, portability, design and testing themes) — not how good it looks:

| Axis | What the marker checks |
|---|---|
| Works | It installs, runs, and the one core feature actually functions |
| Usable | A stranger can complete the core loop without coaching |
| Community relevance | It solves a named local problem, not a generic one |
| Unit concepts | It shows the design/testing/store thinking of the unit |

The fastest way to internalise this is to mark two sample prototypes side by side: one shiny-but-broken, one plain-but-working. Students immediately see that the marks value "works and is usable" over "looks impressive." Criteria are what make feedback fair, and they are exactly what make scope discipline (one finished feature beating five half-finished ones) mark-winning.

## Why the Building Lessons Still Apply

Block tools do not exempt you from design: they still produce silent, bad UX unless the design lens is on. Components in a builder mirror the Compose and SwiftUI ideas of the native-tools lesson; a single Widget/UI check runs the testing pyramid at prototype scale (does it install, does the one feature work, does it crash?); and the store listing and privacy work preview the submission lesson's fees and forms. After the build, a "what we didn't build" map — each builder component set against the native, cross-platform or store concept it replaces — turns the no-code path into an honest trade-off statement rather than a shortcut. Publishing economics favour Google for the assignment: a one-time **US$25** Play account, with App Inventor projects exporting as installable **APK files** for testing outside any store, while iOS multiplies cost and complexity (Apple's US$99/year plus a Mac and Xcode).

## Common Misconceptions

### "You must write code to build an app"
No-code and low-code builders produce working, installable apps — App Inventor alone counts 121 million-plus builds. Writing code is one route, not the only one.

### "No-code apps are toys that can't be published"
App Inventor exports installable APKs and Thunkable publishes to stores. The real limits are budget and store rules, not the tools.

### "A prototype must be full-featured"
The assignment's bar is a finished core idea. One working feature beats five half-finished ones under any honest rubric.

### "More screens means a better app"
Marks reward a complete, usable basic loop. Scope discipline is exactly what the 35-minute kickoff and the milestone checklist enforce.

### "Once the app is built, publishing is free"
Stores charge real fees and rules — US$25 Play one-time, US$99/year Apple, the Data Safety form, the 12-tester closed test — budgeted into the out-of-class milestones.

### "Builders do everything, so the design lessons don't matter"
Block tools still produce bad UX silently. The design and testing lens is what separates a mark-winning prototype from a template dump.

## Summary

This workshop makes the unit concrete: over one 35-minute period you choose a local problem, agree a scoped idea, wireframe three screens and build the first working screen in a no-code builder — then carry the build forward out of class against a dated milestone checklist. The tool is **MIT App Inventor** (free, open-source, block-based, running on the school's Google accounts, with a companion app for live preview on real phones), with Thunkable and Kodular as known alternatives and their store-publishing costs flagged. **Marked Assignment 8** is assigned here and due at Lesson 142, so the workshop is the assignment's opening, not its rehearsal. Scoring is rubric-not-aesthetics — function, usability, community relevance and demonstrated unit concepts — and scope discipline is rewarded: a finished prototype with one genuinely working feature beats a beautiful half-app every time.
