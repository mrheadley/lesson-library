# How to Judge a Trend: Adoption Curves, the Chasm and the Hype Cycle

## Judging Trends Is a Skill

By Lessons 136–138 you have met a rush of trend claims — foldables, super-apps, on-device AI, 5G, Web3 — each announced as the future. Judging them is a *skill*, not an opinion: instead of trusting press releases or launch hype, use structured models and then check the evidence. The toolkit is classic and still the best available: **Rogers' adoption curve** explains how and how fast a technology spreads; **Geoffrey Moore's chasm** explains why most products stall partway; the **Gartner Hype Cycle** maps a technology's emotional/publicity arc; and **critical mass** is the tipping point where adoption stops needing help. Use all of them, then interrogate the actual data. This article turns that toolkit into a reusable "how to judge a trend" frame.

## Rogers' Adoption Curve: Who Adopts When

Everett Rogers' *Diffusion of Innovations* (1962) describes how a new technology spreads through five adopter groups, arranged as an **S-curve** of growing then slowing adoption — the early part of the curve is the slow, risky beginning, the middle is the explosive take-off, and the tail is the lingering stragglers.

| Adopter group | Share of population | Who they are |
|---|---|---|
| Innovators | ~2.5% | Risk-tolerant novelty-seekers; "the friend who buys everything first" |
| Early adopters | ~13.5% | High opinion-leadership; adopt on vision and reference; the group most people are persuaded *by* |
| Early majority | ~34% | Adopt once proven; practicality and references over vision |
| Late majority | ~34% | Skeptical; adopt once "everyone else is doing it" |
| Laggards | ~16% | Traditional, risk-averse; adopt only when forced (obsolescence, regulation) |

Adoption speed is governed by **five attributes** of the innovation itself: **relative advantage** (better than what it replaces), **compatibility** (fits existing habits), **complexity** (easy to understand/use), **trialability** (can be tried cheaply), and **observability** (the results are visible to others). The more favourable the five, the faster the S-curve climbs.

## Critical Mass and the Chasm

**Critical mass** is the point — often around **10–25% adoption** — where interpersonal networks take over and adoption becomes self-sustaining: enough people are using it that using it becomes the reasonable thing to do. But between the early adopters and the early majority sits Moore's **chasm** (from *Crossing the Chasm*, 1991): the discontinuous gap where most tech products fail. Its cause is psychological, not technical — **early adopters buy vision; the early majority buys proven references**. The people who ride the S-curve's climb are not the same people who believe the pitch; they buy reliability, references and low risk, and products that cannot produce those stall forever on the wrong side of the gap.

## The Gartner Hype Cycle: Maturity vs Publicity

The **Gartner Hype Cycle** (introduced in 1995 by Jackie Fenn) tracks a technology on two different axes at once — maturity and publicity — through five phases: **innovation trigger** (birth), **peak of inflated expectations** (media noise at its highest), **trough of disillusionment** (inflated expectations collapse), **slope of enlightenment** (genuinely useful tech recovers and spreads) and **plateau of productivity** (reliable, mainstream). Gartner's own purpose is practical: firms use it to decide *when* to invest — some technologies are cheaper to buy in the trough than at the peak.

The model has a crucial validity caveat, and honest analysis flags it (the checklist below will too). Studies since 2000 find that **only about a fifth of adopted technologies actually follow the full rise-fall-recovery arc**, and roughly **60% of technologies entering the trough never recover**. The Hype Cycle is a lens for questioning hype — not a law of technology.

## Worked Example: Putting a Trend on the Curve and the Cycle

Pick any trend from Lessons 136–138 and place it twice — once on Rogers' curve, once on the Hype Cycle — then justify each placement with a figure.

- **Foldables:** on Rogers' curve, early adopters to early-majority boundary — ~21–30% shipment growth in 2026 sounds huge, but under ~2% of phones sold means the *population* adopting is still tiny (pre-chasm). On the Hype Cycle, they have passed a peak (they are not at inflated-expectation frenzy) but are not yet mainstream — call it the slope of enlightenment, with Apple's entry pending.
- **On-device AI:** early-growth on Rogers' curve (still enthusiasts' hardware — flagship NPUs) but climbing the slope of enlightenment on the cycle, as genuinely useful, shipping features accumulate.
- **Web3 on mobile:** largely *past* a hype-cycle peak and trough into a utility phase — the exhausted hype does not mean the tech is dead, only that survival now depends on real usage (the argument developed in Lesson 140).
- **5G, two placements at once:** globally, 5G is past critical mass — 3 billion-plus subscribers, probably around the early-majority boundary on the curve. In the Caribbean it sits at ~10% of connections — early adopters at best, still pre-chasm. **The same trend is at two curve points simultaneously.** This is the single most important habit this lesson builds: always ask *which market?* before you answer *where is it on the curve?*

**Analogy for the chasm:** imagine convincing your school to switch to a new payment app. The first 2–3 enthusiastic friends join because it is new and cool (innovators + early adopters). Then everyone else asks "does anyone my parents trust use it? is it reliable?" — that is the chasm, and it is crossed with references and reliability, not novelty.

## The "How to Judge a Trend" Frame

Turn the toolkit into a questioning habit you can reuse. For any claimed trend, run these five checks:

1. **Where is it on Rogers' curve — in which market?** Innovators, early adopters, or majority? Re-answer per market (a global early-majority trend can be a regional early-adopter one).
2. **Where is it on the Hype Cycle, and is it peaking on substance or noise?** The peak of inflated expectations is where media noise is highest and reality least tested — hype ≠ adoption.
3. **What is the actual data — and whose?** Shipments, active users, revenue, sustained behaviour — from a *named* source, not a keynote. If two analysts disagree (e.g. foldable growth projected at ~21% by one firm and 20–22%+ or more by another), **say so**. A real trend does not have one undisputed number; flag the conflict rather than silently picking one.
4. **Is adoption self-sustaining, or propped up?** Growth can come from value, hype, or incentives (airdrops, promos). Look for sustained behaviour, not headline growth.
5. **Who benefits — and what driver is real?** Apply the four drivers from Lesson 137: hardware, network, behaviour, policy — and note which ones are genuinely present in the user's market.

Run a trend through that frame and you have an *evidence-based judgement*, not a hot take — the exact reasoning standard CAPE students need when a headline announces that "AR is booming" or "on-device AI is everywhere."

## Common Misconceptions

### "The Hype Cycle is a law that all technology follows"
The evidence says only ~1 in 5 technologies travels the full rise-fall-recovery arc, and ~60% of those entering the trough never return — treat it as a lens, not a rule.

### "If a trend is on TV, it's real"
The peak of inflated expectations is exactly where media noise is highest and reality least tested — hype ≠ adoption.

### "'Everyone has/does X' means the trend is done"
Different markets sit at different curve points; the regional user base (5G ~10%, older devices, data-cost constraints) may sit far behind the global early majority.

### "All adopters are alike; adoption is smooth"
The chasm shows the early majority behaves very differently from early adopters — needing references and reliability, not vision — which is where products stall.

### "A real trend has one undisputed number"
Analysts routinely disagree (foldable growth 21% vs 30%+; differing shipment forecasts) — flag conflicts instead of quietly picking a number.

### "A growing market share equals real user value"
Growth can be driven by value, hype, or incentives/airdrops — evidence-based analysis looks at sustained behaviour, not just headline growth.

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Adoption curve / diffusion of innovations | Rogers' model of the S-shaped spread of a technology through defined adopter groups |
| Innovator | The first ~2.5% to adopt — risk-tolerant, novelty-seeking |
| Early adopter | The ~13.5% with high opinion-leadership who adopt early on vision/reference |
| Early / late majority | The middle ~34% + ~34% — the late majority is skeptical and adopts once "everyone else is doing it" |
| Laggard | The final ~16% — traditional, risk-averse; adopts only when forced |
| Critical mass | The point (~10–25% adoption) where adoption becomes self-sustaining |
| Chasm | Moore's risky gap between early adopters and the early majority, where many tech products fail |
| Gartner Hype Cycle | A five-phase model of a technology's publicity-driven arc, from trigger to productivity plateau (innovation trigger, peak of inflated expectations, trough of disillusionment, slope of enlightenment, plateau of productivity) |
| Trough of disillusionment | The phase where inflated expectations collapse; only genuinely useful tech recovers to the slope of enlightenment |
| Evidence-based analysis | Judging a trend by verifiable data (users, shipments, revenue, real behaviour) and named sources, flagging conflicts rather than silently choosing one |

## Summary

Judging a trend is a skill with a toolkit. Rogers' adoption curve places a technology among five adopter groups on an S-curve, accelerated by five attributes; critical mass (~10–25%) is where adoption becomes self-sustaining; Moore's chasm separates vision-buying early adopters from reference-buying early majority, and most products die there. The Gartner Hype Cycle maps the publicity arc — and is only a ~1-in-5 reliable predictor, so it is a lens, not a law. The same technology can sit at two curve points at once (global 5G past critical mass; Caribbean 5G at ~10%, pre-chasm). The reusable frame — five checks covering curve position, cycle phase, real data, self-sustaining behaviour and genuine drivers — lets any student turn a press release into an evidence-based judgement.
