# Summative Assessment 4: Mobile Ecosystem — Review Article

## What This Summative Covers

Summative 4 brings together everything from **Section 6 — Mobile Ecosystem**. It is sat in a single 35-minute period (up to 30 minutes of assessment), so your preparation happens here, independently. This review is your study guide — it does **not** re-teach the lessons; it connects them, flags the places students most often stumble, and shows you the one journey that runs through every topic.

The assessment blends two kinds of knowledge:

- **How the mobile world works** — the ecosystem and its players, operating systems, the global-vs-Caribbean infrastructure divide, local apps, and mobile UX.
- **How an app is built and judged** — app creation tools and prototyping, then trends and how to reason about them.

The single idea that ties it all together: **an app's success is a chain — real local need → UX that fits the device/bandwidth → built with the right tool set → distributed and marketed for the real platform mix → riding a trend that is genuinely established in-region** (Lesson 144). Hold on to that sentence — everything below hangs off it.

## Key Concepts Review

### The Ecosystem and Operating Systems (Lessons 20, 32–37, 91–99)

A mobile **ecosystem** is the interrelated set of players that make mobile use possible and valuable: **content providers + infrastructure + devices + users + carriers + policy/regulation**. No single player succeeds alone — a device is useless without content, connectivity, and users.

The OS comparison is one of the section's central distinctions:

- **Android** — open platform: Google Play Store, device fragmentation (many manufacturers, sizes, versions), wider distribution paths (APK, sideloading), more flexibility and responsibility.
- **iOS** — closed platform: Apple App Store, strict review and guidelines, tight integration, more control and consistency.

This is the **"walled garden" vs "open garden"** idea (Lesson 39): iOS trades openness for control and consistency; Android trades control for openness and fragmentation. Smart devices/IoT extend the "device" layer, and policy/regulation (spectrum, net neutrality, data protection) shapes everything underneath. **Know which behaviours follow from "open" vs "closed"** — that reasoning is tested directly.

### The Global vs Caribbean Infrastructure Divide (Lessons 100–108)

Global players benefit from high 4G/5G penetration and mature supply chains. The Caribbean has **high smartphone adoption and high social penetration (WhatsApp above 85%)** but faces **constrained data plans, bandwidth, and device floors**. Regional trade and CARICOM digital initiatives open cross-border opportunity.

The recurring test point: **design must be built down to the region's real reality, not up to a global ideal.** A high-bandwidth, high-end approach that ignores local constraints fails at home.

### Local Needs and Mobile UX (Lessons 109–126)

Local apps serve culture, news, services, and community; regional needs (disaster prep, health, education, agriculture) and universal needs both matter. The mobile UX constraints that shape best practice are **screen size, bandwidth, portability, processing power, and accessibility** — design down to these.

**Your practical takeaway:** constraint-led design treats limits as design inputs, not annoyances. A local app that is "globally styled" but ignores local connectivity/device limits is not better — it is broken locally.

### App Creation Tools & Prototyping (Lessons 127–135)

The build path moves from **conceptualisation and user stories → wireframes → SDKs → native vs cross-platform (React Native/Flutter) → no-code/low-code builders → testing → store submission.** The key distinction to know cold: **native** (platform-specific, tuned, costly to maintain twice) vs **cross-platform** (one codebase, faster, sometimes a performance/UX trade-off) vs **no-code/low-code** (fast, limited for complex features). The right choice depends on the product's needs, budget, and team — there is no universal winner.

### Trends and How to Reason About Them (Lessons 136–152)

Current trends (foldables, super-apps, mobile payments, health tech, AI, 5G, AR, edge) are driven by **hardware, network rollout, consumer behaviour, and regulation**. The section's signature skill is the analysis discipline: **separate established / emerging / speculative; separate hype from substance** (the adoption curve and hype cycle of Lesson 139, and the "so-what" test — name the concrete decision a trend forces).

**Your practical takeaway:** apply the so-what test for any trend you read about — if you cannot name the specific feature or decision it changes in an app, it is not yet relevant. And remember trends are **regional, not universal**: a trend "established" globally may still be emerging or irrelevant in the Caribbean.

## The Likely Trouble Spots (where students most often trip)

1. **Open vs closed / Android vs iOS.** Don't treat it as "which is nicer." Connect each behaviour (review, sideloading, fragmentation, control) to the *open/closed philosophy*. Know which consequences follow from which philosophy.
2. **Getting the local-vs-global story backwards.** A global audience is not automatically "better." Mobile-first / local-first is constraint-led design, and "works in my region" is a strength, not a limitation.
3. **Treating a trend as universal or as fact.** A trend is not automatically right for the Caribbean, and a marketed "future" is not automatically coming. Argue with the established/emerging/speculative lens and the so-what test.
4. **Picking a build tool by hype, not need.** Native is not automatically better than cross-platform or no-code; choose by the product's needs, budget, and team — and remember the tools serve the product, not the other way around.
5. **Recalling sections in isolation.** Section 6 is one journey, not a stack. The chain (need → UX → build → distribution → trend) is what you should be able to walk and explain.
6. **Forgetting the numbers.** Anchor regional arguments in real data (WhatsApp ~85%+, ~24.8M social identities, high mobile traffic): the platform mix is what makes a distribution or marketing decision a *reasoned* one.
7. **Confusing distribution, marketing, and monetisation.** Distribution is *where the app lives*; marketing is how you drive awareness; monetisation is how you earn. Keep them distinct.

## Connections to Draw (what makes a well-integrated answer)

- **Ecosystem → local reality:** the ecosystem's infrastructure layer is exactly where Caribbean constraints live (Lesson 100–108), which drives the local-first UX (L118–126).
- **OS → build tools → distribution:** the open/closed philosophy (L39) shapes which tools fit (L127–135) and which distribution models work (L154 pull-forward).
- **UX constraints → prototyping:** the screen/bandwidth/accessibility constraints (L118–126) become the requirements your wireframes and build (L127–135) have to satisfy.
- **Trends → everything below:** a trend (L136–152) is "established" or "speculative" partly because of the hardware/network/regulatory reality you learned in the ecosystem and infrastructure lessons — so trends ripple back down the whole chain.

## Summary

Summative 4 assesses the whole of Section 6 as **one mobile journey: ecosystem → local reality → UX → build → trends.** Master the open-vs-closed OS philosophy and its consequences, the ecosystem players, the Caribbean infrastructure divide, constraint-led mobile UX, the build tool families, and the established/emerging/speculative trend discipline — then connect them into the chain. The persistent traps are treating open/closed as taste, judging global as automatically better, taking trends as universal or as fact, choosing tools by hype, and recalling the section as isolated blocks. Prepare by walking the chain and practising the distinctions above — structure and reasoning beat scattered recall every time.
