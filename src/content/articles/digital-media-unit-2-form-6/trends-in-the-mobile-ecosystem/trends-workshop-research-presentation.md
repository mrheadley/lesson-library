# Trends Workshop: Research & Presentation

## From Research to a Case You Can Defend

The research skills from the last lesson are only useful if you can *communicate* what you found. This workshop is where your trend case study gets presented and put under peer review. The goal is not a pretty slide deck — it is an **evidence-based case**: a claim about a trend, backed with named sources, classified into the established/emerging/speculative buckets, and concluded with what it means for app decisions.

Think of it as a **mini academic conference**: you are not reporting a story; you are defending a position with evidence and accepting (gracefully) thoughtful questions.

## The Claim–Evidence–Implication Structure

The backbone of a clear presentation is three parts:

1. **Claim** — the single point you are making about the trend.
2. **Evidence** — the named sources and facts that support it.
3. **Implication** — what this means for a concrete app decision ("so what").

A presentation built this way beats a long, unstructured slide dump every time, because it forces you to choose what is actually important. If you cannot state your implication as a concrete decision, you have not finished thinking.

## What a Presentation-Ready Case Contains

Your trend case should fit on one page:

- **Trend name** and a one-line definition
- **The claim** you are making about it
- **Three named sources**
- **Evidence bucket** — established / emerging / speculative (Lesson 139)
- **Impact** — on design, development, and/or distribution
- **One company example**
- **One counterpoint or limitation** — a reasonable doubt you can address

The **counterpoint is the strongest mark of maturity**. Naming a limitation and responding to it shows you understand the nuance, rather than hiding the uncertainty.

## A Practical Presentation Rhythm (5–6 minutes)

For the age group and a single-period budget, a tight rhythm works well:

- **1 min** — claim & context
- **2–3 min** — evidence & the case
- **1 min** — implications for an app
- **1 min** — counterpoint & peer questions

> **Tip:** If you can compress your whole case into a **two-minute pitch** using claim–evidence–implication, the longer version is easy. The two-minute version forces you to find the one thing that matters most.

## Peer Evaluation: Criteria, Not Popularity

Peer feedback works when it is **based on agreed criteria**, not a popularity vote. A simple rubric for this workshop covers:

- **Accuracy** — are the facts right and the sources named?
- **Evidence quality** — are the sources credible and sufficient?
- **Clarity** — is the claim–evidence–implication structure clear?
- **Handling of counterpoints** — did the group address reasonable doubts?

Before presenting, the class co-builds this rubric and scores a sample (possibly AI-drafted) case together, so everyone agrees on what "good evidence" looks like *first*. Then each presenting group gets balanced feedback: **one strength, one improvement, one question** — a structured, low-stakes format that supports diverse learners and keeps a talkative class productively engaged.

## Communication Pitfalls to Avoid

- **Reading your slides verbatim** — the deck is supporting material, not your script.
- **Unsourced claims** — every strong statement needs a named source.
- **Ignoring counter-evidence** — acknowledging it makes the case stronger, not weaker.
- **Overclaiming** — treating a probable future as a certainty; stay honest to your evidence bucket.
- **Unreadable visuals on a small screen** — keep text minimal and large, which also helps processing-challenged peers.

## Being Honest About AI in Your Research

In this course you are expected to be transparent about how you worked. If you used AI in your research, **state what you researched versus what you generated**, and cite your sources. This is not a weakness — it is exactly the "accountability as representation" the course teaches: you take ownership of your evidence and your claims rather than passing off unverified output as your own work.

## Worked Example: A Two-Minute Case

**Trend:** On-device AI on phones.

- **Claim:** On-device AI is an *established* trend that lets a simple app work offline — not a speculative gimmick.
- **Evidence:** named iOS/Android releases shipping on-device models; a vendor data report; one local app that advertises "works offline."
- **Implication:** our school app can offer offline suggestions and list "offline" as a selling point and a conversion factor in our store listing.

Now add one counterpoint: on-device models are limited by older hardware (a real regional concern), so we would start with basic offline features and keep heavy work in the cloud. That is a defensible, evidence-based, honest case — ready to present.

## Common Misconceptions

### "More slides mean more marks."
Criteria reward clarity and evidence, not volume. A single strong claim with named sources beats a long, unsourced deck.

### "Peer feedback is just opinions I can ignore."
When criteria-based, peer critique surfaces real gaps — missing evidence, unsourced claims, overclaiming — that improve the work before it is graded.

### "A confident tone can substitute for evidence."
Confidence without sourcing is exactly the overclaiming the course warns against. The evidence-bucket discipline keeps claims honest.

### "Naming a counterpoint makes me look weak."
It shows maturity and makes the case stronger. Addressing a reasonable doubt is a mark of good research, not something to hide.

### "The trend with the best slides wins."
The rubric rewards correct classification and evidence, not polish. Substance wins over decoration.

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Peer evaluation | Structured assessment by fellow students against agreed criteria, not a grade |
| Rubric | A scoring guide (criteria + descriptive levels) making evaluation consistent and transparent |
| Claim–evidence–implication | Argue the point, back it with evidence, say what it means for a decision |
| Overclaiming | Presenting a probable future as a certainty — the opposite of the evidence-bucket discipline |
| One strength / one improvement / one question | A balanced, low-stakes feedback format that keeps critique specific and useful |

## Summary

A presentation is only as strong as the case it defends. Structure every presentation as claim–evidence–implication, keep it to a tight rhythm, and include a named counterpoint. Peer evaluation works when it is criteria-based — build the rubric first, score a sample together, then give balanced feedback. Avoid the pitfalls of reading slides, unsourced claims, and overclaiming, and be transparent about AI-assisted research under the course's "accountability as representation." The goal is not a pretty deck; it is an evidence-based case you can defend.
