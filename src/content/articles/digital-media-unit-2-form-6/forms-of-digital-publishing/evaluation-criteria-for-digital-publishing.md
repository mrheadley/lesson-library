# Evaluation Criteria for Digital Publishing

## Choosing the Right Platform Is a Strategic Decision

Every publishing form has distinct strengths suited to different content types, audiences, and goals. A website offers full control but demands technical maintenance. A podcast creates intimate connections but requires audio production. YouTube delivers massive reach but makes you dependent on an algorithm you don't control.

The platform you choose shapes your content, your audience relationship, and your long-term flexibility. This is not a decision to make casually — it's a strategic evaluation that should consider content type, audience behaviour, budget, technical skills, and long-term goals.

## The Six Publishing Forms

Before evaluating platforms, understand what each form offers:

**Websites** — Full control, rich SEO capabilities, but require technical maintenance. Your digital home base.

**Blogs** — Optimised for frequent updates, community building through comments, and conversational tone. Lower barrier than a full website.

**E-books** — Portable long-form content. Readers can take them anywhere, read offline, and customise display settings. Best for structured, complete works.

**Podcasts** — Intimate, personality-driven audio. Lower bandwidth requirements than video. Strong listener loyalty.

**Video platforms** — High visual engagement but demand significant bandwidth and production resources. Algorithm-dependent discovery.

**Live streaming** — Real-time interaction with audiences. Requires stable internet connectivity and planned content.

## Suitability Criteria

When evaluating platforms, assess these dimensions:

### Content Type
What are you creating? Text-heavy analysis suits blogs and e-books. Visual demonstrations suit video. Personality-driven commentary suits podcasts. Interactive projects suit websites.

### Audience Behaviour
Who will consume your content, and how? A Caribbean student commuting by bus accesses content on a mobile phone over cellular data — a 50MB video download is impractical, but a 2MB podcast episode or a text blog post loads quickly. Consider when, where, and how your audience engages.

### Budget
What resources are available? Free platforms (Blogger, WordPress.com free tier, Medium, Spotify for Podcasters) carry higher lock-in risk because you have less control. Paid options (WordPress.org with hosting, Squarespace) cost money but offer greater flexibility and ownership.

### Technical Skills
What does the team know? A student comfortable with HTML can hand-code a WordPress theme. A student with no web development experience needs a drag-and-drop builder like Wix or Squarespace. Match the platform to the skills, not the aspirations.

### Time Investment
When must the project be live? Blogger can be live in minutes. WordPress.org requires hours for initial setup. Squarespace falls in between. A school deadline in two weeks points toward faster options.

### Monetisation Goals
Are you planning to earn revenue? WordPress.org offers unlimited monetisation — advertising, affiliate links, e-commerce, memberships. Medium's Partner Program pays based on reading time but restricts your control. Free platforms typically limit monetisation options.

## Platform Lock-In and Data Gravity

**Platform lock-in** occurs when switching costs — financial, technical, and reputational — make it impractical to leave a platform. **Data gravity** means that once content accumulates on a platform, migration becomes exponentially more expensive.

### The Vine Example
When Vine shut down, creators who had built their entire audience there lost everything — followers, content, and distribution. Creators who also maintained a website and email list survived the transition. This is the core lesson: never depend entirely on a platform you don't own.

### Egress Fees and Data Portability
Some platforms charge egress fees when you export your data. AWS S3 charges approximately $0.09 per GB for data transfer out — a significant markup over actual bandwidth costs. Before committing to any platform, verify that content export is possible and understand the cost.

## The Multi-Platform Strategy

The recommended approach combines an **owned platform** (your website) as the core hub with **secondary channels** (social media, aggregators, directories) as discovery and distribution arms. This ensures you always own your audience relationship.

### Worked Example: Content Distribution Strategy

1. Write a blog post on your own WordPress site (owned platform, full SEO control)
2. Share key quotes on Instagram and Twitter (discovery channels)
3. Record a podcast episode discussing the same topic (audio format, different audience)
4. Submit the article to a school newsletter (local distribution)
5. Cross-post a summary to Medium with a canonical tag pointing to your site (reach Medium's audience without losing SEO credit)

Each channel serves a different function. Your website is the hub; everything else is distribution.

## Risk Assessment Framework

Before choosing a platform, evaluate:

| Risk Factor | Questions to Ask |
|-------------|-----------------|
| Platform dependence | What happens if the platform shuts down? |
| Content ownership | Who legally owns what I create on this platform? |
| Security exposure | What data is at risk? Who handles security patches? |
| Maintenance burden | Who handles updates, backups, and downtime? |
| Scalability limits | Will the platform grow with my audience? |

### Caribbean-Specific Considerations

Bandwidth limitations in Caribbean environments make text-based platforms (blogs, websites) and audio (podcasts) more practical than video-heavy platforms. Mobile-first design is essential — most Caribbean internet users access content primarily via smartphones. A platform that requires desktop viewing is a poor fit for this audience.

### Worked Example: School Environmental Project Blog

Two students are choosing a platform for a blog documenting a school environmental project:

**Blogger** — free, live in minutes, Google-backed, but limited design options and you're on Google's platform.

**WordPress.com free tier** — more structured, better categories and pages, but WordPress branding and limited customisation.

**WordPress.org with school hosting** — full control, professional URL, unlimited customisation, but requires setup time and hosting cost ($5–10/mo).

For a 3-month school project with no budget, Blogger or WordPress.com free tier is appropriate. For a project intended to continue beyond the school year, investing in WordPress.org and a domain name ($12/yr) is the better long-term decision.

## Common Misconceptions

### "The platform with the most users is always the best choice"
The best platform is the one whose strengths align with your specific content type, audience behaviour, and goals — not necessarily the largest one. YouTube is massive, but a text-based educational blog doesn't need YouTube's infrastructure.

### "If a platform is free, there's no cost"
Free platforms extract value through platform lock-in, limited control, advertising on your content, and the risk of policy changes or shutdowns with no recourse. The cost is in your independence, not your wallet.

### "You should be on every platform to maximise reach"
Spreading across too many platforms dilutes effort and produces mediocre content everywhere. It's better to excel on 2–3 well-chosen platforms than to be mediocre on 10.

### "Once content is published, you can always move it somewhere else"
Many platforms make export difficult or impossible. Embeds, interactive features, comments, and analytics rarely transfer. Always maintain an owned copy of everything you publish.

### "Video is always the most engaging format"
Audio podcasts create deeper personal connections. Text allows deeper reflection. The best format depends on the content and the audience's context — a commuter prefers audio over video.

### "Platform choice doesn't affect content quality"
Platform constraints shape content. Twitter's character limit forces concision. YouTube rewards visual storytelling. Instagram rewards aesthetics. Choose the platform that naturally complements your content strengths.

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| Platform lock-in | High switching costs making it impractical to leave a platform |
| Data gravity | Content attracting more services over time, making migration progressively harder |
| Egress fees | Charges imposed when exporting data from a cloud platform |
| CPM | Revenue earned per thousand impressions or listens |
| Vendor lock-in | Dependence on a specific company's proprietary tools or formats |
| Multi-platform strategy | Core content on owned platforms, distributed through secondary channels |
| Owned platform | Website or blog you fully control — domain, hosting, content, audience data |
| Content audit | Systematic review of published content for performance and relevance |
| Audience funnel | Journey from casual discoverer to regular consumer to paying supporter |
| SEO | Optimising content to rank higher in search engine results |

## Summary

Evaluating digital publishing platforms requires a structured assessment of content type, audience behaviour, budget, technical skills, and long-term goals. Platform lock-in and data gravity make early decisions consequential — always prioritise content ownership and export capability. A multi-platform strategy using your website as a hub and secondary channels for distribution maximises reach while protecting your independence. Caribbean publishers should prioritise text and audio formats for bandwidth efficiency and mobile-first design. The best platform is not the most popular or the most expensive — it's the one that aligns with your specific needs and preserves your ability to adapt.
