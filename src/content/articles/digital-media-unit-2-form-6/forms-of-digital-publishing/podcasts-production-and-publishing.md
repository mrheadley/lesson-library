# Podcasts: Production and Publishing

## What Is a Podcast?

A podcast is a digital audio programme available for streaming or download, typically released in episodic series and distributed through RSS feeds to directories and apps. Think of it as a radio show that listeners can access whenever they want — on the bus, during lunch, or while studying.

The barrier to entry is remarkably low. A smartphone, a quiet room, and a free hosting account can launch a show that reaches a global audience. This makes podcasting one of the most accessible publishing forms, particularly in bandwidth-limited environments where video streaming is impractical.

## The Production Pipeline

Podcast production follows five clear stages:

1. **Record** — Capture raw audio using a microphone or smartphone
2. **Edit** — Cut mistakes, tighten pacing, remove dead air
3. **Master** — Normalise audio levels, apply EQ and compression for consistent quality
4. **Publish** — Upload with metadata, show notes, and artwork
5. **Promote** — Share across social channels and directories to build an audience

Each stage builds on the previous one. Poor recording cannot be fully rescued in editing. Editing without a plan wastes hours. Skipping master means uneven volume that irritates listeners.

## Recording: Equipment and Environment

Recording software ranges from free to professional:

- **Audacity** — free, open-source, runs on all platforms. The workhorse of independent podcasting.
- **GarageBand** — free on Mac and iOS. Clean interface with built-in effects.
- **OBS** — free, ideal for video podcasts or simultaneous screen capture.
- **Riverside.fm** ($15/mo) — records locally on each participant's device, producing separate tracks even for remote interviews. This avoids the quality loss of recording over Zoom.

### Environment Matters More Than Equipment

Audio quality is determined by recording environment more than microphone cost. A quiet room with soft furnishings — carpet, curtains, cushions — absorbs echoes and produces clean audio. Hard surfaces like tile and glass create reverb that makes recordings sound amateurish regardless of the microphone.

Compare: a $300 microphone in a tiled bathroom sounds worse than a phone microphone in a carpeted bedroom.

## Editing: Choosing Your Tool

- **Descript** — revolutionary text-based editing. It transcribes your audio using AI, and you edit by deleting text from the transcript. The corresponding audio is removed automatically. No audio engineering knowledge required.
- **Audacity** — manual waveform editing. More control, steeper learning curve.
- **Adobe Audition** ($23/mo) — professional digital audio workstation (DAW) with advanced features.
- **Hindenburg** — purpose-built for podcast editing with automatic loudness normalisation.

### Optimal Audio Specifications

For spoken-word podcasts, these settings balance quality with file size:
- **Format:** MP3 (most compatible across all players and directories)
- **Bitrate:** 128kbps mono or 192kbps stereo
- **Sample rate:** 44.1kHz (CD quality)

These specifications ensure smooth streaming even on slower connections — important for Caribbean listeners on mobile data.

## Mastering: Making It Sound Consistent

Mastering is the final stage of audio processing where levels, EQ, and compression are adjusted to ensure your podcast sounds polished across different playback devices. The key metric is **loudness**, measured in LUFS (Loudness Units Full Scale).

- **Stereo podcasts:** target -16 LUFS
- **Mono podcasts:** target -19 LUFS

Loudness normalisation ensures that switching between episodes — or between your podcast and another — doesn't require the listener to adjust their volume. Most editing software includes loudness meters or automatic normalisation features.

## Hosting Platforms

Hosting platforms serve as your podcast's home base. They store your audio files and automatically generate your **RSS feed** — an XML file containing episode metadata, audio file URLs, show descriptions, and artwork. Without a hosting platform and its RSS feed, your podcast cannot appear on any major directory.

Key hosting options:

- **Spotify for Podcasters** (formerly Anchor) — free, the easiest entry point. Limited analytics but zero cost.
- **Buzzsprout** ($12/mo) — strong analytics, transcription, and distribution support.
- **Libsyn** ($5/mo) — the oldest podcast host, operating since 2004. Reliable and no-frills.
- **Transistor** ($19/mo) — unlimited shows on one account, ideal if you plan multiple podcasts.

## RSS Feeds: The Backbone of Distribution

An RSS (Really Simple Syndication) feed is a machine-readable XML file that contains everything about your podcast and its episodes. When you submit your podcast to a directory, you're really submitting your RSS feed URL. The directory reads it, lists your episodes, and updates automatically when you publish new ones.

You don't build this file — your hosting platform generates it automatically. But you need to understand what it contains: title, description, artwork, episode list, audio URLs, and publication dates. This is why metadata matters — it's what directories display to potential listeners.

## Directories: Where Listeners Find You

Directories are platforms where listeners discover, subscribe to, and play podcasts. Each has its own submission process:

- **Apple Podcasts** — requires artwork at 3000x3000px, follows content guidelines, review process can take days to weeks.
- **Spotify** — submission through Spotify for Podcasters, generally faster approval.
- **Amazon Music** — growing directory with Amazon ecosystem integration.
- **Google Podcasts** — automatic indexing for RSS feeds (being transitioned to YouTube Music).

The typical workflow: create a hosting account, upload your first episode, wait for your RSS feed to generate, then submit the feed URL to each directory.

## Show Notes and Metadata

Every episode should include:
- **Title** — descriptive and searchable
- **Description** — 2-3 sentences summarising the episode with relevant keywords
- **Timestamps** — for longer episodes, mark key topics
- **Links** — references, guest socials, resources mentioned
- **Artwork** — consistent cover art (minimum 1400x1400px, recommended 3000x3000px)

Show notes improve SEO, help listeners decide whether to press play, and provide a reference for content discussed in the episode.

## Monetisation Pathways

While most student podcasts won't start with advertising revenue, understanding monetisation is part of the publishing landscape:

- **CPM advertising** — $2–7 per thousand listens (1,000 plays). Requires significant audience size.
- **Affiliate marketing** — recommending products with referral links.
- **Listener donations** — platforms like Patreon enable direct supporter relationships.
- **Premium content** — bonus episodes or early access for paying subscribers.
- **Sponsorships** — brands paying for mention or dedicated segments.

### Caribbean Context

Caribbean podcasting is growing rapidly. A student in Trinidad or Jamaica can start a show about local music, CSEC study tips, or community stories with nothing more than a smartphone and a free hosting account. The intimate audio format works well in bandwidth-limited environments — a podcast episode typically uses 1-2MB per minute, compared to 50-100MB for video of equivalent length.

## Consistency Builds Audience

Releasing on a predictable schedule — weekly, biweekly, or even monthly — builds audience trust and improves directory rankings. Most successful podcasts release weekly. A consistent 20-minute show outperforms an inconsistent 60-minute one. Start with a schedule you can sustain, then scale up.

## Common Misconceptions

### "You need expensive equipment to start a podcast"
Many successful podcasters record on smartphones or budget USB microphones. Content quality, consistency, and audio environment matter more than equipment cost. Start with what you have.

### "Podcasts must be long to be valuable"
Many popular podcasts are 15–30 minutes. Episode length should match content depth — a focused 20-minute episode outperforms a rambling 60-minute one.

### "Podcast hosting and directory distribution are the same thing"
Hosting platforms store your audio and generate RSS feeds. Directories (Apple Podcasts, Spotify) are where listeners find and subscribe to your show. You need both — they serve different functions.

### "Once you publish, listeners will find you"
Promotion is essential. Share on social media, ask listeners to subscribe and leave reviews, cross-promote with other podcasts, and appear on other shows. Content without promotion is invisible.

### "RSS feeds are outdated technology"
RSS is the universal standard that enables podcast distribution across every major platform. Without an RSS feed, your show cannot appear on Apple Podcasts or most directories. It remains the backbone of the entire podcast ecosystem.

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| Podcast | Digital audio programme distributed through episodic series via RSS feeds |
| RSS Feed | XML file containing podcast metadata and episode URLs, generated by hosting platforms |
| DAW | Digital Audio Workstation — software for recording, editing, and mixing audio |
| Bitrate | Data processed per second of audio, measured in kbps; 128kbps is standard for spoken word |
| Sample Rate | Audio samples captured per second; 44.1kHz is the podcast standard |
| Loudness Normalisation | Adjusting audio levels for consistent volume; target -16 LUFS stereo, -19 LUFS mono |
| Mastering | Final audio processing stage ensuring consistent quality across devices |
| CPM | Cost Per Mille — advertising revenue per thousand listens |
| Directories | Platforms where listeners discover and subscribe to podcasts |
| Show Notes | Written summaries accompanying each episode, improving SEO and listener experience |

## Summary

Podcast production follows a clear pipeline — record, edit, master, publish, promote — and the tools required range from free (Audacity, Spotify for Podcasters) to professional (Adobe Audition, Riverside.fm). RSS feeds generated by hosting platforms enable automatic distribution to every major directory. The low barrier to entry, minimal bandwidth requirements, and intimate audio format make podcasting one of the most accessible and personal publishing forms available to Caribbean students. Consistency in schedule and quality in content matter far more than equipment cost.
