# Video Capture, Lighting, Audio for Video, Video Editing, and Exporting

## Introduction

You've probably shot dozens of videos on your phone—school events, friends being silly, maybe a short clip for Instagram or TikTok. Most of those videos have one thing in common: they look and sound like every other smartphone video. Flat lighting. Muffled audio. Cuts that feel jarring.

This lesson changes that. We're going to break down what separates professional-looking video from amateur footage, and it's not the camera—it's the **craft**. You'll learn how camera settings shape your image, how three-point lighting transforms a dull scene into something cinematic, why audio quality matters more than video quality, how editing principles keep viewers engaged, and how to export your work so it looks great on any platform.

The good news: you don't need expensive gear. You need knowledge. The techniques in this lesson work with a smartphone, a clip-on microphone, and free software.

## Part 1: Video Capture—Understanding Your Camera

### Why Camera Settings Matter

When you shoot on your phone, it's making dozens of decisions for you—automatically adjusting brightness, focus, and color. That's convenient, but it means every video looks the same. Learning to control your camera manually lets you **make creative choices** instead of accepting defaults.

Think of it this way: an automatic car gets you where you're going, but a manual transmission lets you control *how* you get there. Manual camera settings give you the same control.

### The Exposure Triangle: ISO, Aperture, and Shutter Speed

These three settings work together to determine how bright or dark your image is. Change one, and you'll need to adjust at least one of the others to keep your exposure balanced.

#### ISO (Sensitivity)

**What it does:** Controls how sensitive the camera sensor is to light.

| ISO Value | Result |
|-----------|--------|
| 100–400 | Clean, sharp image with minimal grain. Use in bright conditions. |
| 800–1600 | Noticeable grain starting to appear. Useful in dim indoor settings. |
| 3200+ | Heavy grain/noise. Image quality degrades significantly. |

**Analogy:** ISO is like the volume knob on a radio. Turn it up (higher ISO) and you get more signal—but also more static. Keep ISO as low as your lighting allows.

**Smartphone connection:** Your phone's "Night Mode" cranks up ISO (and does other tricks) to capture images in low light. That's why night photos look softer and grainier than daytime shots.

#### Aperture (The Lens Opening)

**What it does:** Controls the size of the opening in the lens, measured in **f-stops** (f/1.8, f/2.8, f/5.6, etc.). A smaller f-number means a *wider* opening.

Two effects:
- **More light** — A wider aperture (f/1.8) lets in more light.
- **Shallower depth of field** — A wider aperture creates that blurred-background "portrait mode" effect. A narrow aperture (f/11) keeps everything in focus.

**Key rule:** The f-number and the opening size are **inversely related**. This confuses everyone at first. f/1.8 is a *big* opening. f/16 is a *tiny* opening.

**Smartphone connection:** "Portrait Mode" on your phone simulates a shallow depth of field (the background blur) using software. Understanding real aperture helps you know *why* that effect exists and when it works best.

#### Shutter Speed (Exposure Time)

**What it does:** Controls how long the sensor is exposed to light for each frame.

| Shutter Speed | Effect |
|---------------|--------|
| 1/30s or slower | Motion blur visible; too slow for handheld video |
| 1/50s or 1/60s | Standard for 24fps or 30fps video; natural-looking motion |
| 1/125s or faster | Crisp, frozen motion; used for action or high frame rates |

**The 180-degree rule (shutter):** For video, your shutter speed should be approximately **double your frame rate**. Shooting at 24fps? Use 1/50s. Shooting at 30fps? Use 1/60s. This produces natural-looking motion blur—the kind your eyes expect to see.

**Why this matters:** If your shutter speed is too slow (like 1/15s), footage looks mushy and blurry. If it's too fast (like 1/1000s), motion looks unnaturally crisp—like a strobe effect. The double-your-frame-rate rule hits the sweet spot.

### Frame Rate and Resolution

#### Frame Rate (FPS—Frames Per Second)

Frame rate is how many individual images your camera captures each second. Higher frame rates capture more motion detail but produce larger files.

| Frame Rate | Use Case | Feel |
|------------|----------|------|
| 24fps | Cinematic film, narrative content | Classic, "movie" feel |
| 30fps | Standard video, news, YouTube | Smooth, everyday look |
| 60fps | Action, sports, fast motion | Very smooth; can be slowed to 50% speed in editing |
| 120fps+ | Slow motion | Dramatic slow-mo; requires high shutter speed |

**Key insight:** More fps ≠ better quality. 24fps is used in professional cinema *on purpose* because it looks "cinematic." The right frame rate depends on what you're creating.

**Slow-motion trick:** Shoot at 60fps and edit on a 24fps timeline. The footage plays at ~40% speed—smooth, dramatic slow motion with no special equipment.

#### Resolution

Resolution is the number of pixels in each frame.

| Resolution | Pixel Dimensions | Common Use |
|------------|------------------|------------|
| 720p (HD) | 1280 × 720 | Minimum acceptable for web |
| 1080p (Full HD) | 1920 × 1080 | Standard for most content |
| 4K (Ultra HD) | 3840 × 2160 | High-quality production, future-proofing |
| 8K | 7680 × 4320 | Professional cinema, overkill for most projects |

**Common misconception: "Video quality = resolution."** This is one of the biggest myths in video production. A 4K video shot with bad lighting and poor audio looks *worse* than a well-lit, well-auditioned 1080p video. Resolution is just one factor in the quality equation—and often the least important one for web delivery.

### Recording Formats

When your camera saves a video file, two things are involved:

#### Containers vs. Codecs

- **Container** — The file wrapper (.mp4, .mov, .avi). Think of it as a box. Inside the box is the video data, audio data, and metadata.
- **Codec** — The algorithm used to compress the video data (H.264, H.265/HEVC, ProRes). Think of it as the method used to fit everything into the box.

**What you'll typically use:**
- **H.264** — The most compatible codec. Works everywhere. Good for final delivery and social media.
- **H.265/HEVC** — Same quality as H.264 but in smaller files. Increasingly supported but less universal.
- **ProRes** — High-quality codec used in professional editing. Large files, but edits smoothly. Common for archival and professional delivery.

#### Log Profiles (Flat Recording)

Some cameras and apps offer "log" recording modes (e.g., Apple Log, S-Log, C-Log). These record a flat, desaturated image that looks "washed out" on screen—but preserve the maximum range of color and brightness information.

**Analogy:** Recording in log is like shooting on black-and-white film and then painting the color in later. It sounds backwards, but it gives you *far more control* in post-production. You can recover shadow detail, adjust highlights, and apply creative color grades without degrading the image.

**When to use log:** When you plan to do serious color grading in editing. For quick social media content, standard recording profiles are fine.

## Part 2: Three-Point Lighting—The Foundation of Visual Storytelling

### Why Lighting Matters More Than Your Camera

This is the single most important concept in this lesson: **lighting is the biggest factor in video quality.** Not resolution. Not camera brand. Lighting.

A cheap camera in good light produces beautiful images. An expensive camera in bad light produces garbage. If you take nothing else from this lesson, take this: **spend your time on lighting.**

**Analogy:** Lighting is to video what seasoning is to cooking. The fanciest ingredients in the world taste bland without salt. The simplest ingredients taste extraordinary with the right seasoning.

### The Three-Point Lighting Setup

Three-point lighting is the standard lighting technique used in film, television, photography, and video production worldwide. It's been the foundation of visual storytelling for over a century, and it works because of how the human eye perceives depth and form.

**Analogy:** Three-point lighting is like lighting a stage play. You need a main light to see the actor clearly, a softer light to fill in the shadows so details aren't lost, and a light from behind to separate the actor from the backdrop so they don't blend into it.

#### The Three Lights

```
        [BACK LIGHT]
              \
               \
    [FILL] — [SUBJECT] — [CAMERA]
               /
              /
        [KEY LIGHT]
```

**1. Key Light (Main Light)**
- The primary and brightest light source
- Positioned to one side of the camera, typically 30–45 degrees from the subject
- Defines the subject's face and body, creates dimension through shadows
- Without a key light, everything looks flat and uninteresting

**2. Fill Light (Shadow Light)**
- Positioned on the opposite side of the camera from the key light
- Softer and less intense than the key light (typically 50–75% less bright)
- Fills in the harsh shadows created by the key light
- Without fill light, one side of the face is bright and the other is pitch black

**3. Back Light / Rim Light (Hair Light)**
- Positioned behind the subject, usually above and aimed at the shoulders and hair
- Creates a subtle outline of light around the subject's edges
- Separates the subject from the background, adding depth
- Without back light, the subject looks flat against the wall

#### Adjusting the Balance

The **contrast ratio** is the brightness difference between your key light and your fill light.

| Ratio | Effect | Mood |
|-------|--------|------|
| 1:1 | No shadow contrast | Flat, boring, "news broadcast" |
| 2:1 | Subtle shadows | Natural, interview-friendly |
| 4:1 | Moderate shadows | Dramatic, cinematic |
| 8:1+ | Deep shadows | Very dramatic, "film noir" |

**Key principle:** Contrast ratios higher than 8:1 are very difficult to correct in post-production. When in doubt, use more fill light than you think you need—you can always make shadows darker in editing, but you can't easily add detail to pitch-black areas.

### Lighting Types: Hard vs. Soft

#### Hard Light
- Comes from a small, direct source (bare bulb, direct sun, flashlight)
- Creates sharp, well-defined shadows
- Dramatic, edgy feel
- Reveals texture (warts, wrinkles, rough surfaces)

#### Soft Light
- Comes from a large, diffused source (window light, softbox, umbrella, overcast sky)
- Creates gradual, soft-edged shadows
- Flattering, gentle feel
- Smooths out skin texture

**Rule of thumb:** The larger the light source relative to the subject, the softer the light. A small flashlight held close produces hard light. A large window with indirect sunlight produces soft light.

**Practical example:** If you're filming an interview in a classroom, a window with indirect daylight is one of the best light sources available. Position the subject facing the window at a 45-degree angle (key light), add a white poster board on the opposite side to bounce light back (fill), and you've got a basic three-point setup with zero equipment cost.

### Color Temperature

Light isn't just "bright" or "dim"—it also has **color**, measured in **Kelvin (K)**.

| Color Temperature | Source | Feel |
|-------------------|--------|------|
| ~2700K | Candle light, warm tungsten bulbs | Very warm, orange/amber |
| ~3200K | Indoor tungsten lighting | Warm, yellowish |
| ~4000K | Mixed indoor lighting | Neutral |
| ~5600K | Midday sunlight | Neutral, white |
| ~6500K | Overcast sky | Cool, bluish |

**The problem:** If your key light is warm (3200K tungsten) and your fill light is cool (5600K daylight), your subject will look orange on one side and blue on the other. This looks terrible and is very hard to fix in editing.

**Rule:** All your lights should be the **same color temperature**. Match your artificial lights to your ambient light, or use gels (colored filters) to warm up or cool down a light.

**Smartphone connection:** Your phone auto-adjusts white balance, which is why indoor videos sometimes look too orange or too blue. When you shoot in manual, you can lock the white balance to match your scene.

### Common Lighting Mistakes

1. **Backlighting (unintentional)** — Filming someone with a bright window behind them turns them into a silhouette. Always position subjects facing toward the light source, not away from it.

2. **Overhead-only lighting** — Classroom ceiling lights create "raccoon eyes" (dark shadows under the eyes). Add a fill light at face level.

3. **Mixed color temperatures** — Fluorescent overhead lights + daylight from a window = green-and-blue color chaos. Choose one source or match temperatures.

4. **Too much ambient light** — Even a well-lit subject looks bad against a bright, cluttered background. Keep the background darker than the subject.

## Part 3: Audio for Video—The Silent Killer of Quality

### Why Audio Matters More Than You Think

Here's a fact that surprises most students: **viewers will tolerate bad video far longer than bad audio.** A slightly dark or grainy image is watchable. Distorted, muffled, or inaudible audio makes people click away in seconds.

Professional film crews spend enormous time and money on audio. A significant portion of any production budget goes to sound recording. This is not optional.

**Analogy:** Audio is like the bass line in a song. When it's good, you don't consciously notice it—but the whole thing feels wrong when it's missing or off. When it's bad, nothing else matters.

### Microphone Types

Each microphone type is designed for specific situations. Using the wrong mic is like using a hammer to turn a screw—it might work, but poorly.

#### Lavalier Microphones (Lav Mics)

- Small clip-on mics that attach to clothing (typically near the collar)
- **Best for:** Interviews, presentations, talking-head videos
- **Pros:** Hands-free, consistent audio as the subject moves, discreet
- **Cons:** Can rub against clothing, limited range
- **Cost:** Wired lavs from $15–30; wireless systems from $50–200+

**How to use:** Clip 6–8 inches below the subject's chin, on the opposite side from the camera. Route the cable under clothing to hide it.

#### Shotgun Microphones

- Highly directional mic that captures sound in a narrow cone in front of it
- **Best for:** Film-style recording, boom-mounted over subjects, capturing specific sound sources
- **Pros:** Rejects sound from sides and rear, excellent for focused audio
- **Cons:** Requires precise aiming, picks up handling noise, needs a boom pole or mount
- **Cost:** Entry-level from $50–150; professional from $300+

#### Handheld / Dynamic Microphones

- rugged, designed for close-range, direct input
- **Best for:** On-the-go reporting, music recording, noisy environments
- **Pros:** Very durable, excellent at rejecting background noise
- **Cons:** Requires the subject to hold it, not hands-free

#### Built-in Camera/Phone Microphones

- Integrated into the device
- **Reality:** Usually inadequate for professional-quality video
- **Problems:** Too far from the subject's mouth, captures handling noise, omnidirectional (picks up everything equally)
- **Exception:** Can work for ambient sound or nature recordings where the source is distant and atmospheric

**Bottom line:** Built-in mic audio is a backup, not a primary. If you take video seriously, invest at least $20 in a clip-on lav mic for your phone.

### Audio Recording Best Practices

#### Proximity: Get Close

The single most impactful thing you can do for audio quality is **get the microphone close to the sound source.** The closer the mic, the louder the voice relative to background noise.

| Mic Type | Ideal Distance |
|----------|---------------|
| Lavalier | 6–8 inches from mouth |
| Shotgun (on boom) | 1–3 feet from subject |
| Handheld | 2–4 inches from mouth |
| Phone held at arm's length | Not ideal—use a lav instead |

#### Monitoring: Listen While Recording

**Always wear headphones** while recording audio. This lets you catch problems in real time—wind noise, electrical hum, clipping—before they ruin a take. You cannot fix what you didn't hear during recording.

#### Wind Protection

- **Indoors:** Use a pop filter (a small screen in front of the mic) to reduce plosives ("P" and "B" sounds that create bursts of air)
- **Outdoors:** Use a **deadcat** (furry wind cover) or foam windscreen. Wind noise is one of the few audio problems that **cannot be fixed in post-production.**

#### Separate Recording

For the highest quality, record audio on a dedicated external recorder (like a Zoom H1n) while simultaneously recording scratch audio on the camera. Sync the high-quality audio to the video in editing. This gives you full control over audio quality independent of the camera's limitations.

#### Always Backup

Record a backup track. Camera audio is rarely sufficient as a primary source, but having a backup (even the built-in mic) can save you if your main mic fails.

### Common Audio Issues

#### Clipping (Distortion)

**What it sounds like:** Harsh, crackling distortion when the audio is too loud.

**Cause:** Audio input level too high—peaking above 0dB in the recording.

**Prevention:** Set recording levels so peaks stay around **-6dB to -12dB**. This is called leaving "headroom." It's better to record slightly quiet (you can boost in editing) than too loud (distortion is permanent).

**Analogy:** Clipping is like a balloon that's overinflated. You can let air out of a slightly underinflated balloon, but you can't un-pop a burst one.

#### Background Noise

- Air conditioning, computer fans, traffic, refrigerators
- **Prevention:** Record in quiet spaces. Turn off AC during recording. Close windows. Ask people to silence phones.
- **Can it be fixed in post?** To some extent—noise reduction tools exist—but they introduce artifacts and degrade audio quality. Prevention is always better.

#### Room Tone

**What it is:** The ambient sound of a room when no one is talking. Every room has a unique "sonic fingerprint"—a subtle hum, hiss, or tone.

**Why it matters:** When you cut between clips, the room tone changes abruptly and sounds jarring. The solution: record 30–60 seconds of pure room tone at the start of every session. This lets you fill gaps between cuts with consistent ambient sound.

**Analogy:** Room tone is like the silence between songs on an album. It doesn't seem important until it's missing—then everything feels disjointed and unnatural.

#### Wind Noise

Outdoor wind hitting a microphone capsule produces a low, rumbling distortion that **cannot be reliably removed in post-production.** This is why deadcats and wind screens exist. If you're shooting outside without wind protection, you're gambling with your audio.

## Part 4: Video Editing—Shaping the Story

### The Editor's Job

An editor doesn't just "put clips together." An editor **shapes the story.** The same raw footage edited differently can feel like a comedy, a drama, or a documentary. Editing controls pacing, emotion, meaning, and rhythm.

**Analogy:** An editor is like a DJ taking the same song and remixing it—adjusting the tempo, layering in effects, and rearranging the sections to create a completely different experience for the audience.

### Continuity Editing: Making Cuts Invisible

Continuity editing is a set of techniques designed to keep the viewer oriented and immersed. The goal: cuts should be **felt, not seen.** The viewer should never think "wait, where are we?" or "what just happened?"

#### The 180-Degree Rule

Imagine a straight line running through your scene (the "axis of action"). The camera should stay on **one side** of that line at all times. If you cross the line (shoot from the other side), the subject appears to suddenly switch directions on screen—which is disorienting.

**Example:** Two people face each other in a conversation. Person A is on the left, Person B on the right. If you cross the 180-degree line for the next shot, suddenly Person A is on the right and Person B is on the left. The viewer's spatial orientation breaks.

**When to break it intentionally:** Directors sometimes break the 180-degree rule to create a feeling of unease or confusion. But you have to break the rule *on purpose*, not by accident.

#### Match Cuts

Match cuts connect two shots through a shared visual or action element:
- **Match on action:** Person reaches for a door handle in one shot; next shot shows the door opening. Cut during the movement—the viewer's brain fills in the gap.
- **Eye-line match:** Character looks off-screen; next shot shows what they're looking at. The viewer's gaze follows naturally.

#### Shot-Reverse-Shot

The standard technique for filming conversations:
1. **Wide/establishing shot** — Shows both people and the environment
2. **Over-the-shoulder shot** — Shows Person A from behind Person B's shoulder
3. **Close-up on Person A** — Reaction shot
4. **Over-the-shoulder shot** — Shows Person B from behind Person A's shoulder
5. **Close-up on Person B** — Reaction shot

This back-and-forth pattern maintains spatial orientation (respecting the 180-degree rule) while creating intimacy through close-ups.

### J-Cuts and L-Cuts: Audio Transitions

These are two of the most powerful—and underused—editing techniques.

#### J-Cut
The audio from the *next* clip begins before the video cuts to it. The viewer hears the next scene starting while still watching the current scene.

**Visual:** In your editing timeline, the audio waveform of the next clip extends left (before) the video cut—forming a "J" shape.

**Effect:** Creates anticipation. The viewer hears a voice or sound and naturally wants to see what's coming next.

#### L-Cut
The audio from the *current* clip continues after the video has already cut to the next shot.

**Visual:** In your editing timeline, the audio waveform of the current clip extends right (after) the video cut—forming an "L" shape.

**Effect:** Creates a smooth transition between scenes. The viewer doesn't notice the cut because the audio bridges the gap.

**Analogy:** J-cuts are like hearing someone call your name from the next room before you see them. L-cuts are like still hearing a song playing from the other room even after you've walked away.

**When to use them:** Almost always. J-cuts and L-cuts make conversations and scene transitions feel natural. Hard cuts (where both audio and video cut simultaneously) feel abrupt by comparison.

### Pacing: The Rhythm of Your Edit

Pacing is how quickly or slowly your cuts arrive. It controls energy, tension, and emotional engagement.

| Pacing | Cut Frequency | Feeling | Use When |
|--------|--------------|---------|----------|
| Slow | Long takes (5–30+ seconds) | Calm, contemplative, tense | Emotional moments, establishing atmosphere |
| Medium | Moderate cuts (2–5 seconds) | Balanced, professional | Conversations, tutorials, general content |
| Fast | Rapid cuts (0.5–2 seconds) | Energetic, urgent, exciting | Action sequences, music videos, comedy |

**Key principle:** Pacing should match the content's emotional need. A sad scene with rapid-fire cuts feels wrong. An action sequence with long takes feels boring. **Let the story dictate the pace, not your editing software's transition library.**

### Transitions: Use Them Sparingly

#### The Cut (Most Important)
A cut is an instantaneous switch from one shot to the next. It's the most common transition in professional video. **95%+ of transitions in a well-edited video should be cuts.** They're invisible when done well.

#### Dissolve / Cross-Fade
One image gradually fades into another. Use to indicate the passage of time or a shift in location/mood. Overuse makes the video look like a slideshow.

#### Fade to Black / Fade from Black
The screen gradually goes dark (or light). Use sparingly for major chapter breaks or the end of a video. A fade is a "full stop" in visual language—save it for moments that warrant emphasis.

#### Star Wipes, Spin Transitions, etc.
Almost never appropriate for professional work. They draw attention to the transition itself rather than the content. If you find yourself reaching for a fancy transition, ask: "What is this transition *doing* for the story?" If the answer is "nothing," use a cut.

**Common misconception: "More transitions = better video."** This is backwards. Professional editing looks effortless because it's mostly invisible. The best edit is one the viewer doesn't notice. Fancy transitions signal inexperience, not creativity.

### Color Grading and Color Correction

#### Color Correction (Fixing Problems)

Color correction is the process of making the footage look **technically correct**—accurate colors, proper exposure, consistent white balance across clips.

**Primary correction** adjusts the overall image:
- **Exposure** — Overall brightness
- **Contrast** — Difference between light and dark areas
- **Highlights** — Brightest parts of the image
- **Shadows** — Darkest parts of the image
- **Midtones** — Everything in between

**White balance** corrects color casts. Indoor footage under tungsten lights looks orange; correct it to neutral white. Outdoor footage under overcast skies looks blue; warm it up.

**Secondary correction** isolates specific colors for targeted adjustment. For example: making only the sky more blue, or only skin tones warmer, without affecting the rest of the image.

#### Color Grading (Creative Choices)

Color grading is the process of giving your footage a **stylistic look.** This is where you establish mood and tone:

- Warm, golden tones → Nostalgic, romantic, happy
- Cool, blue tones → Cold, clinical, sad, futuristic
- Desaturated → Gritty, serious, documentary
- High contrast + warm highlights → Cinematic blockbuster feel

#### LUTs (Look-Up Tables)

A LUT is a preset color grade that you apply to your footage as a starting point. Think of it like an Instagram filter—but more nuanced and professional.

**Critical distinction:** LUTs are a **starting point, not an endpoint.** Applying a LUT without adjusting the base exposure and white balance first produces inconsistent, unpredictable results. Always correct the image first, *then* apply a creative grade.

**Analogy:** A LUT is like a recipe. You still need to adjust seasoning to taste. The recipe gets you 70% there; your adjustments finish the job.

## Part 5: Exporting—Getting Your Work to the World

### Export Settings That Matter

Exporting is where your carefully edited project becomes a deliverable file. Poor export settings can undo all your hard work—causing pixelation, banding, audio sync issues, or massive file sizes.

#### Key Export Parameters

| Parameter | What It Controls | Recommended for YouTube/Web |
|-----------|-----------------|----------------------------|
| **Codec** | Compression algorithm | H.264 (most compatible) or H.265 (smaller files) |
| **Resolution** | Frame size | Match your timeline (1080p or 4K) |
| **Frame rate** | Frames per second | Match your source (24fps, 30fps, or 60fps) |
| **Bitrate** | Data per second (quality/size) | 10–20 Mbps for 1080p; 35–50 Mbps for 4K |
| **Audio codec** | Audio compression | AAC, 320kbps |
| **Audio sample rate** | Audio frequency resolution | 48kHz (standard for video) |

#### Bitrate Explained

Bitrate is the amount of data used per second of video. Higher bitrate = better quality = larger files.

| Quality Level | 1080p Bitrate | 4K Bitrate |
|---------------|---------------|------------|
| Low (acceptable) | 5–8 Mbps | 15–20 Mbps |
| Medium (recommended) | 10–15 Mbps | 30–40 Mbps |
| High (high quality) | 20–30 Mbps | 50–100 Mbps |

**Trade-off:** Doubling your bitrate roughly doubles your file size. For YouTube and social media, the medium range is ideal—high quality without unnecessarily large files. YouTube will re-compress your video anyway, so uploading at extreme bitrates wastes time without visible improvement.

### Aspect Ratios for Different Platforms

Each platform has its own display format. Planning your aspect ratio **during shooting** (not just in editing) is essential.

| Platform | Aspect Ratio | Dimensions | Notes |
|----------|-------------|------------|-------|
| YouTube (standard) | 16:9 | 1920×1080 | Landscape; the default |
| TikTok | 9:16 | 1080×1920 | Vertical; full-screen mobile |
| Instagram Reels | 9:16 | 1080×1920 | Vertical; same as TikTok |
| Instagram Feed | 1:1 or 4:5 | 1080×1080 or 1080×1350 | Square or portrait |
| Instagram Stories | 9:16 | 1080×1920 | Vertical; full-screen mobile |
| Twitter/X | 16:9 | 1920×1080 | Landscape; max 2:20 video |

**The problem with adapting:** If you shoot everything in landscape (16:9) and later need vertical (9:16) for TikTok, you'll have to crop—which cuts off parts of the image—or add black bars. Plan ahead: shoot with extra headroom so you can reframe for vertical later, or shoot multiple versions.

### Captions and Accessibility

Captions aren't just a nice-to-have—they're essential for accessibility and increasingly required by law for public-facing content.

#### Types of Captions

| Type | Description | Use Case |
|------|-------------|----------|
| **Burned-in (open captions)** | Permanently embedded in the video | Social media (where auto-captions may be inaccurate) |
| **Sidecar files (closed captions)** | Separate file that viewers can toggle on/off | YouTube, professional delivery, streaming platforms |

**Common caption file formats:**
- **SRT (SubRip)** — Most widely supported, simple text format
- **VTT (WebVTT)** — HTML5 standard, supports styling
- **EBU-STL** — Broadcast standard

#### Accessibility Best Practices

- Caption all spoken dialogue and important sound effects (e.g., "[door slams]", "[upbeat music]")
- Keep captions on screen for 1–3 seconds minimum
- Limit to 1–2 lines per caption card
- Use a readable font at a reasonable size
- Place captions where they don't cover important visual information (typically bottom-center)
- Ensure sufficient contrast between text and background (many editors add a semi-transparent background box behind caption text)

## Part 6: Bringing It All Together

### The Production Pipeline

Here's how all five topics connect in a real workflow:

```
CAPTURE → LIGHTING → AUDIO → EDITING → EXPORTING
   |          |         |         |          |
 Camera     Three-    Mic      Cuts,      Codec,
 settings,  point     types,   pacing,     resolution,
 frame      setup,    best     color       bitrate,
 rate,      color     practices, grading    captions
 resolution temp.     backup   LUTs
```

Each stage depends on the ones before it. Bad capture limits what editing can fix. Bad lighting can't be fully corrected in grading. Bad audio is nearly impossible to fix in post. Good capture, lighting, and audio make editing faster and exporting cleaner.

### Common Misconceptions—Debunked

| Misconception | Reality |
|---------------|---------|
| "Video quality = resolution" | Lighting, audio, and composition matter far more than pixel count |
| "It's fine in post" | Poor lighting and audio are difficult or impossible to fix later |
| "More transitions = better video" | Cuts are usually best; effects should serve the story |
| "You need expensive gear" | Good storytelling with decent lighting and audio beats expensive equipment |
| "Editing takes longer than filming" | Well-shot footage requires minimal editing; poor footage requires extensive repair |

### The 10x Rule of Video Production

For every **1 hour** of filming, expect:
- **1–3 hours** of preparation (lighting, audio setup, framing)
- **3–10 hours** of editing (depending on complexity)

This ratio is normal. It's why preparation matters: spending an extra 30 minutes getting lighting and audio right on set can save **hours** of painful editing.

---

## Key Takeaways

1. **Camera settings are creative tools, not technical jargon.** ISO, aperture, and shutter speed control brightness and look. Frame rate and resolution serve different purposes. Choose them intentionally.

2. **Three-point lighting is the foundation of professional video.** Key light defines, fill light softens, back light separates. Match color temperatures. Soft light from a window can replace expensive equipment.

3. **Audio quality is non-negotiable.** Get the mic close, monitor with headphones, record backup tracks, and record room tone. Wind noise and clipping are permanent problems—prevention only.

4. **Editing shapes the story.** The 180-degree rule keeps viewers oriented. J-cuts and L-cuts create natural flow. Pacing should match the content's emotional need. Transitions should be invisible.

5. **Export intentionally.** Match codec, resolution, and bitrate to your delivery platform. Plan aspect ratios during shooting. Add captions for accessibility.

6. **Quality comes from craft, not gear.** The techniques in this lesson work with a smartphone, a $20 clip-on mic, and free software. Lighting and audio knowledge transform your output more than any equipment upgrade.
