# Trends Workshop: Hunting for Real Trends in Web Publishing

## From Passive Reading to Active Research

The last few lessons introduced you to trends in web publishing — current, driving, analysing, and predicting. Now you flip roles. Instead of receiving someone else's trend summary, you build your own: pick one real trend, gather evidence for it, judge whether that evidence is trustworthy, and present it as a case study.

A **trend** is a general direction something is developing over time — not a fad (short-lived) or a permanent shift (a long-lasting structural change). Your job in this workshop is to prove a trend is real and worth caring about, not merely to repeat impressive-sounding numbers. The workshop mirrors a professional research-to-pitch loop: **define the question → gather sources → evaluate them → synthesise a claim → present it.**

A credible trend case study answers four questions:

1. **What is the trend?** Name it clearly.
2. **What evidence supports it?** Real statistics, named products, named companies.
3. **Who is driving or adopting it, and why?** Which people or organisations, and what motivates them.
4. **Does it hold up in a Caribbean context?** Or is it hype built for other markets?

On the last point, remember: global trend numbers are often published from a US or European perspective. They assume fast, cheap internet, high-end devices, and Western-language prompts. Your differentiator is weighing whether a trend is practical given **regional bandwidth, data costs, device mix, and mobile-first behaviour.**

## Rate Your Sources: CRAP + SIFT

The core skill of this workshop is not finding facts — it is knowing **how you know a trend is real**. Two proven tools work together.

### CRAP — the memory hook

Rate each source on four criteria:

- **Currency** — How recent is it? A 2021 stat does not prove a 2026 trend.
- **Reliability** — Is it evidence-backed and confirmable elsewhere?
- **Authority** — Who wrote it, and what is their expertise?
- **Purpose / point of view** — Is this written to inform, persuade, or sell?

A related version, **CRAAP** (California State University, Chico, 2004), adds **Relevance** and **Accuracy**. The two acronyms are widely used interchangeably in school and college writing guides, so use whichever your class prefers.

### SIFT — the workflow

CRAP gives you criteria; SIFT (Mike Caulfield, 2019) gives you a procedure. When you meet a source:

- **Stop** — pause before trusting it.
- **Investigate the source** — who is behind this?
- **Find better coverage** — look for independent reporting.
- **Trace claims back to their original context** — find where a number or quote originally came from.

SIFT relies on **lateral reading**: leaving the page to check its claims, author, and reputation against outside sources. This is far more reliable than **vertical reading** — judging a page by its looks, spelling, and design. Research shows that checklist-only, on-page evaluation is measurably weaker at spotting misinformation than lateral reading. Use CRAP to remember what to check, and SIFT to actually check it.

## Triangulation: One Source Is Not a Trend

A single viral article — or worse, a vendor's own marketing page — does not prove a trend. **Triangulation** means a trend is only credible when at least two independent sources point the same way.

| Signal | Weak evidence | Strong evidence |
|--------|--------------|-----------------|
| Source type | One viral blog post | Two or more independent outlets or studies |
| Origin | Vendor marketing page about its own product | Independent coverage + the vendor's data cross-checked |
| Timing | Old stat recycled without a date | Current (2025–2026) figures with clear dates |
| Agreement | One blockbuster number | Multiple sources roughly agreeing |
| Context | Number quoted out of nowhere | Defined original context and method |

## Stats to Seed Your Case Study (2026)

These figures are common starting points — always check the source and date before citing them.

- **Mobile dominance**: roughly 58–60% of global web traffic is mobile; about 78% of retail traffic is mobile; mobile cart abandonment runs high (~85%) because of UX and performance issues.
- **Short-form video**: around 49% of marketers rank it their top-ROI format; it generates roughly 2.5× more engagement than long-form; about 73–78% of people prefer short video when learning about a product.
- **AI in production**: roughly 63% of marketers use AI for video creation/editing, and near-majority use AI daily for image/video generation; about 93% of web designers reportedly use some kind of AI tool.
- **AI search**: in 2026 around 44% of AI-search users prefer it over traditional search (~31%), and some sources claim AI-driven traffic converts higher. Treat this one as a debate, not a settled fact — the numbers vary heavily by source.

### Caribbean anchor data

GSMA's *Mobile Economy Caribbean 2026* reports mobile contributed roughly **US$21 billion** to the Caribbean economy in 2025 (**~8.1% of GDP**), heading toward about **$23 billion by 2030**; around **38% of regional operators** have launched at least one AI tool. This is your bridge between a global trend and a regional economic story.

### A cautionary tale: "the death of the cookie"

Cookie deprecation is a perfect object lesson in trend reporting. The story kept reversing: Google reversed its Chrome phase-out plan (keeping cookies and dropping its separate consent prompt, April 2025), then retired most of its **Privacy Sandbox** APIs (October 2025), while **Safari and Firefox still block third-party cookies by default** (~17–20% of global traffic is already cookieless). Anyone who confidently reports "the cookie is dead" is oversimplifying. Citing this trend correctly means acknowledging the conflict — exactly what SIFT's "trace back to original context" move forces you to do.

## Choosing a Presentation Tool

Delivering your case study is part of the pitch. Match the tool to your needs:

| Dimension | Canva | Google Slides | PowerPoint |
|-----------|-------|---------------|------------|
| Speed of visual polish | Fastest — huge template library | Medium | Medium |
| Collaboration | Limited | Real-time, best in class | Limited on free tier |
| Offline work | Limited | Requires connection | Strong |
| Cost | Free tier | Free (Google account) | Subscription |
| Best for | Quick, attractive decks | Group work and browser sharing | Precise control, offline delivery |

For a **5-minute share-out**, keep it tight — roughly one slide per 60–90 seconds, so **4–6 slides maximum**, and avoid text walls.

## Worked Example: SIFT-ing "The Cookie Is Dead"

Take a headline claiming "third-party cookies are dead forever."

1. **Stop** — don't quote it yet.
2. **Investigate** — the article ships a product that replaces cookies; it has a commercial stake.
3. **Find better coverage** — check a couple of independent tech-news write-ups from 2025–2026.
4. **Trace back** — find the original Google announcement. It says Chrome kept cookies and retired the Privacy Sandbox APIs.

Result: the trend is real but contradictory — Chrome kept cookies while other browsers blocked them. Your case study is stronger because you report the nuance, and you have two independent sources to prove it.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"A source is credible if it looks good."** Appearance is a poor signal. Lateral reading — checking the author, agenda, and original context against other sources — is far more reliable.
- **"One impressive statistic proves a trend."** A single figure can be cherry-picked, misdated, or fabricated. You need at least two independent, reasonably current sources and a defined context.
- **"Global numbers automatically apply to the Caribbean."** Global stats assume fast, cheap connectivity and high-end devices. Re-check against regional bandwidth, data costs, and device mix.
- **"Vendor marketing pages are reliable trend sources."** A company writing about its own product category has a commercial purpose (the "P" of CRAP). Corroborate it with independent coverage.
- **"More slides and animations make a better presentation."** Assessment should reward credible sourcing, accurate claims, and clear reasoning. Decorative polish without evidence earns lower marks.
- **"'The cookie is dead' is a settled fact."** It's a rolling, contradictory story. Citing it correctly means acknowledging the conflict.
- **"All statistics in a trend article are new."** Trend roundups recycle old figures (for example, "94% of first impressions are design-related" is older research). Check dates before trusting a number.

## Summary

This workshop turns you from a reader of trends into a researcher of them. Define your trend, gather evidence, and judge that evidence with CRAP (currency, reliability, authority, purpose) plus the SIFT workflow (stop, investigate, find better coverage, trace back). Triangulate — two independent sources beat one flashy number — and always stress-test a global trend against a Caribbean context of bandwidth, data costs, and device mix. Then present your claim in 4–6 slides with a tool matched to your collaboration and offline needs. A plain, well-sourced claim always defeats a flashy, unsourced one.
