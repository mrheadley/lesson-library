# Comparing Ecosystems: A Method, Not a Ranking

## Why "Is Country A Better Than Country B?" Is the Wrong Question

The instinct when comparing two mobile ecosystems is to rank them — "Jamaica beats Barbados," "one wins the mobile race." Resist it. An ecosystem is too layered for a single "winner," and the question that actually matters for a designer or policy-maker is *different layers are strong and weak where*. This lesson gives you a **method** — a framework of lenses — for making that comparison fairly, repeatably, and usefully.

## The Framework: Four Layers

Think of a mobile ecosystem as four stacked layers. Every country lives on all four simultaneously, and each layer tells a different story:

1. **Infrastructure (supply)** — the physical system: submarine cables, cell towers, spectrum, fibre, backhaul. Does a usable signal exist, and how robust is it?
2. **Adoption (usage)** — who actually uses it: subscriptions, smartphone ownership, mobile internet use, the usage gap. A country can have strong infrastructure and weak adoption.
3. **Production (ecosystem depth)** — what the market *makes*: local apps, fintech, content, developer communities. A country can adopt heavily while producing little.
4. **Policy (enabling environment)** — the rules: regulation, spectrum allocation, competition, digital-government initiatives. Policy either greases or jams every other layer.

The discipline is to **separate the layers**. If you say "Country A has a better mobile ecosystem," you must specify *which layer* — because almost every real country is strong on some and weak on others. Identifying that mix is the actual value of the analysis.

## The Two Rules of Fair Comparison

Two rules keep a comparison honest, and violating them is the source of most bad analysis.

**Rule 1 — Standardise the metric.** "Penetration" is meaningless until you define what it counts: subscriptions (SIMs) or users (people)? Adults or all residents? Same year or different years? Comparing Jamaica's *subscription* count to Haiti's *adult-user* count is comparing apples to mainframe computers. Force both countries onto the same definition before you compare anything.

**Rule 2 — Match the country type (or say why you didn't).** Comparing a small, rich, tourist island to a large, poorer, populous one tells you about *difference in type*, not about which is "better." Sometimes that contrast *is* the point — but you must choose pairs deliberately and state what the choice is meant to teach.

## Reading the Layers: What Each Comparison Is Really Telling You

Because each layer implies a different kind of opportunity, identifying the layer is not an academic exercise — it tells you what to do:

- A **coverage gap** (weak infrastructure layer) → the need is investment: cables, towers, spectrum.
- A **usage gap** (strong coverage, weak adoption) → the need is **affordability and skills**: cheaper devices, cheaper data, digital literacy. More coverage will not fix it.
- A **production gap** (heavy adoption, thin local supply) → the need is **creator/developer support**: training, capital, distribution help. This is where an *app* opportunity lives.
- A **policy gap** (rules blocking progress — no spectrum, heavy regulation) → the need is **reform**: spectrum auctions, competition, digital-ID and single-market programmes.

So a framework that merely *describes* four layers becomes an *analytical engine* the moment you ask "which layer is the bottleneck here?" — and that is the question that turns comparison into decision.

## Opportunity Analysis: Matching a Gap with an Asset

The framework culminates in a single, concrete move: **opportunity analysis** — matching a gap in one layer with an asset in another.

The mental act is: *this is missing, and the market happens to have that*, therefore *a product or policy could connect them*. The Caribbean stacks of examples are abundant:

- A **production gap** (thin local apps) + a strong **payment/fintech asset** → opportunity for regional payment services built on local rails.
- A **usage gap** in rural areas + a large **diaspora** remittance asset → opportunity for cheap remittance/agent apps.
- Strong **adoption of messaging** (WhatsApp is everywhere) + a weak **local-content asset** → opportunity for a curated hyperlocal information app built on the habit people already have.

Note the discipline inside this. An opportunity is not "an idea I like." It is a claim that must be defensible from the comparison's own two sides: the gap you found and the asset you matched. If either is missing, the opportunity statement collapses.

## Common Misconceptions

### "One number can rank countries' ecosystems"
No single metric — penetration, smartphone rate, revenue, coverage — ranks an ecosystem. The four layers disagree, and that disagreement is the analysis.

### "Bigger population = better ecosystem"
The Dominican Republic is the region's largest market but sits at middle penetration. Scale brings more subscribers, not uniformly better per-user outcomes.

### "Comparing numbers is straightforward"
Definitions differ (subscriptions vs users vs adults; SIMs vs people; coverage vs adoption). Standardising the metric is step one — skipping it produces junk.

### "Infrastructure is the whole story"
A country can have excellent coverage but poor adoption (affordability gap) or heavy adoption but thin production. The framework's whole point is that the layers are distinct.

### "Policy is a side-note"
Spectrum allocation, competition rules and digital-government programmes are decisive — 5G rollout, new operators and digital-ID systems all hinge on the policy layer. It is not decoration.

## Summary

Comparing mobile ecosystems is a method, not a ranking. Use four layers — infrastructure, adoption, production, policy — and keep them separate. Apply two rules of fairness: standardise the metric, and match (or deliberately choose) the country type. Then convert description into decision by asking which layer is the bottleneck: coverage gap → invest; usage gap → affordability/skills; production gap → creator/developer support (the app opportunity); policy gap → reform. Finally, run an opportunity analysis: match the gap you found with an asset the market has, and state it as a defensible one-liner. When students leave this lesson, they should be able to look at any two Caribbean markets and produce, not a ranking, but a layered portrait plus one concrete opportunity — which is precisely the skill they will exercise in the comparison workshop.
