# Caribbean Mobile Infrastructure & Statistics: Measured Island by Island

## The Caribbean Is Not One Market

Ask a simple question — "what's the mobile penetration of the Caribbean?" — and the honest answer is "which Caribbean?" The region is not a telecoms market in the way France or even the United States is. It is a mosaic: independent nations, overseas territories, and islands speaking English, Spanish, French and Dutch, each with its own regulator, its own operators, and its own place on the connectivity curve.

That matters for a simple reason: **averages hide more than they reveal.** A region-wide "108% penetration" flattens a spread that runs from Haiti (around 60%) to affluent small territories above 140%. To think clearly about the Caribbean's mobile ecosystem, you must think island by island — and always separate *subscriptions* from *people*, *coverage* from *usage*, and *"mobile is available"* from *"mobile is affordable."*

## Mobile Is the Internet Here

In most of the Caribbean there is no "broadband vs. mobile" choice — for the majority of households, **mobile data is the internet.** Fixed fiber-optic-to-the-home (FTTH) reaches only about **1.9 million households** across the region, while mobile networks cover far more of the population. So the phone in a Caribbean user's hand is not one way to go online; it is usually the only way. This is why every app built for Caribbean users must treat mobile-first and data-light as hard requirements rather than pleasant options (a theme later lessons develop).

## Subscriptions Are Not People: Why Penetration Exceeds 100%

Applause the same ladder you met in the global lesson:

- **Mobile-cellular subscriptions** — SIM cards sold and active, whether prepaid or postpaid.
- **Unique mobile subscribers** — actual human beings who use a mobile service.

Because one person can hold two or three SIMs (chasing cheaper data bundles, or separate lines for calls and data), **subscription counts routinely pass the population count.** Regional penetration averaged about **108% in 2025**, and the English-speaking islands — with their two-operator duopolies and multi-SIM habits — typically report higher numbers than the larger Spanish-speaking markets.

The practical takeaway: "143% penetration" (Trinidad) does not mean everyone in Trinidad owns 1.43 phones. It means there are more active SIMs than residents. It is a proxy for connectivity activity, not a count of connected people.

## Prepaid Is the Norm

A second structural fact shapes everything downstream: **the Caribbean is a prepaid region.** Users buy data bundles in advance and top up as they go, rather than signing monthly postpaid contracts. The postpaid-credit-card-default that much global app design assumes simply does not apply. Prepaid builds in an expectation of metered, budgeted data — which is exactly why data price (not network quality) is the leading reason Caribbean consumers switch operators. An app that silently burns through a user's bundle will be abandoned, not forgiven. (Designing for this returns in the bandwidth lesson.)

## The Two Great Gaps

Separate these two, because people loop them together far too easily:

- **Coverage gap** — people with *no signal at all*. In the Caribbean this is smaller, because mobile networks do reach most of the population.
- **Usage gap** — people who live *inside* a signal but do not subscribe or use mobile internet, usually because data or a device costs too much.

"Coverage" tells you where a signal exists; it says almost nothing about whether people can afford to use it. A map declaring high mobile coverage is compatible with a large population still on the wrong side of the affordability gap.

## 5G Has Arrived — On Selected Islands

The regional 5G map is the clearest illustration of "two-speed Caribbean" you will see. As of early 2026:

- **Active commercial 5G:** Jamaica (Digicel launched in Kingston in October 2024 and expanded across the capital corridor), Trinidad & Tobago (Digicel, Port of Spain and the East–West corridor), the Cayman Islands (Digicel on Grand Cayman), and **Puerto Rico**, which is genuinely mature — island-wide 5G from T-Mobile (2023) and Claro (2024).
- **Racing to launch:** Barbados (spectrum approved, Bridgetown first), the Bahamas (spectrum pending regulator consultation, Nassau first), and the Dominican Republic (a 5G auction in 2025, with Claro and Altice targeting Santo Domingo by end-2026).
- **Waiting on 4G:** most of the rest, including much of the OECS, Haiti, Suriname and Guyana, projected to stay on 4G LTE into 2027 on published operator roadmaps.

Now put a number on the pace: **5G is only about 10% of Caribbean connections in 2025**, forecast to reach roughly **23% by 2030**. Witness the inequality inside that number. The three largest markets — Puerto Rico, Jamaica and Trinidad — are expected to account for **more than 65% of all 5G connections**. Puerto Rico alone has about a **31% subscriber adoption** rate for 5G; the Cayman Islands about **22%**; most markets are far lower or at zero. "5G is in the Caribbean" is technically true; "5G is everywhere in the Caribbean" is not.

And a structural warning beneath the map: consumer willingness to *pay more* for 5G is limited. In Jamaica, around 72% of smartphone users are *aware* of 5G, but only about 22% would pay 20% or more for it — and the main driver of interest is **home-broadband replacement**, not mobile-specific apps. Operators must educate on use cases, not sell speed specs.

## Reading the Country Data

A quick tour of the spread (figures are adult-population penetration unless noted, from ITU-derived and national-regulator data):

| Market | Penetration | Smartphone rate | 5G status |
|--------|-------------|-----------------|-----------|
| Haiti | ~60% | ~45% | 3G / limited 4G |
| Dominican Republic | ~78–84% | ~68% | 4G (~88% coverage); 5G from end-2026 |
| Guyana | ~80–103% | ~72% | 4G (E-Networks launched some 5G VoLTE) |
| Small Eastern Caribbean (OECS) | ~82% | ~72% | 4G-only expected to 2027 |
| Bahamas | ~85–105% | ~79% | pending regulator approval |
| Barbados | ~88% | ~78% | spectrum approved |
| Jamaica | ~92–112% | ~82% | active (Digicel, 2024) |
| Trinidad & Tobago | ~90–143% | ~80% | active (Digicel, late 2024) |
| Puerto Rico | ~107% | high | mature (island-wide) |
| Cayman Islands | ~143% | high | active (Digicel, ~22%) |

Notice the two ways to read "leading": the **Dominican Republic is the region's largest market by subscribers** — about **9.2 million active SIMs** — yet sits at a middle penetration of ~84%, because scale (more people to dilute the count) differs from density. Meanwhile tiny, high-income **Cayman and Bermuda** post the highest numbers through a combination of small populations, tourism-driven visitor SIMs, and wealthy, financed customers.

## The Operators: Two Giants and a Field

Throughout the English-speaking Caribbean, the competition is largely a **two-horse race**:

- **Digicel** — operates across Jamaica, Trinidad & Tobago, Barbados, the Cayman Islands, Guyana, Suriname, Haiti, Curaçao and most OECS states; the main mover behind the region's early 5G launches.
- **Liberty Latin America (Flow / C&W / BTC)** — the fixed-line incumbent turned mobile competitor across the English islands and the Bahamas.

Beyond these, the Dominican Republic runs its own three-operator contest — **Claro (América Móvil) ~51%**, **Altice ~31%**, **Digicel ~18%** — and Puerto Rico operates under US (FCC) rules with T-Mobile, AT&T and Claro. Cuba is a state monopoly (ETECSA). The Dutch Caribbean has its own mix of operators and regulators. The shape of competition — a duopoly here, a full market there — directly sets data prices, and therefore which apps succeed.

## Why All This Matters to an App Designer

Every number in this lesson is a design constraint in disguise:

- **Penetration over 100%** says "don't assume one user = one device"; a Caribbean user may juggle SIMs and devices.
- **Prepaid as default** says "meter the data"; design light.
- **The 5G map** says "assume 4G, often slower and pricier"; a heavy app fails most of the region for years to come.
- **The usage gap** says "the barrier is cost, not coverage"; affordability is a feature, not an extra.
- **Fragmentation** says "build to serve more than one island or you'll outgrow your market in an afternoon."

## Common Misconceptions

### "Penetration above 100% means everyone is connected"
It counts SIMs, not people. Dual-SIM and prepaid habits inflate the count while real usage gaps remain.

### "The Caribbean is one mobile market"
It is dozens — different regulators, operators, income levels and 5G timelines, from Puerto Rico to Haiti.

### "5G is about to be everywhere here"
As of 2026 only four markets had active commercial 5G, and it is still ~10% of connections. Most islands remain on 4G for years.

### "High coverage means high usage"
Coverage is where a signal exists; affordability is a separate, binding constraint. The two must not be conflated.

### "Postpaid contracts dominate like in North America"
The Caribbean is overwhelmingly prepaid — which reshapes data pricing and how apps must treat connectivity.

## Summary

Treat the Caribbean as a set of distinct, two-speed markets, not one region. Mobile data, not fixed broadband (only ~1.9 million FTTH households), is the internet for most people. Penetration above 100% reflects subscriptions and multi-SIM habits, not connected people. Prepaid is the structural norm, making data price — not network quality — the main churn driver. 5G is real but concentrated: four active markets, ~10% of connections, with Puerto Rico, Jamaica and Trinidad driving most of it while most islands wait on 4G. Every one of these figures is a design constraint: build light, meter data, assume 4G, and design for a fragmented, prepaid, affordability-bound market.
