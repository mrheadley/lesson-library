# Consumption vs. Production: The Global Mobile Economy

## Two Very Different Maps

Hold two maps of the mobile world side by side and they barely overlap. One map shows **who consumes** — who scrolls, streams, downloads, and uses apps. That map covers almost everyone: mobile is the primary way a majority of humanity gets online, and the Caribbean's mobile-first reality is the global norm, not an exception. The other map shows **who produces** — who builds and owns the apps, platforms and content. That map is brutally concentrated: a small handful of companies, overwhelmingly headquartered in the United States and China, generate most of the value.

This asymmetry — everyone consumes, a few produce — is the single most important structural fact about the global digital economy. It explains why your phone is full of apps made thousands of miles away, and why "we use these apps" is not the same as "we take part in the economy that makes them."

## Consumption Is Near-Universal

Consumption spreads across the whole planet for a simple reason: you don't need much to be a consumer. A prepaid data bundle and (increasingly) a smartphone let almost anyone stream, chat, and post. That is why the world's most-used apps — WhatsApp, Facebook, Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, Google — dominate in essentially every market, including the Caribbean, regardless of local language or income. They solve needs everyone has: communicating, connecting, watching, finding things.

Two consequences follow. First, **the top apps are the same almost everywhere** — a handful of property owners capture usage in country after country. Second, **"mobile-first" is not a luxury-there default**: for most of the world's users, including the Caribbean, mobile is the *only* screen and the *only* connection, and it runs on lower-end phones and slower, more expensive data than in wealthy markets.

## Production Is Concentrated

Production is a different game. Building and *owning* a platform requires capital, engineering talent, distribution, and — critically — control of a gate. That gate is the **app store**:

- **Apple (iOS / App Store)** and **Google (Android / Google Play)** together run the world's two app markets. The overwhelming share of apps is distributed through them, and their rules govern what gets built, how it is paid for, and what cut the platform takes.
- Beyond the stores, the **operating systems, search, social, messaging, streaming and payment rails** that everything runs on are also produced by a handful of US- and China-headquartered firms.

So production concentrates in a small set of "hubs" — Silicon Valley, China's tech centers (Shenzhen, Beijing), and a few others — while consumption spreads everywhere. The Caribbean, like most of the world, sits on the consumption side: it uses these products heavily but produces almost none of the underlying platforms.

## The Phone in Your Pocket Is a Global Supply Chain

Production is not just software. The physical device is itself a lesson in global supply chains. No country "makes" a smartphone end to end:

- **Designed** in the US or South Korea.
- **Chips fabricated** in Taiwan (and other foundry hubs).
- **Assembled** in China, India, or Vietnam.
- **Burdened** by a chain of raw materials, components, screens and batteries from anywhere on Earth.

You are consuming a product no single nation produces alone. This is why smartphone *cost* is such a demand-side constraint in lower-income markets: the cheapest usable handset still costs a meaningful slice of a monthly income, so feature phones and "lite" app versions persist. The device itself is a daily, tactile reminder that production is geographically layered while consumption is universal.

## The Creator Economy: Open to Create, Closed to Own

Here is the nuance that trips people up. Anyone can now *create* content — post a video, upload music, start a podcast — with essentially no barrier. Entry into content creation is wide open. But *owning the channel that monetises it* is not. Creators upload onto platforms owned elsewhere; the ad networks, recommendation engines and payment systems that decide who earns are centralised.

So "production" has two meanings, and you must keep them apart:

- **Producing content** — open, democratic, anyone.
- **Producing the platform** — closed, concentrated, a handful of owners.

That distinction is the whole story of the consumption–production gap. A Caribbean creator can absolutely make a TikTok; but the revenue systems, the audience-owning algorithm, and the platform itself remain foreign-owned. **Creating on a platform is not the same as owning the platform.**

## Why App Revenue Is Not the Same as App Users

A final misfit to master: **where revenue sits is not where users sit.** iOS has historically generated more *revenue per user*, while Android has more *users worldwide*. Value concentrates in the highest-spending markets. So the global app economy's revenue map — overweighted toward wealthy, postpaid, credit-card-economies — looks nothing like the population map. When someone quotes "the app industry is worth X," always ask *whose apps* and *which users' money*.

## Common Misconceptions

### "Because we use global apps, we are part of global production"
Using is consumption; the value-owning layer (ownership of the platform) remains elsewhere. Participation in the *use* of an app is not participation in the *economics of making* it.

### "Anyone can produce, so production is now equal"
Creating content is open, but owning the distribution and monetisation platform is still centralised. The asymmetry remains: open to create, closed to own.

### "Mobile-first is a developed-market luxury"
Most of the world — including the Caribbean — is mobile-first because fixed broadband is scarce. The constraint is data/device *cost*, not choice.

### "A smartphone is made in one country"
It is a global supply-chain product. No single market produces a phone end-to-end.

### "Revenue data tells you where users are"
It tells you where the *money* is. Users are everywhere; the buying power that drives app revenue is not evenly spread.

## Summary

Consumption is universal; production is concentrated. Almost everyone uses mobile apps — and the top apps are the same US/China-owned handful nearly everywhere — because the needs they serve are universal and consumption needs little beyond a device and a data plan. Production, by contrast, is gated by app stores, operating systems and payment rails owned by a few firms clustered in a few hubs. The phone itself is a supply-chain product distributed across many countries. The creator economy is open to *create* but closed to *own*. And the revenue map differs from the user map because spending power is uneven. The Caribbean, like most of the world, consumes far more mobile value than it produces — and that asymmetry is precisely the opportunity that later lessons on local apps turn into a design brief.
