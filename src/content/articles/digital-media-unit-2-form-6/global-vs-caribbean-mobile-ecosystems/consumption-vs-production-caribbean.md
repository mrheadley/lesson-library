# Consumption vs. Production: The Caribbean Reality

## A Region That Consumes More Than It Makes

The chapter's title is the whole argument in six words. The Caribbean is a heavy, sophisticated *consumer* of global digital content and services, and a much smaller — but real and growing — *producer*. Understanding which is which, and where local production genuinely succeeds, is the difference between wishful thinking and a workable app strategy.

Here is the honest profile: Caribbean users spend most of their digital time inside a small set of foreign-owned apps — **WhatsApp, Facebook, Instagram, TikTok, YouTube** — through which they also consume most of their information and culture. The region *produces* comparatively few globally significant platforms. But it *does* produce meaningful things in specific niches: local banking and payment apps, government-service apps, and cultural content aimed at the diaspora. The task of this lesson is to map that asymmetry precisely.

## Consumption: Mobile-First and Messaging-Led

Two words capture Caribbean consumption: **mobile-first** and **messaging-led**.

- **Mobile-first** because, as the previous lessons made clear, mobile data is the internet for most households and fixed broadband is scarce.
- **Messaging-led** because WhatsApp in particular has become near-universal — effectively public infrastructure. In the Caribbean it is not only how people talk to each other; it is how businesses take orders, how schools message parents, how groups organise events, and how news spreads (for better and worse). Alongside it, Facebook, Instagram and TikTok dominate time and attention.

The point to absorb: the region consumes *through* foreign-owned channels. Its heavy usage of these apps is consumption, not production. Local consumption happening *on* a foreign platform is still consumption.

## The Local Content Gap

If Caribbean users are consuming so much digital content, why is the region's own digital content and app production so thin? The answer is a **local content gap**: people here use and enjoy a large amount of foreign-made content because there is comparatively little genuinely local material — regional news aggregation, Caribbean music beyond what global streamers carry, local-language apps, community services — produced and packaged for them.

The gap is not a flaw or a failure; it is the most important **opportunity** in this part of the course. Wherever a need is real and the local supply is thin, there is room for a product made for and by the region. The later lessons on local apps (culture, news, services) are all, in a sense, strategies for closing this one gap.

## The Diaspora: An Audience and an Engine

Any honest map of Caribbean consumption and production must include the diaspora — the millions of Caribbean nationals living abroad, overwhelmingly in the United States, the United Kingdom and Canada.

The diaspora matters twice over:

- **As an audience:** a Caribbean cultural or news app does not only serve the islands; it serves a transnational community that outnumbers many home markets. A product aimed at "Caribbean people" has a combined reach far larger than any single island.
- **As an engine:** the diaspora drives remittances — money sent home that is a pillar of many Caribbean economies, often flowing through digital apps — and supplies talent, investment and cultural demand that local producers draw on.

Diaspora reality also reshapes consumption: a family member abroad influences expectations (of service, speed, quality) that Caribbean users bring to local apps, and provides a concrete use case (staying connected to home news, music and services) that local products are uniquely placed to serve.

## Where Caribbean Production Actually Succeeds

Production is not absent — it is *concentrated* in niches that match local reality. The standout is **financial services**:

- Mobile banking and payment apps are genuinely locally produced and heavily used. Over half of Caribbean adults have used a mobile banking app (up from under a third in 2020), and roughly two-thirds have made a digital payment in the past year.
- Platforms like **Lynk** (Jamaica), **WiPay** (Trinidad and regionally), **SurePay** (Barbados) and others succeeded because they solve a *universal* need — moving money — with *local* rails: prepaid, QR, USSD, remittances, and a high unbanked population to serve.

The pattern generalises: **Caribbean production wins where it (1) meets a real local need, (2) works within the region's device/data reality, and (3) is localised — language, currency, payment — rather than copying a foreign template.** Government apps (like the "One Jamaica" consolidation) and diaspora-facing cultural content are the other two success pockets. What the region does not produce is globally owned *platforms* end to end.

## The Money Story Behind the Apps

Two economic numbers anchor the region's digital role:

- **Remittances** are enormous relative to the region's size — online flows in the broader Latin America & Caribbean channel run to tens of billions of dollars a year — and they are increasingly digitised, which is exactly why payment apps are a viable local production niche: they sit on a real, large, ongoing money flow.
- **The mobile economy itself** is material: mobile technologies contributed about **$21 billion to the Caribbean economy in 2025**, roughly **8.1% of GDP**, forecast to reach about **$23 billion by 2030**. The pie is real — and the question is whether the region's creators and developers capture a local slice of it.

## Common Misconceptions

### "Because we use digital apps heavily, we're a digital producer"
Heavy usage is consumption. "Producer" means creating or owning the value layer, not being a power user of someone else's product.

### "The Caribbean produces nothing digital"
It produces strong local services — banking, payments, government — and diaspora-facing cultural content. What it lacks is ownership of global *platforms*, which is a very different (and reasonable) thing to say.

### "The diaspora is a separate, unrelated market"
It is an integrated audience and funding engine — in addition to being an audience at home, it is a remittance and demand source that shapes both consumption and local production.

### "High app usage proves high local production"
Usage is consumption of foreign platforms. Local production must be measured separately, and it is concentrated, not broad.

### "Caribbean fintech is a copy of Silicon Valley"
It grew out of local needs — inclusion, remittances, a large unbanked population — and is tailored to a prepaid, mobile-first reality. It is a local product for local conditions, not an import.

## Summary

The Caribbean is a mobile-first, messaging-led consumer of globally produced apps, and a concentrated, niche producer of its own. Its consumption happens through foreign-owned channels (WhatsApp, Facebook, TikTok, YouTube); its production wins in payments, government, and diaspora culture — where local need, local rails and true localisation align. The **local content gap** — rich consumption, thin local supply — is the central opportunity. The **diaspora** multiplies both audience and demand. Remittances and a ~$21 billion mobile economy make the money real. And the rule that separates success from imitation: meet a real local need, respect the region's device and data reality, and localise properly — because using the world's apps does not make you the economy that makes them.
