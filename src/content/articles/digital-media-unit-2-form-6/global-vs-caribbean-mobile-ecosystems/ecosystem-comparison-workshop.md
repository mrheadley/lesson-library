# Ecosystem Comparison Workshop: How to Run a Fair Comparison

## The Workshop's Job

The preceding lessons built the theory: the four-layer framework (infrastructure, adoption, production, policy), the two rules of fairness (standardise the metric, match the country type), and opportunity analysis (match a gap with an asset). This is the lesson where you *do* it — where theory becomes a method you can actually run, in a workshop, by hand, and defend aloud.

The whole activity reduces to one sentence: **pick two countries, force their data onto the same definitions, compare them across the four layers, and end with one defensible opportunity—not a ranking.**

## The Protocol: Five Steps

A workshop without a protocol drifts. Fix it in five steps:

**Step 1 — Choose the pair deliberately.** The countries are not interchangeable; the choice *is* the lesson. Good pairs teach a contrast:

- **Puerto Rico vs. Haiti** — the spread/inequality story.
- **Jamaica vs. Barbados** — similar markets, different 5G timing.
- **Dominican Republic vs. Trinidad** — scale vs. penetration.
- **Bahamas vs. Cayman** — the role of high income and archipelago.

Match the type, or deliberately choose the mismatch and say why.

**Step 2 — Fix the framework axes.** Define what you will compare: standardised penetration, smartphone rate, network generation/5G status, data affordability, operator structure, production depth (local services/fintech/content), policy/regulator context. Write these down before gathering data so you don't cherry-pick favourable numbers later.

**Step 3 — Standardise the metrics.** Force both countries onto the same definitions: same measure (subscriptions vs. people vs. adults), same year, same source type (ITU, GSMA, or a national regulator — applied consistently). This step is where "fair" is actually won or lost.

**Step 4 — Gather, then analyse.** Pull the data, fill the four layers, and *read* them: which layer is each country's bottleneck? Where is one strong and the other weak? Describe the portrait — do not collapse it into a single score.

**Step 5 — Produce one opportunity statement.** End with a sentence of the form: *"Country X has gap G (from the data) and asset A (from the data), therefore a product/policy for [need] makes sense."* The opportunity must be defensible from both sides of the comparison.

## The Two Rules That Keep It Honest

Two guard-rails govern the whole exercise, worth restating because they are the difference between analysis and nonsense:

**Rule 1 — Standardise the metric.** "Penetration," "smartphone rate" and "coverage" are meaningless until defined. Mixing Jamaica's SIM-count with Haiti's adult-user count is not a comparison; it is a category error.

**Rule 2 — Match the country type (or justify it).** Comparing a tiny rich tourist island to a large poor populous one measures *difference in type*, not "better." That contrast is legitimate if you say it is the point; it is illegitimate if you present it as a neutral ranking.

## Reading the Comparison as a Portrait, Not a Score

Here is the skill a good comparison develops: the ability to give a *layered* description instead of a one-word verdict. For each country you should be able to say all of the following without contradiction:

- *Infrastructure layer:* coverage and 5G maturity (e.g. Puerto Rico island-wide 5G; Haiti 3G/limited 4G).
- *Adoption layer:* standardised penetration and smartphone rate (e.g. Cayman 143%/high; Haiti ~60%/~45%).
- *Production layer:* how much the local market makes (e.g. Jamaica's fintech and government apps; elsewhere thin).
- *Policy layer:* the regulatory posture (e.g. Bahamas awaiting spectrum; DR auctioning 5G in 2025).

The willingness to hold all four in view at once — to say "A leads on infrastructure but lags on adoption" — is the analytical maturity the workshop is there to build. A ranking throws that away.

## The Opportunity Statement: The Payoff

The workshop does not end with a table; it ends with **opportunity analysis** — matching the gap you found with the asset the market has. The form:

> *"Jamaica has a production gap (thin local original apps) and a strong payment/fintech asset (a mature mobile-money base); therefore a locally-served regional payment or diaspora-facing service is a credible opportunity."*

Or:

> *"Haiti has an adoption gap driven by cost and a flood of remittances; therefore a lite/USSD, agent-driven remittance and payment service targeting the device floor is a credible opportunity."*

The statement is only as good as its two legs: if you cannot point to the **gap** in the data *and* the **asset** in the data, the opportunity collapses back into an unsupported idea. That insistence on evidence is the whole value of the exercise.

## Common Misconceptions

### "The workshop just needs numbers"
The *analysis* — fair comparison plus an opportunity conclusion — is the point. Raw numbers without standardisation are misleading.

### "Any two countries will do"
Choice matters. Pick pairs that teach a specific contrast or that you can actually get consistent data for.

### "One country emerges the winner"
Each country is strong on some layers and weak on others. "Better" is relative to the layer and the purpose, not a single verdict.

### "Coverage is the final word"
The other layers — affordability, production, policy — often weigh more for a developer deciding what to build than raw coverage does.

## Summary

This workshop turns the four-layer framework into a repeatable, defensible method: choose the pair deliberately, fix the axes, standardise the metrics, read the layers as a portrait, and end with a single evidence-backed opportunity statement. Two rules govern fairness — standardise the metric and match (or justify) the country type — and the analysis rejects rankings in favour of layered portraits that can hold contradictions ("strong on infrastructure, weak on adoption"). The payoff is opportunity analysis: matching a gap with an asset into a defensible one-liner. Students who complete this workshop leave able to look at any two Caribbean markets and produce a fair comparison *and* a concrete opportunity — the exact skill later lessons will turn into an actual app concept.
