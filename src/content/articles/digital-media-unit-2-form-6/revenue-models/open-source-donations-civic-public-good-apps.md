# Open Source, Donations & Civic/Public Good Apps: The Purpose-Driven End

## The Question No Other Revenue Lesson Asks

Every previous revenue model answered *who pays and how* — through customers. But a significant, legitimate slice of digital media answers a different question:

> What if the point isn't profit at all — but mission, trust, and public good?

This lesson covers the **mission-funded** end of the revenue spectrum: open-source software, donation-supported apps, and civic/public-good apps. Here the "revenue" is **sustainability funding** (grants, donations, sponsorships, foundations) rather than customer transactions, and success is measured in **service and impact**, not revenue per user.

It is the deliberate counterweight to the previous profit-focused lessons — a self-aware reminder that maximising consumer monetisation is *not* the only end of digital media.

## Open Source ≠ Free-to-Use ≠ No Money

The biggest misunderstanding to clear up first: **open source does not mean the developers earn nothing.**

- **Open source** means the source code is openly published under a licence (MIT/Apache — permissive; or GPL — copyleft) allowing anyone to use and modify it.
- But someone still has to **maintain** it, and that work has to be funded.

The chronic problem is **sustainability**: open-source software is the backbone of the digital world (browsers, operating systems, frameworks, AI tooling all ride on it), yet its maintainers are chronically under-funded. So the real "revenue model" question for OSS is: *how do we keep this alive?*

## The Four Ways Open Source Gets Paid

1. **Commercial sponsorships** — companies/industries pay for features or credibility (sometimes via tax incentives).
2. **Donations** — small recurring gifts through platforms like GitHub Sponsors or Open Collective, from individuals who use the software.
3. **Open-core / dual licensing** — a free open-source **core** plus **paid enterprise/certified features** (this is a *hybrid* of the earlier models and one of the most sustainable).
4. **Grants & foundations** — institutional funding from bodies like NLNet, the Sovereign Tech Fund, or ecosystem-specific grant programs (2025–26 data shows the sums growing as governments wake up to the value of funding open infrastructure).

**Worked example — the open-core sort:** take an app's feature list. Separate the free open-source core from the paid enterprise features, and explain how the paid features fund the whole project while the open code builds trust and adoption. The open core is the growth engine; the paid tier keeps it alive.

## Donations: Voluntary, Trust-Based, Post-Value

Some apps carry a **"donate / sponsor / pay what you can"** route instead of a price. The key psychological difference from a paywall:

- A paywall is **pre-value, enforced** — pay before you get the thing.
- A donate button is **post-value, voluntary** — you give *after* you have felt the worth, because you want to support it.

Because it is voluntary, a donation model runs on **trust**: users donate when they believe the project is genuinely useful and genuinely funded for the mission, not for exploitation. It suits small, passionate, community apps far better than a hard price.

## Civic and Public Good Apps: Two Shapes

Civic/public-good apps come in two forms, and knowing the distinction matters:

1. **Government/public-sector apps** — service delivery (health, transport, education, election info), built and funded by the state as a public service, at no direct user cost.
2. **Civic-tech NGOs/charities** — mission-driven collectives (e.g. education-on-existential-risk organisations, health-app NGOs) funded through **membership, grants, and donations** rather than app-store monetisation.

The distinguishing feature of both: they often **reject attention-maximising monetisation** (ads, subscriptions) *by design*, because their product is the public good itself. A health-information app that spams you with ads is failing its mission, not succeeding at it.

**Worked example — the civic design brief:** design a small public-good app for your community (a transport timetable, a school timetable, a clinic locator) and decide *who pays for it* (the state? an NGO?) and why it must not rely on ad-supported attention-maximising — connecting this lesson to your ethics work.

## Who Really Pays for What You Use?

A powerful exercise for seeing the invisible economy: take the digital tools you use daily — a browser, a video player, an OS, a maps service, a messaging app — and trace **who actually funds each**. Some are ad-supported, some subscription, some open source + sponsors, some state-funded. "Free to you" tells you nothing about the funding mechanism; "free" is about your cost, not the money behind it.

## Regional Reality

In the Caribbean, civic and public-good digital services are often **state- or NGO-funded**, and regional payment infrastructure (only about a third of people with mobile money) and public-sector capacity shape how that funding flows. A government health/transport app uses the state budget; an NGO's is grant/donation-funded — neither leans on in-app ads or subscriptions as its primary funding. For a genuinely public-good local app, a mission-funded path is not a "no model" — it is a deliberate, defensible model.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"Open source means the developers earn nothing."** OSS is funded through sponsorships, dual-licensing, support, and grants — sustainability funding is a real revenue model.
- **"Free-to-use = ad-supported."** Free can also be open-source, state-funded, or donation-supported; "free" is about the user's cost, not the funding mechanism.
- **"Civic apps are just governments copying commercial apps."** Civic apps often reject attention-maximising monetisation by design, funded by state budget or grants — a deliberate ethical choice.
- **"Donations are unreliable, so a bad model."** For mission projects, recurring donations + grants + sponsorships are a durable, trust-based mix measured by impact, not revenue.
- **"Every app should scale revenue."** Purpose-driven apps optimise reach and impact, not revenue per user.

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Open source / licence | Source published under MIT/Apache/GPL allowing use/modification |
| Open core | Free open-source core + paid enterprise features (a hybrid) |
| Donation / sponsor model | Voluntary, trust-based "pay what you can" after value is delivered |
| Civic / public-good app | State- or NGO-funded app for health, transport, education, etc. |
| Sustainability funding | Non-customer income (sponsorship, grants, donations) keeping a mission alive |
| Digital commons | Shared, openly-accessible software/data everyone uses, no owner-for-profit |

## Summary

Not every app exists to maximise profit. Open source, donation, and civic/public-good models monetise mission, trust, and public good through **sustainability funding** — sponsorships, dual-licensing/open-core, donations, and grants — rather than customer transactions. Distinctively, these apps may **reject** attention-maximising ads/subscriptions by design because the product is the public good. Ask "who really pays for what you use?" to surface the invisible economy, remember "free" says nothing about funding mechanism, and understand that for a genuinely public-good local app, a mission-funded path is a deliberate, defensible choice — regionally, often state- or NGO-funded.
