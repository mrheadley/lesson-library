# Presenting and Evaluating the Marketing Plan: Defence, Feedback, and Reflection

## What This Session Is For

This is the **execution & evaluation** session of the Section 7 capstone. Your group finishes and **presents** its marketing plan to the class, and the class uses **structured peer evaluation** to judge it fairly. It is the public, assessed moment of the whole course's "accountability as representation" — the plan becomes something you stand behind with evidence, and something others interrogate.

The organising tension: **execution discipline** (standing behind your decisions with evidence) **and evaluation honesty** (interrogating others' decisions fairly, without crediting the last thing or dismissing the rest). The discipline of correlation-vs-causation from the case-study lessons applies here in full.

## What the Rubric Polices

Evaluation does not reward fashion — it tests **logic**. A fair evaluation rubric checks, across every plan:

- **Audience fit** — is the target audience named and the value real?
- **Distribution/channel logic** — did they choose channels because they reach that audience, or by habit?
- **Measurement** — is there a real funnel and metric plan, not "see how it does"?
- **Revenue-model justification** — model chosen by fit with evidence, not a name dropped?
- **Economics** — is LTV vs CAC examined?
- **Regional realism** — does it survive Caribbean reality (data, mobile money, purchasing power)?
- **Presentation quality** — is the defence clear?

These dimensions are the ones we've built all session: the rubric is the Section 7 journey turned into a score.

## Strong vs Weak Feedback

The difference is **mechanism** vs **outcome**:

- **Weak:** "The plan is good because a subscription worked in another case." (crediting an outcome)
- **Strong:** "This model will fit here because the app is used daily by a pay-willing niche, and the plan quotes the churn economics." (naming why)

Give feedback that names *mechanisms*, not outcomes — and make it **actionable**: one specific, concrete change, not generic praise or criticism. "The model needs a regional-pricing justification" beats "good job."

## The Through-Line to Check in Every Plan

Across every plan you evaluate, run the **economic through-line** from the case-study lesson:

- **Fit** — did the model match usage/audience/value?
- **LTV vs CAC** — is the economics examined and believable?
- **Retention** — did the plan show how users stay long enough to pay or engage?

A plan's sections may all be filled, but if acquisition, retention, and monetisation don't line up into a sustainable whole, it fails the through-line — that is the deepest check the rubric polices.

## Common Cross-Plan Weaknesses to Watch

When you evaluate, look (fairly) for the patterns the marketing-case lesson flagged as failure:

- A fuzzy or unfocused target audience.
- Unexamined LTV vs CAC.
- A revenue model named but not justified.
- Channels picked by habit without reason.
- No measurement plan.
- A globally-styled plan ignoring regional data/payment reality.

Flagging one of these *specifically and helpfully* is exactly the point of peer evaluation.

## Peer + Self + Teacher Triangulation

Full-circle feedback comes from three directions:

1. **Peer evaluation** — scores and comments on the fixed rubric.
2. **Self-reflection** — what your own team would change. This turns the presentation into learning, not just performance.
3. **Teacher review** — the final judgement.

Between presentations, each team notes its own **single weakest section and one concrete fix** — applying the feedback immediately rather than waiting.

## Regional Reality Is Fair Game

Part of fair evaluation is the regional lens. A plan that ignores Caribbean data budgets, purchasing power, or WhatsApp-led distribution is weaker *on its own terms* — and a strong plan explicitly refits to local reality. Flag this kindly and specifically; it is one of the most valuable corrections a peer can give.

## Reflection: From Capstone to Course

Close by distilling the shared **lessons for taking an app to market** across all the plans — the class-level takeaways that point at the final summative and the full-course integration. Writing "what I'd change about our plan and what I learned from another's" makes the presentation a growth moment, not a grade event.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"Presenting just means showing slides."** The value is the *defence* — standing behind decisions with evidence, which is what evaluation tests.
- **"Peer feedback is just kind words or harsh criticism."** A structured rubric + one specific actionable point keeps it fair and usable.
- **"A stylish plan is a good plan."** The rubric polices logic; style without reasoning misses.
- **"Evaluation is the teacher's job only."** Peer + self + teacher triangulation gives full-circle learning.
- **"Once we present, we're done."** Reflection (what would you change?) is part of the capstone and feeds the summative and course integration.

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Plan presentation | Publicly walking audience, channel, revenue, economics with evidence and defence |
| Peer evaluation / structured feedback | Grading another group against a fixed rubric with specific, actionable points |
| Self-reflection | The team's honest "what we'd change" after presenting |
| Execution vs evaluation honesty | Standing behind decisions with evidence while fairly interrogating others' |
| Rubric | Fixed evaluation dimensions keeping feedback fair and specific |

## Summary

Present your marketing plan as a defence, and evaluate the class's plans fairly on the fixed rubric (audience fit, channels, measurement, model justification, economics, regional realism, quality). Give **mechanism-level, actionable** feedback, not vague praise, and check every plan's economic through-line (fit, LTV>CAC, retention). Combine peer + self + teacher review, apply the regional lens, and finish by reflecting on what your team would change and what the class learned about taking an app to market. The capstone closes one journey — and points at the course's final assessment.
