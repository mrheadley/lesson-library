# Content Layout Workshop

## From Theory to Practice

This lesson is a hands-on workshop where you apply the full content layout planning process — from concept map to sitemap — on a realistic project brief. You'll work in teams to produce a complete planning document for a multi-page website.

The goal is not to produce a perfect structure. The goal is to **practise the process**: moving from a content brief through concept mapping, navigational charting, sitemap building, and documentation — and to receive peer feedback that improves your thinking.

## Workshop Structure

### Phase 1: Brief and Setup (5 minutes)

The teacher will provide a project brief — a description of what the website needs to accomplish, who it's for, and what content it includes. Read the brief carefully and identify:

- **Purpose**: What is this site for?
- **Audience**: Who will use it?
- **Key tasks**: What are the 2–3 most important things users need to do?
- **Content list**: What content needs to exist?

Choose your tools: paper and markers for a quick, collaborative approach, or a digital tool (Miro, draw.io, Whimsical) for a more polished result. For this workshop, speed and collaboration matter more than polish — paper works well.

### Phase 2: Build (25 minutes)

Work through the planning process in order:

**Step 1 — Concept Map (8 minutes)**
Start with the site's core purpose as the central node. Branch outward to major content areas. Label relationships. Look for cross-links, orphan content, and redundancies. Don't overthink it — get ideas on paper quickly.

**Step 2 — Sitemap (8 minutes)**
Translate the concept map into a page hierarchy. Decide which concepts become pages, how they nest, and which get primary navigation. Assign page templates (article, gallery, form, landing page, etc.).

**Step 3 — Documentation (9 minutes)**
Write a brief planning document that captures your sitemap, page-level notes, and navigation model. Record the *rationale* behind key decisions — why you chose this structure over alternatives.

As you work, the teacher will circulate and ask probing questions:
- "Where would a user go to find [specific content]?"
- "Why is this in the global nav instead of the footer?"
- "What happens if we add 20 more blog posts — does the structure still work?"

### Phase 3: Share-Out (5 minutes)

Each team presents their sitemap in **60 seconds**. The class gives feedback using the "Star and Wish" format:
- **Star**: One strength of the structure (what works well)

## Workshop Checklist

Before presenting, make sure your planning document includes:

- [ ] **Concept map** with labelled relationships
- [ ] **Sitemap** with clear hierarchy (2–3 levels)
- [ ] **Page-level notes** describing what each page contains
- [ ] **Page templates** assigned to each page type
- [ ] **Navigation model** specifying which nav components appear where
- [ ] **Rationale** for key structural decisions
- [ ] **Utility pages** (contact, privacy policy, 404) accounted for

## Common Pitfalls to Watch For

### Over-nesting
If your sitemap has more than 3 levels of hierarchy, consider whether the structure can be flattened. Deep nesting makes content hard to find and navigation confusing.

### Forgetting utility pages
Every site needs a contact page, a privacy policy, and an error page. These are easy to forget when focusing on primary content, but they're essential.

### Jargon labels
If your page names sound like they came from an internal meeting ("Resources Hub," "Knowledge Base," "LMS Portal"), simplify them. Users should be able to guess what a page contains from its name.

### Skipping the concept map
Some teams jump straight to the sitemap. This often misses important relationships that the concept map would have revealed — cross-links between sections, content that belongs together but was separated, or orphan content that needs a home.

## What Good Looks Like

A strong planning document:
- Has a clear, logical hierarchy that a new visitor could navigate intuitively
- Uses labels that match user language, not internal jargon
- Includes all content from the brief, with nothing missing or orphaned
- Accounts for growth — new content can be added without restructuring
- Records the reasoning behind decisions, not just the decisions themselves

A weak planning document:
- Has too many levels of nesting
- Uses vague or jargon-heavy labels
- Forgets utility pages
- Doesn't explain why the structure is the way it is

## After the Workshop

The planning document you produce today becomes the foundation for everything that follows — wireframes, design, and development. In later lessons, you'll learn how to turn this structure into actual web pages using HTML and CSS. For now, the important thing is that the structure makes sense, serves the user, and is documented well enough for someone else to build from.

## Key Takeaways

- Site planning is an **iterative, collaborative process** — not a single-pass activity
- The process moves from **abstract to concrete**: concept map → sitemap → documentation
- **Peer feedback** reveals blind spots that individual thinking misses
- The goal is a **documented, reasoned structure** that someone else can build from
