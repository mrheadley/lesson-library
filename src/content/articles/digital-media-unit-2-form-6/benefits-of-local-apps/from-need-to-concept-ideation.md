# From Need to Concept: Ideation Done Rigorously

## Start with the Need, Not the Solution

The most common mistake in app design is beginning with a solution: "a fitness app," "a social app for x." Strong concepts begin the other way around — **with a need**: a real problem a real person has. Only after the need is clear do you derive the app that meets it.

The discipline is to state the need with enough specificity that it points toward a design:

- **Weak need:** "Seniors need help."
- **Strong need:** "Senior citizens in my parish can't get weather and health alerts they can understand and act on."

The strong version names the **who** (seniors in a specific parish), **where** (a specific place), and **pain** (inaccessible alerts). From that, an app concept emerges almost on its own; from the weak version, nothing does.

## Framing the Need: The "How Might We" Prompt

A rigorous way to phrase a need is the **"How Might We"** prompt, which keeps the statement user-centred and actionable:

> *"How might we help [user] do [task] given [constraint]?"*

Example: *"How might we help a smallholder farmer get today's market price and weather forecast on a basic phone without a data plan?"* The prompt packs in the user, the job and the constraint — and because the constraint is included, it forces design that respects reality from the start. This is a genuinely useful, exam-friendly habit.

## Ideation: Diverge, Then Converge

Ideation is the controlled process of generating and refining ideas, and it runs in two phases:

**Diverge** — generate many concepts freely, no self-censoring. Wild, overlapping, or silly ideas are welcome; quantity first. The region's need-categories from earlier lessons supply the raw material:

- **Culture & heritage** (Lesson 109).
- **News & information** (Lesson 110).
- **Services & payments** (Lesson 111).
- **Regional needs** — disaster, health, education, agriculture (Lesson 112).
- **Universal needs, locally served** (Lesson 113).

**Converge** — filter and combine. Now apply the testable checklist from Lesson 114 as an explicit filter:

- Does it respect the **constraint floor** (device, data, power)?
- Is it **localised** (language, currency, rails, culture)?
- Does it **earn trust** (security, transparency)?
- Is it **in scope** (buildable)?
- Does it **reuse a universal core** and localise the surface?

This is where the checklist earns its keep: it rejects concepts that can't survive the region's reality and sharpens the ones that can.

## Scope: The MVP Discipline

A winning concept is disciplined about **scope**. The discipline is the **MVP — the minimum viable product**: the smallest usable version that tests the core idea.

Ask the two scoping questions:

- **Who exactly is the target user?** (One named user type beats "everyone.")
- **What is the single core job?** (One screen that does the essential thing well beats five screens poorly.)

A good test: can you describe the concept as "the single most important screen and what it does"? If the MVP is that screen, the scope is right. Roughly, "one screen that alerts a parish senior to storm warnings and tells them what to do" is an MVP; "a full citizen-services platform" is a project that will never ship.

## Classify Each Candidate: Local, Regional, or Universal?

Run each concept through the **local/regional/universal filter** (from Lessons 109–113) — because the classification shapes the design:

- **Local** — serves one community's specific need (a parish notice board) → design for deep localisation, small audience.
- **Regional** — serves a need shared across islands (disaster alerts, revision for a shared syllabus) → design for the universal-core-plus-localised-surface model and the fragmentation realities of Lesson 107.
- **Universal, locally served** — a need everyone has, won on local context (payments L111, culture L109) → design to compete on the localised layer the globals ignore.

Being able to *defend the classification* is part of good ideation — it proves you understand who the product is for and how it should scale.

## Constraints Are the Filter, Not the Limit

Here is the reframe that makes ideation productive: the region's constraints (Lesson 106) are not obstacles to be endured — they are the **filter** that turns fantasy into a workable regional product. A concept that "would work if everyone had a flagship phone and unlimited data" is not a concept; it is a daydream. A concept that works on a budget phone, on prepaid data, offline-scarred and trusted is a *real* opportunity — because that is exactly the reality most users live in, and exactly the reality global imports ignore.

## Common Misconceptions

### "Start with a cool app idea"
Strong concepts start from a real, well-framed need. "Cool features" without a user and a pain go nowhere.

### "More features = better concept"
The MVP discipline is a scope-defining virtue. Small, focused concepts are buildable and testable; sprawling ones are not.

### "Constraints are boring barriers"
They are the filter that turns fantasy into a workable regional product. Constraint-aware concepts are the real ones.

### "Ideation is just random brainstorming"
It is a structured diverge-then-converge process with explicit framing and filtering.

### "Any need can be met by a global app"
Many local and regional needs are precisely where local concepts win — that is the opportunity.

## Summary

Ideation begins with a **need**, not a solution, phrased tightly enough to point at a design — the "How Might We" prompt is a rigorous way to do it. Then **diverge** (generate freely from the need-categories of the earlier lessons) and **converge** (filter through the Lesson 114 checklist). Discipline the result with **MVP scope** — a named user, a single core job, one essential screen. **Classify** each concept as local, regional or universal to shape how it should be designed and scaled. And hold the reframe throughout: constraints are the filter, not the limit — the concept that works on the region's real devices and connections is the one that is genuinely worth building. This is exactly the method you will put into practice in the design workshop next.
