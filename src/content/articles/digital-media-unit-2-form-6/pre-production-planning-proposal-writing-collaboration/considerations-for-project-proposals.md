# Pre-Production: Considerations for Project Proposals (Writing, Sources of Funding, Budgeting)

## Introduction

You've defined the problem. You've researched the audience. You've brainstormed solutions. Now comes the moment where you have to convince someone else—your teacher, a potential funder, an external stakeholder—that your project deserves to exist.

That's what a proposal does. It's a persuasive argument that your project solves a real problem, is achievable with available resources, and will deliver value to its intended audience.

In this lesson, we'll learn how to write compelling proposals, identify appropriate funding sources, and create realistic budgets that demonstrate both ambition and fiscal responsibility.

---

## Part 1: Anatomy of a Strong Proposal

### The Core Structure

A complete proposal answers these questions:

1. **What problem are you solving?** (Problem statement)
2. **Who has this problem?** (Target audience)
3. **What will you do about it?** (Solution/approach)
4. **How will you do it?** (Methodology)
5. **What will you produce?** (Deliverables)
6. **How long will it take?** (Timeline)
7. **What will it cost?** (Budget)
8. **How will you know if you succeeded?** (Evaluation)
9. **Why should we fund YOU?** (Team qualification)

### The Problem Statement

This is where proposals live or die. A weak problem statement:

> "Many students need help with studying."

A strong problem statement:

> "Form 5 and 6 students at our school struggle to access CAPE exam schedules, registration deadlines, and study resources because the current system relies on paper notices and a school website that is difficult to navigate on mobile devices. This leads to missed deadlines, duplicated efforts, and unnecessary stress during an already demanding period."

**The strong version is:**
- **Specific** — Identifies exactly who's affected
- **Quantifiable** — Can be measured ("missed deadlines")
- **Root-cause focused** — Identifies the actual problem, not just symptoms
- **Contextual** — Explains why this matters in this setting

### Writing Style for Proposals

**Do:**
- Use active voice ("We will create" not "The project will be created")
- Be specific and concrete ("3 weeks" not "a few weeks")
- Support claims with evidence ("Based on interviews with 15 Form 6 students...")
- Focus on benefits, not just features
- Be honest about constraints and challenges

**Don't:**
- Use jargon without explanation
- Overpromise or inflate impact
- Be vague about timelines or costs
- Ignore potential problems or risks

## Part 2: The Budget Section

### Why Budgets Matter

A budget demonstrates:
- **Realism** — You've researched actual costs, not guessed
- **Fiscal responsibility** — You can manage money thoughtfully
- **Value awareness** — You understand the relationship between resources and outcomes
- **Credibility** — A thoughtful budget signals professional maturity

### Budget Categories

**1. Personnel (if applicable)**
- Student stipends (if receiving payment)
- Facilitators, mentors, expert consultants
- Project manager time (even if volunteer)

**2. Hardware**
- Computers, cameras, microphones
- Lighting equipment
- Storage devices (hard drives, cards)
- Mobile devices for testing
- Accessories (stands, cables, batteries)

**3. Software**
- Licenses (Adobe Creative Cloud, Figma, etc.)
- Consider free/open-source alternatives:
  - GIMP (image editing) instead of Photoshop
  - Audacity (audio) instead of Audition
  - DaVinci Resolve (video) instead of Premiere
  - Blender (3D) instead of Maya/3DS Max
  - Figma (design) — has free tier

**4. Materials**
- Printed handouts
- Physical props for prototyping
- Workshop supplies

**5. Infrastructure**
- Internet/cloud storage costs
- Domain names
- Web hosting
- API costs

**6. Distribution**
- App store fees (Google Play: one-time $25; Apple: $99/year)
- Publishing platform costs
- Marketing/launch expenses

**7. Contingency**
- 10-15% buffer for unexpected costs
- Accounts for mistakes, changes, emergencies

### Sample Budget Format

| Category | Item | Quantity | Unit Cost | Total |
|----------|------|----------|-----------|-------|
| Hardware | SD Card | 2 | $15 | $30 |
| Software | Adobe CC (annual) | 1 | $60 | $60 |
| Materials | Printed handouts | 50 | $0.10 | $5 |
| Contingency | — | — | 10% | $9.50 |
| **Total** | | | | **$104.50** |

## Part 3: Sources of Funding

### Internal Sources (School-Based)

**School/Department Budgets**
- Course materials funding
- Equipment loans (cameras, computers)
- Software licenses (school-wide)
- Print/copy facilities

**How to access:** Talk to your teacher, department head, or school administration. Present a clear proposal with budget.

**Student Activity Funds**
- School clubs and organizations
- Fundraising proceeds
- Alumni donations

**How to access:** Apply through your student government or relevant administrator.

### External Sources

**Government Programs**
- Ministry of Education grants
- Science and technology initiatives
- Cultural/historical preservation funds
- Youth entrepreneurship programs

**International Development**
- UNESCO funding for cultural projects
- UNDP initiatives in education and technology
- Regional development banks (Caribbean Development Bank)
- British Council, French Embassy cultural funds

**Corporate Sponsorship**
- Local businesses (banks, telecom, retail)
- Tech companies (Google.org, Microsoft Philanthropies)
- Foundations (Rockefeller, Ford Foundation)
- Industry associations (advertising, media, tech)

**Crowdfunding**
- Kickstarter, Indiegogo (for public-facing projects)
- GoFundMe (for community/individual needs)
- Targeted campaigns to family, friends, community

### Grant Writing Tips

1. **Read eligibility requirements carefully** — Don't apply if you don't qualify
2. **Follow instructions exactly** — Missing sections disqualify applications
3. **Tell a compelling story** — Connect your project to human impact
4. **Demonstrate community support** — Letters of support, partnerships
5. **Be realistic about what you can achieve** — Credibility matters
6. **Budget accurately** — Funders notice inflated or vague budgets

## Part 4: Budget Realism

### Common Budget Mistakes

**Underestimating costs:**
- Forgetting software licenses
- Ignoring consumables (printing, storage)
- Not accounting for time (your own time has value)
- Missing hidden costs (taxes, shipping, exchange rates)

**Overestimating needs:**
- Asking for top-of-the-line equipment when mid-range works
- Including everything "just in case"
- Double-counting items

**Budget bloat:**
- Padding budgets to seem more serious
- Including items that don't directly serve the project

### Budget Justification

Every significant budget item should have a brief justification:

| Item | Cost | Justification |
|------|------|---------------|
| External microphone | $45 | Built-in phone mic produces audible background noise; this shotgun mic provides clean audio for podcast recordings |
| Cloud storage (1 year) | $25 | Required for sharing large video files between team members; alternative (USB drives) creates version control problems |

## Part 5: The Evaluation Section

Funders want to know: Will this project actually work?

### Defining Success Metrics

**Quantitative measures:**
- Number of users/participants
- Engagement metrics (time on site, return visits)
- Completion rates (for courses/training)
- Revenue/sustainability metrics

**Qualitative measures:**
- User satisfaction surveys
- Testimonials and stories
- Accessibility improvements
- Community impact

### Evaluation Timeline

| Phase | Evaluation Activity | Success Criteria |
|-------|-------------------|------------------|
| Mid-project | User testing session | 80% of test users can complete core task |
| Launch | Analytics review | 100 unique users in first week |
| 1 month post | Survey | 70% satisfaction rating |

## Key Takeaways

1. **Proposals are persuasive arguments** — Not just formal documents; they must convince stakeholders that your project deserves support.

2. **Problem statements must be specific** — Quantifiable, root-cause focused, contextual. "Many students struggle" is weak; specific scope with evidence is strong.

3. **Budgets demonstrate professionalism** — Realistic, researched, justified budgets signal maturity and credibility.

4. **Funding sources are diverse** — Internal (school), government, corporate, crowdfunding. Match source to project type and eligibility.

5. **Evaluation matters** — Funders want to know their investment produced value. Define success metrics before you start, not after.

6. **Free tools exist** — Before budgeting for expensive software, research open-source alternatives.
