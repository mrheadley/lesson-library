# Summative Assessment 1 Review: Pre-Production — Scheduling, Collaboration & Proposals

## Overview

This summative assessment covers three interconnected topics from the pre-production phase of digital media production: **production scheduling and work plans** (Lesson 9), **collaboration tools and workflows** (Lesson 10), and **project proposals, funding, and budgeting** (Lesson 11). Together, these topics answer the question: *How do you take a project from an idea on paper to a realistic, funded, team-executed plan?*

You are expected to understand not just the individual concepts, but how they connect — how a work plan feeds into a proposal, how collaboration tools support a schedule, and how a budget reflects the scope you've planned. The assessment will test your ability to apply these concepts, not just recall definitions.

---

## Key Concepts Review

### Production Scheduling and Work Plans

**Task breakdown** is the starting point. A large project gets decomposed into manageable work packages — chunks of 5 to 15 hours for a student project. Each task needs a specific name, a defined outcome ("done" looks like...), an owner, a due date, and required resources. Vague task names like "do design work" are a red flag; "Create homepage wireframes in Figma" is what examiners want to see.

**Dependencies** determine task order. Some work can't start until other work finishes — you can't animate characters before the storyboard is approved, and you can't edit video before you've captured footage. Dependencies create a chain, and the longest chain through your project is the **critical path**. Any delay on the critical path delays the entire project. Not every task is equally critical; identifying the critical path tells you where to focus monitoring energy.

**Timeline tools** include Gantt charts (horizontal bars showing task duration and overlap), the Critical Path Method (finding the longest dependent sequence), Kanban boards (To Do → In Progress → Review → Done), and Agile sprints (fixed-length iterations with committed deliverables). A Gantt chart is the most common visual for communicating timelines to stakeholders; Kanban is better for day-to-day team workflow.

**Buffers** exist because task estimation is unreliable — students consistently underestimate by 40 to 60 percent. The three types are task buffers (extra time within a task), project buffers (extra time at the end), and critical path buffer (extra time for dependencies on the critical path). For student projects, adding 50 percent to every estimate is a reasonable rule of thumb. Buffers are not "extra" time; they are essential time for reality.

**Work plans** go beyond task lists. A RACI matrix documents who is Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, and Informed for each deliverable. Version control in work plans answers: what gets saved, in what format, who has access, how are changes tracked, and what happens when conflicts arise.

### Collaboration Tools and Workflows

**The tool stack** spans several categories: file storage and sharing (Google Drive, Dropbox), real-time document editing (Google Docs, Figma, Miro), communication (Slack, Teams, Discord), video conferencing (Google Meet, Zoom), project management (Trello, Asana, Notion), design and prototyping (Figma), and code collaboration (GitHub). The key principle is choosing tools for purpose, not popularity — match the tool to the collaboration need.

**Cloud-based collaboration** enables real-time co-editing, commenting, version history, and fine-grained sharing permissions (viewer, commenter, editor, owner). Figma stands out for design teams because it runs in the browser, supports live multi-user editing, includes component libraries, and enables prototyping without code. Miro and Mural provide infinite whiteboard canvases for brainstorming, user journey mapping, and storyboarding.

**Collaboration norms** matter more than tools. Norms cover communication expectations (response times, which channel for what), file and version conventions (naming formats like YYYYMMDD_description_vX, folder structures, backup strategies), and feedback standards (specific and actionable, not "make it pop"). Tools enable collaboration but do not create it — people and processes create it.

**Workflow patterns** include parallel work (different members on different parts simultaneously), sequential handoff (work flows person to person), iterative refinement (group revisits shared output), and pair/group critique (regular feedback loops). Each pattern suits different project phases and team sizes.

**Common collaboration challenges** — "I didn't know that changed," "Two people edited the same thing," "Feedback got lost in chat," "We're not aligned on the vision," "Some team members aren't participating" — each have solutions rooted in norms and structure, not just better tools. Notifications, contextual commenting, regular vision check-ins, clear role definitions, and peer accountability address these problems.

### Project Proposals, Funding, and Budgeting

**Proposal structure** follows a predictable format: title page, executive summary, problem statement, objectives (SMART goals), background/rationale, methodology, deliverables, timeline, budget, evaluation, and conclusion. The problem statement is where proposals live or die — it must be specific, quantifiable, root-cause focused, and contextual. "Many students struggle" is weak; "Form 5 and 6 students miss CAPE registration deadlines because the current system relies on paper notices and a school website that is difficult to navigate on mobile" is strong.

**Writing style for proposals** uses active voice, specific and concrete language, evidence-based claims, benefit-oriented framing, and a clear call to action. Proposals are persuasive arguments, not just formal documents — form follows function. Every section should answer: why should someone fund or approve this?

**Budget categories** include personnel, hardware, software, materials, infrastructure, distribution, and contingency (10 to 15 percent buffer). Each significant item needs a justification explaining why it is necessary. Budget realism means researching actual costs, including hidden costs (taxes, shipping, consumables), avoiding budget bloat (padding to seem serious), and considering open-source alternatives before budgeting for expensive licenses. GIMP replaces Photoshop, Audacity replaces Audition, DaVinci Resolve replaces Premiere, Blender replaces Maya.

**Funding sources** split into internal (school budgets, departmental grants, student activity funds) and external (government grants, international development agencies like UNESCO and the Caribbean Development Bank, corporate sponsorship, foundations, crowdfunding, and industry partnerships). Each source has eligibility criteria, application deadlines, reporting requirements, and sometimes co-funding or matching requirements. The key is matching the source to the project type and eligibility.

**Evaluation** defines success before the project starts, not after. Quantitative measures include user numbers, engagement metrics, and completion rates. Qualitative measures include satisfaction surveys, testimonials, and community impact. An evaluation timeline maps evaluation activities to project phases with specific success criteria.

## Likely Trouble Spots

**Confusing scheduling tools with scheduling thinking.** A Gantt chart is a visualisation; the real work is task breakdown, dependency identification, and buffer allocation. Tools support the process — they do not replace it. If you're asked about scheduling, focus on the reasoning, not the software.

**Treating all tasks as equally critical.** Most projects have a clear critical path. "Everything is critical" means nothing is — examiners want to see you distinguish between tasks that directly delay the project and tasks that have float (flexibility in timing).

**Underestimating time without acknowledging it.** If your schedule shows a complex task taking one hour, it will likely take two to three. The assessment tests whether you know this and plan for it. Always mention buffers and explain why they exist.

**Listing collaboration tools without explaining norms.** Naming Trello, Figma, and Google Drive is easy. Explaining *why* you chose each tool, what norms govern their use, and how they fit into the workflow is what earns marks. The question is never "what tool?" — it's "how does this tool support this specific collaboration need?"

**Writing weak problem statements in proposals.** A vague problem statement ("students need better resources") collapses the entire proposal. Strong problem statements identify who is affected, what the specific pain point is, why it matters, and what constraints exist. This connects directly back to the persona-framing from Lesson 7.

**Forgetting budget justification.** A budget table without justification columns is just a shopping list. Funders want to know *why* each item is necessary, not just what it costs. An external microphone needs a justification; a contingency line needs an explanation of what it covers.

**Confusing funding with the project goal.** Funding enables the project — the project's actual value is the goal. A proposal that reads like a begging letter ("please give us money") will fail. A proposal that reads like a compelling solution to a real problem ("here is the problem, here is our solution, here is why it works, here is what it costs") will succeed.

**Skipping the evaluation section.** Funders want to know their investment produced value. Defining success metrics before the project starts — not after — demonstrates professional maturity. Include both quantitative and qualitative measures.

## Connections Between Topics

These three topics are not separate modules — they are stages of a single planning pipeline.

**Problem definition → Proposal writing.** The problem statement you craft at the beginning of a project (identifying who is affected, what the pain point is, why it matters) becomes the opening section of your proposal. A strong problem statement does double duty: it keeps your team aligned during production, and it convinces funders during proposal review.

**Brainstorming → Scheduling.** The ideas you generate and the directions you select determine the tasks, deliverables, and dependencies in your work plan. You cannot schedule a project you haven't scoped, and you cannot scope a project you haven't brainstormed. The number of concepts you explore and the complexity of your chosen direction directly affect your timeline.

**Scheduling → Budgeting.** Your timeline determines your resource needs. A longer project requires more software licenses, more hosting time, and potentially more personnel hours. The critical path reveals which tasks consume the most time and resources, which informs budget priorities. Contingency in time (buffers) maps directly to contingency in budget.

**Collaboration tools → Scheduling.** The tools you choose affect how efficiently your team executes the schedule. A Kanban board in Trello makes task progress visible; shared deadlines in Google Calendar keep everyone aligned; Figma's real-time editing reduces handoff delays. The right tools accelerate the schedule; the wrong tools create friction.

**Collaboration tools → Proposals.** Your proposal should demonstrate that your team can execute — and part of that demonstration is showing you have a collaboration infrastructure. Mentioning your tool stack (Google Drive for file management, Trello for task tracking, Figma for design) in the methodology section signals preparedness.

**Budgeting ↔ Everything.** The budget reflects every other decision. Task breakdown reveals what needs to be purchased. Collaboration tools may have subscription costs. Team size affects personnel costs. Timeline length affects hosting and license duration. The budget is the financial translation of your entire pre-production plan.

**Evaluation → Problem definition.** The evaluation metrics you define should directly measure whether you solved the problem you identified at the start. If your problem was "students miss CAPE registration deadlines," your evaluation should measure whether deadlines are no longer missed. This closes the loop.

## Final Preparation Tips

1. **Review the connections, not just the definitions.** The assessment rewards understanding how scheduling, collaboration, and proposals fit together. If you can explain *why* a budget line item exists in terms of the work plan, you understand the material.

2. **Practice writing strong problem statements.** Take three different scenarios and write specific, quantifiable, root-cause-focused problem statements for each. Compare them to weak versions. This is a skill that transfers across all three topics.

3. **Build a sample work plan from scratch.** Pick a simple digital media project (a 2-minute explainer video, a school event website, a photo essay). Break it into tasks, identify dependencies, find the critical path, add buffers, and assign roles. This exercises scheduling, collaboration, and feeds naturally into a proposal.

4. **Write a one-page proposal for your sample project.** Include the problem statement, objectives, methodology, deliverables, timeline (referencing your work plan), budget (with justifications), and evaluation criteria. This ties all three topics together in one deliverable.

5. **Know the difference between tools and processes.** If you're asked about collaboration, discuss norms, workflows, and communication patterns — then mention tools as implementations of those processes. Never lead with tool names.

6. **Understand budget realism.** Research actual costs for software, hardware, and services. Know what open-source alternatives exist and when they are appropriate. A budget with "$60 for Adobe Creative Cloud" next to "GIMP (free alternative available)" shows you understand both the cost landscape and the options.

7. **Connect evaluation back to your problem statement.** If you cannot explain how your success metrics measure whether you solved the problem, your evaluation section is incomplete. The strongest proposals create a clear line from problem → solution → evaluation.
