# Lesson 30 — The Sea Monster, the Statues, and the Discus
### Form 1 English B · Greek Myths · Unit 3 · *Perseus* (part 3)

---

## The story so far

Perseus holds the Gorgon's head in a bag, invisible and winged and shield-bright — a hero who
just beat the unbeatable monster by changing the rules. He is flying home, victorious, carrying
a weapon too dangerous to show.

As he flies, the story sets one more trap for him — and, in traffic he cannot see, for everyone
he loves.

---

## 1. A country in terror

Below him lies a kingdom in despair. A **sea serpent** is eating its people:

> It devoured swimmers, snatched fishermen off the beach, and even dragged itself ashore to
> grab people off the town streets and carry them out to sea.

The king asks his fortune-teller what to do. The answer comes down to a sacrifice: the gods are
angry, and the only offering that will do is the king's **most precious possession** — his own
daughter, **Andromeda**.

Chained to a ledge on the cliff, left as bait for the serpent, Andromeda catches sight of her
supposed fiancé, **Phineas**, among the crowd — and begs him to save her. His reply is a classic
study in cowardice dressed up as duty:

> "They say nothing else will work. Do you want us all to be eaten in our beds, you selfish
> girl? You ought to be proud to do this for your country."

And he hurries away with his fingers in his ears.

---

## 2. The rescue from the sky

Perseus, flying overhead, hears a sound:

> a small, piping cry, like a seagull's call.

It is Andromeda — and it is enough. "Like a hawk," the text says, "Perseus swooped out of the
sky." The fight is not clean or easy:

- The serpent rears and flings him against the cliff.
- His shield, helmet, and bag fall from him.
- He draws his sword anyway, leaps between the princess and the monster's open mouth, and
  plunges his blade into the beast's shoulder.
- The serpent's tail knocks him into the sea; he clings to the only thing he can — **the
  monster's slimy neck** — and stabs again and again until the beast rolls over, dead.

When Perseus drags himself wearily out of the water, the people on the clifftops cheer. The king
is overwhelmed with gratitude. And Perseus — who beat Medusa with clever tricks — asks for the
one reward a hero's heart can settle on:

> "She is so beautiful. The best reward I could ever think of is to have Andromeda for my
> wife."

---

## 3. The wedding interrupted — fifty spears

The wedding begins. And in crashes Phineas, the man who let Andromeda be chained to a rock,
with an army of fifty spearmen:

> "How dare he steal the girl promised to me in marriage!"

Andromeda answers him with the sharpest truth in the myth:

> "You were quite happy for me to be eaten by the sea monster! Perseus risked his life to
> save mine."

Fifty spears raise. Perseus has one sword and no chance in a fair fight — so he reaches for the
bag. He roars a warning first:

> "All those who love Perseus, shut your eyes!"

And drags out the Gorgon's head.

> Fifty spears clattered to the floor, dropped by hands that had turned to stone. His attackers
> stood about like ugly statues, doomed to stand for ever with one arm lifted.

Fifty wedding-crashers, frozen mid-throw in his wedding hall. The lesson fires the exact image
from Lesson 29 home: the still-dangerous head, used exactly once, at exactly the right moment.

---

## 4. Home — and the five hundred statues

Perseus and Andromeda sail home. But King Polydectes, who sent him to die, has moved against
his mother: he has surrounded Danaë with five hundred soldiers and declared her wedding day.

Perseus walks into the middle of it. He tells the king he brought the Gorgon's head. Polydectes
laughs — "Nobody could do that!" — and boasts that he will marry Danaë anyway.

So Perseus shows him. And the lesson repeats two numbers you should keep separate:

- **50** — the wedding attackers frozen in the wedding hall (Lesson 30, part 3).
- **500** — Polydectes' soldiers, who now stare at the head and "are staring still."

> Polydectes and his soldiers all stared at the thing in his hand. They stared and stared, and
> are staring still, still standing as they stood in that moment when the Gorgon Medusa's head
> turned them all to stone.

The tyrant who plotted Perseus's death ends as a permanent display. And the kind fisherman
Dictys — first to shelter the baby in the chest — becomes king in his place, and marries Danaë
at last.

---

## 5. The discus — and the prophecy the king forgot

Now the myth delivers its deepest card. At the wedding celebrations, a sports day is held:
running, jumping, wrestling, javelin — and **discus-throwing**. Perseus enters.

> He threw the brass discus much higher and harder and further than any other competitor. But
> the wind caught it and spun it into the watching crowd.

An old man is struck and killed. Perseus tears his hair in anguish. And then the hardest line
in the whole story:

> "Who is he? Doesn't anybody know?"
>
> "It is your grandfather," said Danaë gently. "Poor man. It was his fate that you should kill
> him. Don't blame yourself. Nobody can cheat Fate."

Read that again. **King Acrisius — the man who locked a baby in a chest and pushed it out to
sea to escape a prophecy — was killed after all, by the same grandson, by pure accident, at a
celebration.** Every attempt to dodge Fate just rearranged the furniture on the path to it.
The prophecy said the boy would kill him; and the boy, grown and unknowing, did.

Perseus refuses to rule Acrisius's kingdom — too sad to take a crown bought with his own
grandfather's life. Instead he travels to an empty land with Andromeda and founds what the text
calls "the noblest kingdom in all the world."

---

## 6. The image to keep in your pocket

Two pictures, one lesson: **fifty statues in a wedding hall and five hundred in a palace court,
all made by the same head in the same bag — plus one brass discus flying on the wind, proving
that no chest, lock, or sea can stop what Fate has decided.**

The hero of this myth wins by cleverness. But the *story* is won by the same law Midas met in
Unit 2: no matter how you plan, **what is written lands anyway.**

---

### Key terms for this lesson
- **Sacrifice** — giving up something precious to calm angry gods; Andromeda is chained as one.
- **Sea serpent** — the monster terrorising the kingdom; slain by Perseus.
- **Andromeda** — the princess Perseus rescues and marries (also a constellation name — a Greek
  myth footprint in the sky).
- **Discus** — the heavy bronze disc of Greek sport; the instrument of the prophecy.
- **Petrify (recap)** — turn to stone, the fate of the 50 wedding attackers and the 500 soldiers.
- **Fate (recap)** — the certainty that even a king's best plan cannot escape.

### Things to remember
- Andromeda is chained as a sacrifice to a sea serpent; her fiancé Phineas refuses to save her.
- Perseus fights the serpent in the water and slays it; he asks for Andromeda's hand.
- Phineas crashes the wedding with 50 spearmen — the bagged head turns them all to stone.
- At home, Polydectes is about to force Danaë to marry him; Perseus shows the head to him and
  his 500 soldiers — all frozen forever.
- Perseus kills his grandfather Acrisius **by accident** with a mis-thrown discus — fulfilling
  the prophecy from Lesson 28 that the king tried to escape.
- Moral taken by the course: "Nobody can cheat Fate."
