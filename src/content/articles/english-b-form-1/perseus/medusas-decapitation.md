# Lesson 29 — The Gorgon's Head
### Form 1 English B · Greek Myths · Unit 3 · *Perseus* (part 2)

---

## The story so far

Polydectes has lost patience. He cannot make Danaë his wife while her son stands guard, so he
gives Perseus a dare built to be a death sentence: **fetch the head of the Gorgon Medusa.**

Perseus, brave and a little reckless, accepts at once — which is exactly what the king hoped.

But the gods are watching. And the gods *like* brave boys.

---

## 1. The dare

Polydectes declares his challenge before his court:

> "I dare you to fetch me the head of the Gorgon Medusa."

"Gorgon" is a word the lesson must unpack: Medusa, the slides tell you, **was once a beautiful
woman** who made the mistake of boasting — in the gods' hearing — that not even a goddess was
more beautiful than she. For that vanity, she was transformed into a monster: **snakes for
hair**, glaring eyes, and a gaze that **turns anyone who looks at her to stone**.

Way too dangerous to kill, the king figures. So, a perfect trap.

> Perseus fell right into the king's trap. "I leave at once!" he cried.

The courtiers cheer. Polydectes thinks, *He'll die, of course.* And up on Mount Olympus, the
gods are delighted.

> "What a brave boy that Perseus is. He deserves our help."

---

## 2. Divine equipment — the four gifts

The gods choose an interesting way to help. They do **not** fly down and hold Perseus's hand.
Instead, Perseus finds, one by one, four objects "lying in the road" — as if planted for him:

- **Hermes' feathered sandals** — buckle them on and you walk on air, up over the treetops.
- **Athene's bright shield** — polished mirror-bright; a weapon *and* a mirror.
- **Pluto's helmet of invisibility** — put it on and even the helmet itself disappears.
- **A thick bag** from Pluto — for carrying Medusa's head *safely*.

Notice who gives what, because the gifts are personal: the messenger god (who flies) lends
wings; the goddess of wisdom (who plans) lends a mirror; the god of the Underworld (who knows
about the dead) lends a bag for a severed head. **Each god lends the tool that matches their
own skill.**

And Zeus? Zeus says the most telling line of all:

> "I shall watch, but not help. Perseus must match his brave words with brave deeds."

The king of the gods wants to *test* the hero, not simply save him. That is a Unit 3 idea in
one sentence: **the gods help — but they expect you to earn the win.**

---

## 3. The secret of the mirror shield — and the trick that delivers it

Perseus realizes the problem before the fight: he cannot fight a monster he may not even
**look at**. One glance and he is a statue.

Then the sun flashes on Athene's bright shield, and the idea arrives:

> Holding the shield up in front of his face, he flew backwards. For the first time, he was
> able to look at the monster, **reflected** in the shiny metal.

That backwards flight is the lesson's brilliant core. The shield wins the battle *before*
Perseus swings the sword, because it lets him **see the enemy without meeting its eyes**.

---

## 4. The Grey Sisters and their single eye

To find Medusa at all, Perseus needs directions no map can give. The only creatures who know
where she makes her lair are the three **Grey Sisters** — and they are as strange as the
Gorgon herself:

> Between the three of them, they had only one grey eye.

One eye. One rotten tooth. They take turns, squabbling over whose turn it is — and when one
sister drops the eye to pass it, invisible Perseus snatches it out of the air.

Their wailing is comic, but their choice is real: they beg for the eye back, and Perseus makes
a fair, monstrous exchange:

> "…as soon as you tell me how to find the Gorgon Medusa."

They give him the directions (three days east, two days north, one day west, an hour south).
Perseus tosses the eye back. But listen to their whispered threat as he leaves:

> "One glimpse of the Gorgon Medusa will turn that thief to stone!"

Even the sisters, beat, are banking on the monster.

---

## 5. The beheading that must not look at its target

Medusa's island lies below Perseus "like a single grey eye in the face of the sea." He does not
search by sight — one sight kills. Instead he finds her **by the sound of her hair**: the
writhing, hissing snakes tell him where she is. Around her sleep the proof of her power: a
hundred petrified figures, "turned to stone in the very moment that they had first glimpsed
Medusa."

Then the careful, almost surgical kill:

- Medusa is **asleep** — though her snakes never sleep. They hiss and jab at the air, sensing
  something near, but they cannot see him.
- Invisible, Perseus kicks the snakes aside.
- He swings his sword in a great arc.

> Medusa's hideous head fell to the ground with a thud. Only the snakes did not die.

He puts the head **very cautiously** into the bag. Heroic win — but note what the bag tells us:
the head is a *weapon still loaded*. The story is not done with it. Any future trouble, Perseus
can dip into that bag.

---

## 6. The image to keep in your pocket

Keep this one: **a hero flying backwards into a fight, watching his enemy in a mirror, so that
the monster's own power can never reach him.**

It is the whole myth's trick in a single picture: the smartest way to fight something unbeatable
is to **change the rules** — use a mirror, not a stare; use the bag, not the bare head.

---

### Key terms for this lesson
- **Gorgon** — a monstrous female creature; Medusa is the Gorgon of this story.
- **Medusa** — once a vain beauty, transformed into a snake-haired monster whose gaze turns
  people to stone.
- **Decapitation** — cutting off the head; the act Perseus performs.
- **Grey Sisters (Graeae)** — three sisters sharing one eye (and one tooth) between them.
- **Petrify** — to turn to stone.
- **Divine gifts** — Hermes' sandals, Athene's shield, Pluto's helmet and bag — each lent by
  the god whose skill matches the tool.

### Things to remember
- Polydectes dares Perseus to fetch Medusa's head, planning his death.
- The gods help by leaving tools in the road: flying sandals, a mirror-bright shield, an
  invisibility helmet, and a strong bag.
- Zeus refuses to help directly: "Perseus must match his brave words with brave deeds."
- The mirror shield lets him approach backwards — see her without meeting her gaze.
- He steals the Grey Sisters' single eye and trades it for directions.
- Asleep Medusa is beheaded; her head goes into the bag — still dangerous.
