# Lesson 25 — The Music Contest & The Ass's Ears
### Form 1 English B · Greek Myths · Unit 2 · *King Midas* (part 1)

---

## The story so far

The unit has run a gauntlet of flawed behaviour and its costs. One story family remains — and it
belongs to a king. A king who is about to be handed the power to turn everything to gold, and
who we will meet here in two instalments: the bad judgement, and the golden consequence.

---

## 1. Midas — the king who is "almost as stupid as he is greedy"

Let the story introduce its main character the way it chooses:

> King Midas — almost as **stupid** as he was **greedy**.

Two faults, one sentence, and the whole myth hangs from them: Midas is not especially evil. He
is foolish and grasping — the kind of man whose own good fortune is the greatest danger in his
life.

---

## 2. The great music contest

One day, a musical contest is held — and it is the strangest judging panel in ancient history:
**two gods compete, and a king decides.**

The two competitors couldn't be more different:

- **Apollo** — god of the sun, of music, of poetry — plays his **golden lyre**, and the music is
  so lovely it stills the river, hushes the animals, and makes even the sternest listeners wipe
  their eyes.
- **Pan** — god of the wild, of shepherds and their reed-pipes — plays his **pipes**, and his
  tune is... simpler. Earthy. Rustic.

Here is the comparison the story makes, in a phrase you'll want to remember:

> Judging these two is like comparing **a golden trumpet with a tin whistle.**

---

## 3. The biased judge

There is one fact we know, and one fact Midas forgets.

The fact we know: **Pan is Midas's friend.** The king and the woodland god have shared many a
feast. Midas adores him.

The fact Midas forgets: **a judge does not judge friends — a judge judges the music.**

Apollo finishes. The crowd is spellbound. Pan finishes. The crowd politely claps.

And King Midas — sweaty, loyal, glowing with friendship — thunders his verdict:

> **"Pan was the better! Oh, definitely!"**

For one ecstatic moment it is genuine. Then he looks at the faces around him. Even his own
courtiers look embarrassed. He has just declared a tin whistle better than a golden trumpet —
**because the tin-whistle player was his friend.**

---

## 4. The punishment — ass's ears

Apollo is not amused. In fact, the story says he is "scarlet with rage."

> He points his magic finger at the king — the same gesture of divine fury you have seen in
> this unit more than once — and pronounces the curse:
>
> "Since you would not judge between the true and the false, **you shall grow ass's ears.**"

The king's ears swell — grow long, furry, and unmistakably **donkey's ears**.

Read the curse with a mythology-student's eye. This is not punishment for having a favourite.
It is punishment for **lying about the truth because of friendship** — for refusing to "judge
between the true and the false." Apollo's problem is not Midas's taste. It is Midas's honesty.

The word in the pack, for the ears: **ass's ears** — old English for donkey's ears.

---

## 5. The tall hat — a kingdom of one secret

Now the story turns comic, and the comedy is doing serious work.

Midas cannot walk around wearing giant donkey ears. So he does what kings do: he changes the
fashion.

> He hides his ears under a **tall hat** — and suddenly everyone in the kingdom wants a tall hat.

The entire country is now wearing headgear invented to hide one king's shame. No one dares
laugh. No one dares admit what the hats are for. A whole kingdom is quietly in costume for a
single secret — and everyone except the king knows it.

Hold that picture: the more absurd the hat, the more the secret strains at the seams. The myth
is preparing its punchline.

---

## 6. The barber who couldn't keep it in

A king must have his hair cut. And his barber, on trimming the towering hat, sees the ears.

> The barber is sworn to secrecy — but bubbles to tell someone.

He carries the secret until he thinks he will burst. So the barber does the oldest thing in
the world:

> He digs a **little hole** in the riverbank, where the reeds grow, **whispers the secret into
> the hole**, covers it over, and goes home.

The secret is safe. The hole is safe. The reeds above the hole, however, are about to do what
reeds do.

---

## 7. The reeds take over

Time passes. The reeds grow tall over the buried whisper. And when the wind moves through them —

> — the reeds bend and whisper to the whole world:

> **"KING MIDAS HAS LONG DONKEY'S EARS!"**

Everyone hears it at last. The kingdom gasps. The king's secret is out — carried on the breath
of every passing breeze.

Here is the myth's natural-explanation gift, tucked inside the comedy:

> The sound the wind makes **blowing through the reeds** sounds like whispering voices — and so
> the story says the reeds are the ones who told the secrets of kings.

---

## 8. A difference in retellings — and why it matters

Before Lesson 26, one note about the story's cast — because different retellings of this myth
disagree about *who* granted Midas his wish, and your course book handles that disagreement with
a smile.

In some tellings — including the course's main story text — the magical helper who grants
Midas his golden wish is a **centaur**: half-man, half-horse.

In other tellings — including the course's slides — the same helper appears as **"the lost
satyr"**: half-man, half-goat.

There is no "right" version to memorise. Myths were told aloud, from memory, for centuries —
and storytellers changed the details. That is what *oral tradition* means: the same story, told
twice, never quite the same. What never changes is the *moral*: a wish for greed goes wrong,
and a king learns it the hard way (Lesson 26).

For the test, know **both** creatures by name and one fact about each:

- **Centaur** — half-man, half-horse.
- **Satyr** — half-man, half-goat.

### Key terms for this lesson
- **Midas** — the king who is "almost as stupid as he is greedy."
- **Apollo** — god of the sun, music, and poetry; plays the golden lyre.
- **Pan** — god of the wild and shepherds; plays the reed-pipes; Midas's friend.
- **Ass's (donkey's) ears** — the punishment for judging by friendship instead of truth.
- **Satire / biased judge** — friendship overriding honesty; the lesson's hinge.
- **Centaur** — half-man, half-horse (the course's story text).
- **Satyr** — half-man, half-goat (the course's slides say "the lost satyr").
- **Reeds** — the plants that "keep the secret," then whisper it to the wind.

### Things to remember
- Midas is "almost as stupid as he was greedy."
- Apollo's lyre ("golden trumpet") vs. Pan's pipes ("tin whistle") — not a fair match.
- Midas judges for friendship: "Pan was the better! Oh, definitely!"
- Apollo curses him with ass's ears for refusing to judge truth from falsehood.
- The tall-hat fashion hides the shame; the barber buries the secret in a riverside hole.
- Reeds whisper "King Midas has long donkey's ears!" — natural explanation for wind in the
  reeds.
- Centaur vs. satyr: both appear in different retellings; know both as names + halves.
