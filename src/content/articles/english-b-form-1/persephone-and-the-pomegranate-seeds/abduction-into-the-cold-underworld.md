# Lesson 14 — The Abduction into the Cold Underworld
### Form 1 English B · Greek Myths · Unit 2 · *Persephone and the Pomegranate Seeds* (part 1)

---

## The story so far

Unit 1 gave you the tools: myths explain the world, teach lessons, and preserve a culture's
beliefs. You met Zeus, king of the gods, and the three realms he shares with his brothers —
Hades rules the Underworld, Poseidon the sea, and Zeus the sky.

Now Unit 2 begins — and it starts under the ground.

---

## 1. The two worlds of Persephone

The ancient Greeks believed the world had halves people should never mix: the **bright world
above** and the **cold world below**.

- **Above:** sunlight, rivers, meadows, spring flowers. This is where **Persephone** lives —
  the daughter of **Demeter**, goddess of the harvest (the one who makes crops grow).
- **Below:** the **Underworld**, kingdom of **Pluto** — dark, silent, and cold. (Pluto is the
  Roman form of the Greek name **Hades**, and you will often see both.)

The story begins with a simple problem: Pluto, ruler of the Underworld, is **lonely**. He has
no queen. He lives in a palace of shadows, and nobody comes to visit.

---

## 2. The job Demeter does for the world

Before the story gets dramatic, meet Demeter properly. Look at her job — because everything
that goes wrong in this myth happens when she stops doing it:

**Demeter is the goddess of agriculture.** She is the one who makes the corn grow, the
fields ripen, and the trees fruit. When she is happy, the world is green and full.

The ancient Greeks knew the world depends on her. If Demeter turned her face away, nothing
would grow. That fact — her power over the harvest — is the secret engine of the whole story.

---

## 3. The abduction

One day Persephone is gathering flowers in the meadows — so lovely it seems "a pity to pick
them." She reaches for a perfect blossom at the edge of the field.

That is the moment Pluto chooses.

He comes up out of the earth in his dark chariot, scoops her up, and carries her down with him
"to the shadowy palpitating realm of the dead." She cries out — but nobody hears.

Details the text wants you to notice:

- He rides a **chariot** — the same chariot that, in the explanation-layer, is said to make
  the ground tremble as it passes (Lesson 15's surprise about earthquakes).
- As he carries her off, he throws a **belt** into the water of the Styx — the river of the
  dead — which is a trick detail to understand later (Lesson 15).
- She is taken to the **Underworld palace**, where Pluto begins to behave like a hopeful host.

Persephone has vanished. And no god can tell her mother what happened — because the only
witnesses are the Sun, who sees everything, and the pitiless dead themselves.

---

## 4. Demeter's world-ending grief

What does Demeter do when she realises her daughter is gone?

She goes mad with grief. She had assumed Persephone was safe, gathering flowers. When she is
not there, Demeter cannot sleep, cannot eat — she even forgets the gifts of the gods.

And here is the myth's engine, clicking into place:

> **Nature reacts to Demeter's mood.**

The moment Demeter turns her face away from the world she usually tends, **everything stops
growing**. The flowers wither. The fields go bare. The land turns cold and grey. Her grief is
not just personal — it is the first winter the world has ever known.

The people who wrote this story lived in a world where winter really did mean their harvest
could fail — and famine could follow. This is the myth explaining their deepest fear with a
single image: *the earth fails because the goddess of growth is grieving.*

---

## 5. A closed heaven and a locked earth

Notice what happens *upstairs* while Demeter weeps:

- The **gods** (who like things tidy and profitable) grow annoyed. A frozen earth means no
  crops, no flowers, and worst of all for them — **no more offerings and sacrifices** from
  humans who have nothing left to give.
- Zeus cannot simply order his brother to give the girl back — the Underworld has its own
  rules.
- And Demeter, in her grief, refuses to do her job at all. She stays shut in her temple, and
  the world stays cold.

The stage is set for a god-sized argument about a daughter — one that will end in a deal about
seasons, a rule about pomegranate seeds, and a surprising change of heart (all in Lessons
15–16).

---

## 6. The image to keep in your pocket

If you remember one picture from this lesson, remember this: **a mother stopped mid-word,
reaching for a flower that was never a flower at all** — the moment the bright world became
the world of grief, and winter was born.

---

### Key terms for this lesson
- **Persephone** — daughter of Demeter, goddess of spring; taken to the Underworld.
- **Demeter** — goddess of agriculture, the harvest, and growth; Persephone's mother.
- **Pluto / Hades** — ruler of the Underworld; the god who takes Persephone.
- **Underworld** — the dark, cold kingdom of the dead, ruled by Pluto.
- **Abduction** — being taken away by force.

### Things to remember
- The world has two halves: bright above, cold below.
- Demeter's mood controls the harvest — her grief stops the world growing.
- Pluto acts from loneliness, not cruelty.
- The abduction is the myth's way of explaining why the world has a dead season.
