# Lesson 19 — The Prison of Crete
### Form 1 English B · Greek Myths · Unit 2 · *Daedalus and Icarus* (part 1)

---

## The story so far

Echo fades to a voice, Narcissus to a flower, and Hera to regret. The gods have been teaching
their lessons in a messy, human way. Now the course turns to a different kind of storyteller —
**not a god, but a man** — and his story carries the unit into the air.

---

## 1. Enter Daedalus — the greatest inventor there ever was

**Daedalus** is not a god, a king, or a hero with a sword. He is the ancient world's answer to a
modern inventor: he makes things, brilliant things, out of wood, wool, and ingenuity.

A man with a brain like Daedalus is a prize. And prizes attract kings who want to own them.

The king of the island of **Crete** is **Minos** — and his "reputation for wickedness" has
already "spread to every shore." Minos summons Daedalus and his young son **Icarus** to build
him a palace: towering, gorgeous, fit for a king who collects clever people.

Daedalus builds it. And then the real job begins.

---

## 2. The labyrinth in the cellars

Minos has another assignment for his pet inventor — this one under the palace. In the cellars,
Daedalus is ordered to construct a **labyrinth**: a maze of corridors so twisting and dark that
anyone who walks in will never find their way out again.

Even Daedalus, the man who built it, is not entirely comfortable. He asks what it is for.

Minos's reply is one of the coldest lines in the whole unit:

> "I pay you to build, not to ask questions."

And the truth of what the maze is for arrives soon enough. At the heart of the labyrinth Minos
houses a **monster** — called the **Minotaur** — and he feeds it on men and women.

Read that sentence again. The maze is a feeding pen. The inventor has built the monster's
cafeteria.

---

## 3. The murder-machine that came back to bite the builder

Here is the twist the story slides in: **the one person who knows the maze's secret is the man
who built it.**

Daedalus decides he wants to leave Crete and forget both maze and Minotaur. He asks to go.

Minos laughs — and refuses:

> "You are the only man who knows the secret of the maze and how to escape from it. I must
> keep you and Icarus here… just until you die."

Notice how friendly the prison is being offered: Minos adds that he has "plenty of work" for a
man as clever as Daedalus. The cruelty is wrapped in a smile — a trick you have seen before
(Zeus's gift-wrapped revenge in Unit 1).

Keep this pattern in your head: Daedalus **built** the labyrinth, and that is exactly why he
cannot leave it. His own cleverness is the trap.

---

## 4. The comfortable prison

This lesson's most important idea is hiding in plain sight. Minos does not throw Daedalus and
Icarus into a dungeon. He gives them **a beautiful tower**:

- **The rooms:** the highest tower of the palace, with views for miles.
- **The food:** "delectable food and expensive clothes."
- **The catch:** every night the door is **locked** from the outside, and a **guard stands by**.

The story's phrase for it:

> A comfortable prison, but it was a prison, even so.

That is the sentence to hold onto. Freedom is not the same as comfort. You can be well-fed,
well-warmed, and well-dressed — and still be a prisoner.

---

## 5. Icarus — the boy who cannot see the cage

Here is where the story becomes quietly painful. Daedalus seethes in the tower. Icarus? Icarus
is happy:

> "I like it here. The king gives us gold and this tall tower to live in."

He cannot see that they are trapped. He knows they left home, but blind, soft captivity feels
like a treat to him. Daedalus answers him with a wince of a lesson:

> "But to work for such a wicked man, Icarus! And to be prisoners all our days… We shan't stay.
> We shan't!"

Icarus thinks his father is being dramatic. He laughs:

> "How can anybody escape from an island? **Fly?**"

A laugh. A single word — *fly* — dropped as a joke.

And Daedalus says nothing in reply. Because Daedalus has just begun to think.

---

## 6. The birds and the seed — the first sketch of an idea

One detail the story plants for the next lesson: Daedalus has begun feeding the birds that come
to the tower window. He loves that they can come and go freely — that their wings make them
lords of the air, untouched by Minos's walls.

Every day the same birds. Every day a little more seed. Every day Daedalus watching them —
and, you might guess, counting their feathers.

Lesson 20 will answer the question the story has just asked: **what do you do when your jail is
an island and your window is full of birds?** You build wings.

---

### Key terms for this lesson
- **Daedalus** — the greatest inventor of the ancient world; builds the labyrinth.
- **Icarus** — Daedalus's young son; too cheerful to see the prison.
- **Minos** — the wicked king of Crete who traps them.
- **Labyrinth / maze** — the twisting, dark passages under the palace.
- **Minotaur** — the monster at the maze's heart, fed on people.
- **Comfortable prison** — comfort without freedom; delicious food plus a locked door.

### Things to remember
- Daedalus built the labyrinth — which is why Minos will never let him leave.
- The Minotaur is fed on men and women; the maze is a feeding pen.
- "A comfortable prison, but it was a prison, even so."
- Icarus cannot see the cage — "I like it here."
- "How can anybody escape from an island? Fly?" — the joke that becomes the plan.
- Daedalus watches the birds and begins to think.
