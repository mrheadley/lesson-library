# Lesson 20 — Designing the Wings
### Form 1 English B · Greek Myths · Unit 2 · *Daedalus and Icarus* (part 2)

---

## The story so far

Daedalus and Icarus are locked in a comfortable tower on the island of Crete. Minos needs the
inventor alive, because Daedalus is the only man who knows the labyrinth's secret. Icarus is
happy; Daedalus is plotting. And Daedalus has begun feeding the birds that visit the window.

Now the plot becomes an engineering project.

---

## 1. One feather at a time

Daedalus's escape plan starts as the quietest conspiracy in all of mythology — a year of tiny,
repeated acts:

- Every morning, Daedalus puts seed on the window sill.
- A bird lands; Daedalus **"begs it to spare one feather."**
- The bird obliges. The feather joins a growing bundle.
- Every night, by candlelight, Daedalus works on "his greatest invention of all."

A whole year goes by. Early mornings. Late nights. Feather after feather, sewn and smoothed.

Notice what the story is not doing: it is not handing the heroes a magical gift from a god.
Daedalus is **making his escape with his own hands** — a mortal doing what only an inventor
can do.

---

## 2. The materials: no magic required

Here is the sentence students always remember, because it sounds like a school project list:

> I sewed the feathers together with **strands of wool from my blanket**. … He melts a **candle**
> and daubs Icarus's shoulders with sticky wax. … He **sticks two of the wings to Icarus's
> shoulder-blades**.

Break that into parts:

| Material | What it is for |
| :-- | :-- |
| **Feathers** (from the birds) | The flight surface — what catches the air |
| **Blanket-wool** | The thread — sewing the feathers together |
| **Melting candle-wax** | The glue — fixing the wings to their shoulders |

Nothing supernatural. You have seen all three things: feathers, a blanket, a candle. The myth
tells you the wings are built from ordinary pieces of a prison — the clothing on their backs
and the wax from the candles they read by at night. That is what makes the tragedy (Lesson 21)
so much harder to take.

---

## 3. The warning — the most important lines in the story

Before the first flight, Daedalus gives Icarus the one instruction that will decide everything.
The story doubles the warning, twice:

> "Courage, son! Keep your arms out wide and fly close to me. **Above all — are you listening,
> Icarus? — don't fly too high! Don't fly too close to the sun!**"

There it is. The whole story in one sentence:

- Keep your arms out wide → flight is a skill.
- Fly close to me → stay near the one who knows.
- **Don't fly too high** → the rule.
- **Don't fly too close to the sun** → the reason (Lesson 21 explains the physics).

Warned once, Icarus nods. We will see if the warning holds.

---

## 4. The launch

Now the moment pulls the breath in. Icarus stands on the window ledge, looking down at the
drop —

- His **knees are knocking**.
- His **wings droop**.
- He is terrified.

His father gives him a nudge — and with a **crack**, the feathers behind Icarus's back **fill
with wind**, and he is flying.

The story wants you to feel the miracle: a boy who could not imagine leaving an island is
suddenly above the fields, seeing "the fields and streams as he had never seen them before."
He can turn by dipping one wing, then the other. Flight is real; freedom is real.

Over the sea, the seagulls come. They peck at him — they are his territory now, after all. So
Icarus flies **higher** than the gulls and crows at them: "You can't catch me!"

It is fun. It is triumphant. And inside that crowing sentence, the story plants exactly what
Lesson 21 is about to punish. Watch Icarus's eye drift from the wide world to the sun.

---

## 5. The engine of the tragedy, already started

Never let go of this pair of details:

1. **Daedalus warned clearly.** The instruction was specific, repeated, and practical: don't fly
   too high.
2. **Icarus is already violating it for fun.** He flies up to escape a few seagulls — and likes
   how it feels.

Readers of this myth are always told "Icarus disobeyed." Look closer: the disobedience begins
in this very lesson, hidden inside a joke at the seagulls. The fall (Lesson 21) will only be
the end of a climb he was warned not to begin.

---

### Key terms for this lesson
- **Ingenuity** — clever, practical invention (Daedalus's trademark).
- **Feathers** — the flight surface of the wings.
- **Wool / wax** — the "thread" (blanket wool) and "glue" (melted candle) of the construction.
- **Warning** — a clear, protective instruction; the story's flashpoint.
- **Glide / soar** — flight vocabulary for the miracle in the air.

### Things to remember
- The escape plan takes a year — one feather begged per bird, mornings and nights.
- The wings are real, built from feathers + blanket-wool + candle-wax — no magic.
- The warning, twice: "don't fly too high; don't fly too close to the sun."
- Icarus is terrified at launch — then thrilled.
- The disobedience begins when he flies above the seagulls and crows "You can't catch me!"
