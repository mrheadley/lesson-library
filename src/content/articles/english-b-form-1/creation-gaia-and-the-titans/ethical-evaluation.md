# Lesson 06 — Is There a "Good" God in Greek Myth? An Ethical Evaluation
### Form 1 English B · Greek Myths · Unit 1

---

## Open with a question that has no easy answer

In the Biblical account from Lesson 05, the creator is described as **fundamentally good** —
love, order, justice, mercy, all in one.

Now look at the Greek gods we have met. They created. They ruled. But are they *good*?

This lesson is the first time you will make a **moral judgement using evidence** — an *ethical
evaluation* means deciding whether a character's actions are right or wrong, using proof from the
story rather than just feelings. And today you start the first marked assignment of the term.

---

## 1. The evidence sheet: what Zeus has actually done

Judge Zeus — king of the gods — on what the story *shows* us. Collect the evidence before
forming your opinion. (Lesson 10 will turn this into a formal skill; today we just practise.)

| Piece of evidence | What it shows | Verdict it supports |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| He sent Prometheus and Epimetheus to make living creatures because the earth looked empty | He acts without a serious plan; he wants life on earth for his own reasons | Impulsive |
| He was angry that the mud-men "looked like us gods" | He feels threatened by the shape he allowed humans to have | Jealous |
| He chained Prometheus to a cliff and set eagles on him — forever — for the stolen fire | He punishes with total, never-ending cruelty | Vengeful, ruthless |
| He built a beautiful woman, gave her as a "reward," and buried a trap in the wedding gift | He plays a trick on humankind and grins about it | Childish, deceptive |
| He did not wipe humans out completely — and Hope survives the box | There is a limit; something good is left | Merciful |

Notice how the *same* god shows opposite evidence. Zeus is impulsive **and** patient with his
revenge, cruel **and** sparing, childish **and** godlike. That contradiction is the point — not
a mistake in the myth.

---

## 2. Comparing to a "fundamentally good" God

Now bring in the comparison frame from Lesson 05.

- The **monotheistic God** of Genesis is described in the course as good *in His nature* — what
  He does flows from who He is.
- **Zeus** is good and bad *by mood*. His nature is powerful and deeply imperfect.

So the Greek creator forces are not a "good God." They are something else the class should name
honestly: **very powerful forces with normal human-sized flaws.** The Greek myths never claim
their gods are examples of goodness. They claim their gods are *real enough to explain the world* —
and an explanation of storms, jealousy, love, and cruelty needs characters who have all of them.

---

## 3. Worked example: an evidence-based verdict

**Claim:** *Zeus shows mercy, but only a little — and only when it suits him.*

**Evidence 1:** The world is not destroyed; mankind continues even after receiving the gift of
fire and enduring Pandora.
**Evidence 2:** Hope survives the box — in the course's version it stays to comfort humans and
even visits Prometheus on his cliff.
**Evidence 3 (the other side):** He is the same Zeus who chains a fellow god to a cliff for the
theft of a tiny sliver of fire — a punishment that never ends because gods cannot die.

**Conclusion:** Zeus's mercy is real but small and selective. It makes him a complex
character — never simply "good," never purely evil.

That paragraph is exactly the kind of thinking this unit rewards: claims, then evidence, then a
balanced conclusion.

## 4. The homework you are starting today (A1)

**Assignment — "Comparison of Creation Stories."**

Your task is to write a short comparison of the Greek creation account and the Biblical Genesis
account, using the skills from Lessons 04–06:

- What are the **similarities**? (Both: a creator figure; humans made from the earth; a single
  event that brings suffering into a once-good world; hope or promise surviving afterwards.)
- What are the **differences**? (One God vs. a family of gods; goodness vs. flawed character;
  suffering sent from above vs. suffering chosen by humans.)
- **So what?** One closing sentence on what the contrast teaches about the two cultures.

**Assigned today (Lesson 06 / Week 2, Lesson 2.3).** The course assigns it at the end of the
ethical-evaluation lesson and it is **due in Lesson 08** (Week 3, Lesson 3.2). You have a full
week of working time. Use it.

## 5. What the ancients actually meant by all this

Keep the frame from Lessons 01 and 05: the Greek myths existed to **make sense of life**. A
world that has disease, cruelty, war, and hope at the same time needs a story that explains all
four with the same cast of characters.

The lesson hidden in Zeus's mixed record is the lesson of the whole unit:

> **Obey those in authority. Never lose hope, even when everything around you seems negative.
> Be careful of gifts from enemies. Curiosity can have dangerous consequences.**

The gods are not our moral examples. The *stories* are.

---

### Key terms for this lesson
- **Ethical evaluation** — judging whether behaviour is right or wrong using evidence, not
  feelings.
- **Vindictive** — wanting revenge and not letting go of it.
- **Merciful** — choosing not to punish, even when you could.
- **Moral** — to do with right and wrong.

### Things to remember
- Collect evidence before you make your claim — always.
- Zeus shows *both* cruelty and mercy; a balanced verdict uses both.
- Greek gods are not moral role models; the stories teach the morals.
