# Lesson 35 — The Gift That Was a Trap
### Form 1 English B · Greek Myths · Unit 3 · *The Wooden Horse of Troy*

---

## The story so far

For three lessons you've watched heroes armed with cleverness out-smart monsters and kings.
Now the unit's final hero-story raises the stakes: a whole *city* is the problem, and one of
history's cleverest tricks — with a name you still use today — is the answer.

The horse. The trap. And a warning that the Trojans refused to hear.

---

## 1. The woman who started it all

> There was once a woman who hatched from an egg, like a bird, and her name was Helen.

Helen becomes the most beautiful woman in the world — the wife of old King **Menelaus** of
Sparta. Every prince and duke wants her. And then **Paris, Prince of Troy**, comes to Sparta,
sees her, and "finds her too beautiful to forget." He steals her love and takes her to Troy —
the city proud of nothing more than its horses.

The course's word for what Paris did: **theft**. (An older, kinder telling says Helen went
with him willingly — but the class text teaches the theft, as you'll see in the review.)

Menelaus's grief turns to anger, and that anger is the biggest the Greek world ever raised:
he calls an army of **fifty thousand** men and the greatest heroes alive — **Achilles the
brave, Odysseus the cunning, Ajax the proud** — and a **thousand ships** sail to the tall
white walls of Troy.

---

## 2. Ten years that decided nothing

Here is the part every movie skips: the war lasts **ten years**.

> Week followed week, month followed month, and year followed year. The single combat was the
> same every time: sword against sword, chariot against chariot. It decided nothing.

By year ten, the glory is gone: **Achilles is dead** (the hero with the famous heel), **Ajax
lies in a flower-covered grave**, and **Paris too is dead**. The city is still standing. And
the prize at the centre of it all hasn't changed:

> Only Helen remained as lovely as ever — a precious prize locked inside Troy.

Ten years of deaths, one unresolved answer. This is where Lesson 33's shapeshaper comes back:
the Greeks have been trying strength. Strength did not finish the war. Now someone must propose
the other thing.

---

## 3. Odysseus's idea

> "I think I know how we can get inside the city of Troy."

**Odysseus, the cunning.** His name has meant "clever" since before the Trojan War. When some
heroes sneer it'll "never work" and others mutter it's "too dangerous," old King Menelaus
gives the order that starts the end of the war:

> "Do it, Odysseus."

For days, Trojans on the walls hear "sawing and hammering" across the plain. Then silence.

They look over the walls — and find the Greeks **gone**. Tents packed, ships launched, sailing
away. And left behind, alone on the beach:

> A huge wooden horse.

---

## 4. The trap the Trojans towed in themselves

Why would anyone pull a stranger's giant horse through their own city walls? Read what the
Trojans shout as they swarm over it:

> "A tribute to Troy! A tribute to the City of Horses!"

That's the trick's genius, and it's the detail the PPT adds: Troy's patron god is **Poseidon**,
whose sacred animal is the horse. The Trojans don't see a leftover weapon — they see a *divine
gift*. The city of horses receives a tribute horse. Of course they welcome it.

One old man throws a spear at the horse. The town laughs at the hollow thud. And the old man
says the most famous warning in the story:

> "**Beware of Greeks even when they give you presents!**"

They ignore him, celebrate, drink, dance — and **tow the horse in through the gates**.

---

## 5. Inside the midnight horse

What the Trojans don't know — what the whole war rested on:

> A dozen Greek soldiers crouched in the hollow body of the horse, pressed together, knee
> against knee, elbow against ear, gripping swords and holding their breath. One sneeze and
> they would be found out!

And then the story adds its quiet, tense beat: the Greek wife **Helen** — who knows Greek
tricks because she is Greek — looks out at the horse and thinks, *"This is a trick."* She
turns from the window, **"put a finger to her lips and sat quite still, waiting."**

She does not betray the plan. She waits — and, in the legend's kinder version, returns to
Menelaus in peace when the city falls.

The Trojans dance until they sleep. And that night:

> A secret door creaked open in the stomach of the horse. A knotted rope dropped down, and the
> dozen Greeks climbed out and opened the city gates.

Over the horizon — where they hid, not left — the thousand ships sail back. The Greeks storm
the open gates, and the ten-year siege ends **in a single night**: Troy is burned, its young
men killed, Helen seized, and the war that cost fifty thousand is over because one man built a
hollow present.

---

## 6. The idioms the horse gave the world

The outline wants three take-aways from this lesson, and two of them are language the world
still uses every day:

- **"Trojan Horse"** — a **deceptive trap that looks like a gift**. That's why a computer
  "Trojan" is a file or email that looks friendly and sneaks malware inside. The structure is
  identical: *looks helpful, opens a door.*
- **"Beware of Greeks bearing gifts"** — the proverbial warning: don't trust an enemy's
  apparent kindness. (The old man's line in the text is "Beware of Greeks even when they give
  you presents.")
- **A siege** — surrounding a fortified city with an army until it falls; "under siege" means
  trapped with supply lines cut.

And the moral the whole unit has been building toward, now applied to a war: **"Don't trust
your enemies."** Cleverness beat strength — the term's argument in one lesson — but at the
price of a burned city.

---

## 7. The image to keep in your pocket

A dark beach, a giant wooden horse, and a city that joyfully pulls its own doom through the
gates — while an old man's warning echoes over the party that nobody listens to.

It joins the unit's other warning images (Lesson 34's black sail, Lesson 31's empty wine
glass). The hero-era of the course ends not with a hero at all — but with a *trick*, and with
the word every Trojan wished they'd listened to: *trap*.

---

### Key terms for this lesson
- **Siege** — surrounding a city with an army until it falls.
- **City-state (polis)** — ancient Greece's self-ruled city-kingdom; Troy is one (its real site,
  Hisarlik in modern Turkey, was found by archaeologists).
- **Trojan Horse** — idiom for a deceptive trap; also the computer malware with the same
  structure.
- **"Beware of Greeks bearing gifts"** — the proverb; don't trust an enemy's kindness.
- **Odysseus** — King of Ithaca; the "cunning" hero whose plan ends the war.
- **Helen (of Troy)** — "the face that launched a thousand ships"; her seizure begins the war.

### Things to remember
- Paris steals Menelaus's wife Helen; the Greeks sail with a thousand ships to get her back.
- The siege lasts ten years and decides nothing; Achilles, Ajax and Paris are all dead by year ten.
- Odysseus builds a hollow wooden horse; a **dozen** soldiers hide inside.
- The Trojans see it as "a tribute to Troy! A tribute to the City of Horses!" — Poseidon's
  sacred animal — and tow it in.
- An old man warns, "Beware of Greeks even when they give you presents!" — nobody listens.
- A secret door opens at night; the fleet returns over the horizon; Troy falls in one night.
- Idioms: "Trojan Horse" and "Beware of Greeks bearing gifts"; the computing link is on the test.
