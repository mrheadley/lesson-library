# Lesson 09 — Hope in the Storm: The Box Opens
### Form 1 English B · Greek Myths · Unit 1 · *In the Beginning and Pandora's Box* (part 3)

---

## The lid comes off

Pandora turns the golden key. *BANG!* The lid flies back.

An icy, howling wind full of grit bursts out. It tears the curtains and stains them brown. And
after the wind come the *survivors of the story* — not people, not monsters exactly, but the
most frightening things in the Greek imagination. Each one names itself as it pours out:

> **Disease. Cruelty. Pain. Old Age. Disappointment. Hate. Jealousy. War. And Death.**

Count them. This is not a random freak-show list. Every single one is a real thing that happens
to human beings. That is the story's whole move: the world's worst pains, named one by one,
climbing out of a wedding gift.

The moment they escape, the consequences are immediate and physical. Flowers shrivel. Fruit on
the trees goes mouldy. The sky turns a filthy yellow. The town fills with the sound of crying.
From now on people will be unkind, afraid, greedy, unhappy — and one day they will all die and
go to live as ghosts in the cold, dark **Underworld**.

---

## 1. The scientific explanation hiding in the myth

The ancient Greeks did not have a psychology textbook or an epidemiology journal. They had
stories. So ask the myth's own question: **why do these nine evils exist?** And the story's
answer is: because they were once shut up and let loose.

Notice exactly what the story *doesn't* say. It does not say the world was always hard. It says
the world *became* hard — at a specific moment, by a specific choice, in a specific story. That
is an **origin story** — the same shape as Genesis (Lesson 05), answering the question *why is
life so difficult?* with a single, unforgettable moment.

---

## 2. The last thing left inside

Pandora slams the lid down. The storm is out, but something remains trapped in the chest — a
small voice, pleading:

> *"Let me go! If you shut me inside, that will be your worst mistake of all!"*

She refuses — she is not fooled twice. But the voice answers:

> *"I am Hope. Without me, the world won't be able to bear all the unhappiness you have turned
> loose!"*

So Pandora lifts the lid one more time. And a **white flicker, small as a butterfly**, flits
out. As it passes through the window, the watery sun comes out and shines on the wilted garden.

Here is the whole myth in miniature:

- The world now contains **all nine evils** — permanently.
- The world also contains **one small, fragile thing** — Hope — tiny enough to be blown about
  by the storm, and bright enough to survive it.

The fires are out. The butterfly remains.

---

## 3. Hope visits Prometheus (do not skip this scene)

The story does something deliberate now — it cuts to the cliff where Prometheus is chained.

He cannot help the "little mud-people" he made. He hears the crying everywhere. The world has
gone hard and cold, and the gods cannot die, so his chains will never break and his eagles never
stop.

Then — out of the corner of his eye — the small white flicker of light. Hope touches his bare
breast. And Prometheus, chained and eternal, feels *strength, a sort of courage*:

> *"No matter how bad things are today, tomorrow may be better. One day someone may come this way —
> take pity on me — break these chains — and set me free. One day!"*

The eagles peck at the fluttering light and miss. Hope blows on — "round the world like a single
tiny tongue of flame."

Do not miss the symmetry. The nine evils escaped and entered the whole world. Hope, the single
small light, visits *the one person who has been hurt the most* — and keeps him going. The
myth is not just "evil is everywhere." It is **"and yet — hope is everywhere too."**

---

## 4. One famous disagreement inside the myth itself

Here is a genuinely interesting puzzle — and the course wants you to notice it.

- The **core text we read** has Pandora *release* Hope: it flies out the window and travels the
  world, comforting even Prometheus.
- The **outline's summary wording** says Hope *remained inside* the box, to comfort humans and
  Prometheus on his cliff.

Two versions, both told for thousands of years. This is what **oral tradition** looks like:
stories change in the telling, and both tellings survive. For class quizzes and this unit's
test, answer with the outline's wording (**Hope remained inside to comfort humans**). For
debate and discussion, the *released* version gives you the butterfly-on-the-cliff imagery.

Same lesson either way: **hope survives the storm.** Whatever the mechanics, the meaning is the
same — and the meaning is the point.

---

## 5. What this myth explains — and what it teaches

**The explanation (natural-phenomena layer):**
- why animals, men, and women exist;
- how fire was discovered;
- **why evil exists in the world;**
- **why humans keep hoping despite non-stop suffering.**

**The lessons (moral layer) — the unit's core morals:**
1. **Obey those in authority** — the box is forbidden because it is forbidden.
2. **Curiosity can have dangerous consequences** — one peep opens a flood.
3. **Be careful of gifts from enemies** — the wedding present was a weapon.
4. **Never lose hope, even when everything seems negative** — the butterfly survives the storm.

---

## 6. A final image to keep

Every time the unit asks you about hope, picture the same picture: a white butterfly-like
flicker against a yellow, screaming sky. The evils are enormous and loud. Hope is tiny and
quiet. And it is the tiny quiet thing that outlasts the storm. From here on, whenever the unit
needs Hope, it will bring you back to this image.

---

### Key terms for this lesson
- **The nine evils** — Disease, Cruelty, Pain, Old Age, Disappointment, Hate, Jealousy, War, and
  Death.
- **Hope** — the butterfly-like flicker that remains/released, comforting humans and Prometheus.
- **Oral tradition** — stories passed by word of mouth, hence multiple versions.
- **Underworld** — the after-life realm where all mortals eventually go as ghosts.

### Things to remember
- The evils are real human pains, named one by one — the story's "why suffering exists."
- Hope is the smallest, most fragile force — and the one that survives.
- For tests: Hope **remained inside** (outline wording); for discussion: it can also be told
  as *released*.
