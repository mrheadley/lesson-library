# Lesson 10 — Constructing Evidence-Based Arguments: Claim, Quote, Explain
### Form 1 English B · Greek Myths · Unit 1

---

## The rule of this class

> **You cannot say *"Zeus is the worst god"* unless you can prove it from the story.**

That sentence — straight from the course — is the single most useful skill of the unit. It turns
every opinion into a tiny court case: make a claim, bring your evidence, explain the link. The
skill has a name we will use all term: **claim → quote → explain**. P-E-E for short (Point,
Evidence, Explain).

This lesson is where that skill stops being a vague idea and becomes a habit — the same habit
that powers the debate next lesson, the weekly journal, and Test 1.

---

## 1. The three moves, made simple

| Move | What you do | What it looks like |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Claim** | State your point in one arguable sentence | "Zeus acts more like a jealous school bully than a wise king." |
| **Quote** | Put your finger on the *shortest* phrase in the text that proves it | e.g. Zeus jealously complaining that the mud-men "look like us gods" |
| **Explain** | Connect the quote to the claim in one or two sentences | "If he were simply a law-giver, he would not care what shape his creations took. The quote shows he feels *threatened* — which is the behaviour of a jealous bully, not a secure ruler." |

That is the whole engine. Nothing fancy. Now the practice.

---

## 2. Practising: quote-mining like a pro

**Quote-mining** is the act of hunting through a text for the exact phrases that prove your
point. A few rules:

- **Short beats long.** The sharpest quote is the shortest phrase that carries the point. A
  twelve-word sentence beats a whole paragraph.
- **Attribution matters.** Name the speaker and the moment: "When Zeus says…", "Early in the
  story, the narrator tells us…"
- **Paraphrase is not evidence.** Restating the idea in your own words ("Zeus was jealous about
  the mud-men") shows understanding — but it is not *proof*. Only a direct quote, in quotation
  marks, is evidence.
- **Tailor the quote to the claim.** Find the phrase that *specifically* supports what you are
  arguing, not a famous line that only vaguely matches.

Now run the exercise on the story you already know.

---

## 3. Worked example (the class's evidence bank)

**Working claim:** *Zeus is cruel, not just strict.*

**Quote 1 (proving cruelty):** In the Pandora story Zeus builds a "reward" he plans as a trap —
he crafts a bride and gives "a wedding present" he knows is an iron-bound chest of trouble. A
careful quote-miner pulls the moment where he hands it over "with a grin on his face."
**Explain:** The grin is the giveaway — the punishment is a joke *to him*. A strict ruler
enforces rules; a cruel one enjoys the suffering.

**Quote 2 (proving the other side — mercy):** Near the end of the story, Hope survives the box,
comforting even Prometheus on his cliff, "No matter how bad things are today, tomorrow may be
better."
**Explain:** Even inside a world Zeus chose to sour, something good is officially allowed to
survive. That is a mercy, however small — which is why the honest answer about Zeus is
"complex," not "pure villain."

Notice the structure: each paragraph uses **one claim, one quote, one explain.** That rhythm is
the backbone of every strong answer in this unit and every journal entry.

---

## 4. The Reading Journal connection (your marking rubric, in plain English)

The journal entry you wrote in Lesson 09 (and the six to come) is graded on exactly these
nerves:

1. **Text-anchored** — does it point at the story (characters, scenes, words)?
2. **Thoughtful voice** — is it *your* thinking, in your own words?
3. **Structure & care** — readable, organised, well-presented?
4. **Bound to the claim** — does everything in the paragraph serve one idea?

A reflection that quotes "I am Hope. Without me the world won't be able to bear all the
unhappiness you have turned loose!" and then explains *why that quote changes the meaning of
the whole myth* is going to score higher than a reflection that only says "the box had lots of
bad stuff in it and also hope." Claim → quote → explain is how you turn "recognising the story"
into "analysing the story."

---

## 5. A trap to avoid: the "paragraph of summary"

The most common failure in evidence-based writing is not weak quoting — it is **summary
disguised as analysis**. Compare:

- **Summary:** "First Prometheus made the animals, then he made man, then he stole fire, then
  Zeus was angry, then he made Pandora…" (tells the events, adds nothing).
- **Analysis:** "Zeus punishes *the whole race* for one thief's act — crafting a weapon and
  calling it a gift. The text wants us to be uncomfortable with that scale of punishment."

Same story. One recites; the other *argues*. Every P-E-E paragraph you write should pass one
test: **if I deleted the quote and the explanation, would my teacher still know my claim?** If
yes, you are describing. If no, you are analysing.

---

## 6. Exit challenge (used in many lessons ahead)

Three sentence-starters to get you into the habit this week:

1. "I think Zeus is ________ because in the text he ________."
2. "The most important quote is '________' because ________."
3. "If '________' had not happened, then ________."

Master these three and the unit's debate (next lesson), its journal, and its tests all open up.

---

### Key terms for this lesson
- **Claim** — your arguable point (one sentence).
- **Quote** — the shortest phrase from the text that supports the point, in quotation marks.
- **Explain** — the sentence that links quote to claim.
- **Quote-mining** — deliberately hunting the text for proving phrases.
- **Paraphrase** — restating in your own words (useful, but not proof).

### Things to remember
- No claim without a quote; no quote without an explanation; no explanation without text.
- Short quotes beat long ones.
- Summary is not analysis — argue, don't recite.
- The journal rubric values the same P-E-E skill as the debate and the test.
