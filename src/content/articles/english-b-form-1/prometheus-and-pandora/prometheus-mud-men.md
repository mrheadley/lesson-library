# Lesson 07 — Prometheus's Mud-Men: The First Man and the Stolen Fire
### Form 1 English B · Greek Myths · Unit 1 · *In the Beginning and Pandora's Box* (part 1)

---

## The story so far

Zeus, king of the gods, looks down from Mount Olympus and sees an empty world. No birds, no
beasts, no people. So he gives his two brothers — **Prometheus** and **Epimetheus** — the job of
down to earth and filling it with living creatures.

That one sentence is the doorway into the first big story of the unit. What happens next looks
like a simple crafting job — and becomes the first crime in heaven.

---

## 1. Epimetheus, the creature-craftsman

Epimetheus gets to work immediately, and he is brilliant:

- The turtle gets its **shell**.
- The horse gets its **tail and mane**.
- The anteater gets its impossible **long nose and tongue**.
- The birds get the **gift of flight**.

Gift by gift, he equips every creature with what it needs to survive. Epimetheus is a wonderful
craftsman. But — and the story says this plainly — *he is not nearly as clever as his brother*
Prometheus.

His name is the giveaway. **Epimetheus means "afterthought."** He is the one who acts first and
thinks later.

---

## 2. Prometheus, the thinker — and First Man

**Prometheus means "forethought"** — thinking ahead. Every riddle of this story comes from
those two names. His brother hands out every available gift to the animals. Prometheus steps in
for the last creature — the one with nothing left.

He takes **soil and mud**, and moulds **First Man**.

And he does something no other craftsman did. He shapes Man upright — two legs, standing tall —
"just like us gods," looking up at the stars instead of down at the ground:

> *"I'll make him just like us gods — two legs, two arms, and upright — not crawling on all fours.
> All the other beasts spend their days looking at the ground, but Man will look at the stars."*

That line is the heart of the story. Prometheus's pride is not vanity — it is **aspiration**.
He has made a creature with dignity, one that looks *up*.

---

## 3. The theft of fire: the crime that had a good motive

When the gifts run out, First Man has nothing. Epimetheus tries to help: *"Give him a tail… a
trunk… some fur."* All gone. Every creature got one.

Prometheus suddenly knows exactly what Man needs most — the one gift that would change
everything. He climbs to heaven, all the way to the **fiery chariot of the sun**, and from the
rim of its bright wheel he steals **one tiny sliver of fire** — so small he hides it inside a
stalk of grass and hurries back to earth, and nobody sees him.

Fire. Divine fire. The "secret of fire." For the ancient Greeks, fire was not just warmth and
cooking — it was *the* civilising force: the thing that lifted humans out of darkness. Stealing
it from the gods was the single greatest gift one being could give another.

But it was also the single greatest act of disobedience to Zeus — and Zeus had a gift for
noticing.

---

## 4. Zeus's fury and the punishment

Zeus looks down and sees it: a glimmer of red and yellow under a column of grey smoke. Fire —
*on earth*.

- He is furious at **Prometheus** — for sharing the gods' belongings with "those mud-men."
- He is equally angry at **Man** for *accepting* the stolen gift — although, as the story says,
  "you would never have thought so." Zeus's outward manner stays pleasant. Plotting, even while
  enraged, is his style.
- He declares Prometheus made a fatal error: he put his "little mud-people" *before* the gods.

Zeus's punishment for Prometheus is terrible and permanent. Prometheus is chained to a cliff,
and every day the eagles come and peck at him. You or I would die — but **the gods can never
die**, so the pain never ends. The chains never break. The eagles never stop.

But something hurts him worse than the eagles: *hopelessness*. The knowledge that nothing will
ever change.

File that detail away. Hope — and its opposite — is the secret engine of this whole story
(Lesson 09).

---

## 5. What this lesson is really teaching

Deep under the surface of mud-men and stolen fire, the ancient Greeks were doing serious work
with this story. Notice these three hidden lessons:

1. **The explanation layer.** This myth explains the creation of animals, of men, and of women —
   and the *discovery of fire* in the human story.
2. **The human-ambition layer.** Fire marks the moment humans stopped being just another
   creature. The story celebrates that ambition — even while showing its cost.
3. **The pride layer.** Prometheus defies a god's authority. He acts from love for his
   creation, but he overreaches. The Greek word the ancients used for that overreaching is one
   you will meet again: **hubris** — excessive pride that invites a fall.

Prometheus pays for his hubris — while the world he loved pays for it too. That double cost is
the hook that pulls the *whole unit* forward.

---

### Key terms for this lesson
- **Prometheus** — "forethought"; the Titan who created First Man and stole fire.
- **Epimetheus** — "afterthought"; the brother who made the animals and never quite suspects what
  is coming.
- **Titan** — Prometheus's kind: the divine generation before the Olympian gods.
- **Hubris** — pride that overreaches and invites destruction.
- **Divine fire** — the gods' own fire; the secret of civilisation in the story.

### Things to remember
- Names matter: forethought vs. afterthought.
- Man is made from mud but shaped to look at the stars — dignity, not dust.
- Fire is stolen with a good motive and creates a huge punishment.
- Zeus is smiling on the outside and planning revenge on the inside.
