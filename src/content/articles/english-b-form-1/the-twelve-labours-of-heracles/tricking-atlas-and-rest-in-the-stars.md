# Lesson 33 — The Apples, the Giant, and the Weight of the Sky
### Form 1 English B · Greek Myths · Unit 3 · *The Twelve Labours of Heracles* (part 3)

---

## The story so far

Heracles has strangled kings' nightmares, burned the Hydra's necks, and washed the foulest
valley in Greece with a dammed river. Labours 6–8 (birds, a mad bull, wild horses — the text
rattles them off in a line) came and went, and even the belt of the Amazon Queen was won by
politeness rather than force.

One labour remains — the twelfth and greediest. And it turns the whole hero-test on its head:
**the final labour of the strongest man alive is won with a trick.**

---

## 1. The command — the apples of the Hesperides

Eurystheus, never satisfied, wants treasure: **the golden apples of the Hesperides**, magical
fruit that grow on a tree in a garden at the end of the world.

And around that tree coils something even Heracles won't fight:

> a dragon which never slept.

For once in his life, Heracles' courage balks. Even with all his strength, fighting an
ever-wakeful dragon guarding a garden at the world's edge looks like a death sentence. So the
hero does what the medium-strong do not: he decides to **ask**.

---

## 2. Atlas — the giant who holds the sky

The only person who can walk to the end of the world and stroll past that dragon is the giant
**Atlas** — and not because Augean rivers or Hydra-heads are his speed, but because he has a
worse job than any labour:

> Atlas was the biggest man in the world, and towered above houses, trees, cliffs and hills.
> He was so tall that the gods had given him the task of **holding up the sky and keeping the
> stars from falling.** The sun scorched his neck and the new moon shaved his beard. And for
> thousands of years he had stood in the one spot.

Atlas's garden knowledge is a family perk — the gardeners of the Hesperides' tree are his own
family. But when Heracles asks him to fetch the apples, Atlas objects with a comedian's timing:

> "How can I go to the end of the world? **How can I go *anywhere*?**"

He has a point. Which is exactly why Heracles makes him an offer no bored giant can refuse:

> "I could hold the sky for you while you were gone."

---

## 3. The swap — and the second trick

Atlas strides off, delighted. Heracles takes the sky on his back — and the text does not let
you miss the weight:

> though it was the heaviest burden he had ever carried.

The gardens are happy to hand over the apples. But on the long walk home, Atlas begins to taste
an emotion he hasn't felt in thousands of years: **freedom**. And he decides to keep it:

> "I've decided! I'm going to let you go on holding up the sky, and I'll deliver these apples
> to King Eurystheus."

Heracles — winded, bone-bent, underneath all of heaven — somehow stays calm. He does what the
most powerful person in history has to do. He plays the *small* request, the whimper disguised
as gratitude:

> "Fine. Thank you. It's a great honour to be allowed to hold up heaven. But if you could just
> help me get a **pad across my shoulders** before you go... these stars do prickle..."

The request is absurdly tiny. A cushion. A pad. And it depends on one thing: **Atlas must take
the sky back** — if only for the thirty seconds it takes Heracles to adjust his shoulders.

Down comes the sky onto Atlas again. Heracles, apples in both hands, **scurries away**:

> "Most grateful for your help. Perhaps next time, you'll get the better of me."

The strongest man alive did not beat the sky-lifter. He **tricked** the sky-lifter into holding
his own curse. That is wit beating giant strength — the exact lesson the Hydra and the stables
set up in Lesson 32, now landing as the finale.

---

## 4. The end of the labours — seven years at last

Seven years of labour end. Heracles is free — but the text finishes his story with a truthful,
sober note:

> But he was never free from his sorrow at taking that first glass of wine: not until the day
> he died.

The tragedy of Lesson 31 is not erased by the victories. Heracles paid for the murder of his
family for the rest of his life; the labours redeemed him *in the eyes of the world* but never
quieted *his own memory*.

## 5. Rest in the stars

And then the myth does something strange and generous for a hero:

> Being only a man and not a god, he did die. But the gods did not forget him. They **cut him
> out in stars and hung him in the sky**, to rest from his labours for all time, among the
> singing planets.

Heracles dies — mortals do — but the sky becomes his monument. To this day the constellation we
call **Hercules** is in the heavens; Greeks explained it as the hero's final resting place, as
the story-summaries put it — a place among the stars.

Collect the name-loans from this one hero, because the test loves them:

- **Atlas** → the giant who holds up the sky → and the book of maps called an **atlas** (and
  the **Atlas mountains**).
- **Heracles** (Greek) / **Hercules** (Roman) → a **Herculean task** (Lesson 32).

---

## 6. The image to keep in your pocket

**A giant, twelve stories tall, standing exactly where he has stood for a thousand years —
holding up empty sky — because the mightiest hero alive asked him, in a small voice, for a
cushion.**

The funniest picture in the unit is also its most important: the strongest man wins the
hardest labour not by lifting more, but by asking for a pad. When the problem is too big to
fight, **be the one who solves it sideways.**

---

### Key terms for this lesson
- **Hesperides** — the garden at the end of the world, home of the golden apples.
- **Atlas** — the giant who holds up the sky; family connection to the apple garden.
- **Burden** — a heavy load (the sky itself; also the symbolic weight of Heracles's crime).
- **Cunning / wit** — cleverness that wins where strength alone would fail.
- **Catasterism** — (advanced vocabulary) the placement of a hero among the stars; Heracles's
  reward.
- **Pad / cushion** — the tiny request that flips the whole bargain — the lesson's key detail.

### Things to remember
- The 12th labour: fetch the golden apples of the Hesperides, guarded by an ever-wakeful dragon.
- Heracles doesn't fight; he asks Atlas (whose family tends the garden) to fetch the apples.
- Heracles takes the sky on his back; Atlas, loving freedom, decides not to take it back.
- Heracles wins by asking for a *pad for his shoulders* — forcing Atlas to grab the sky, then
  running off with the apples.
- The labours take seven years; Heracles is free but never free of the sorrow of Lesson 31.
- The gods set him among the stars — the constellation Hercules; Atlas lives on in *atlas*.
