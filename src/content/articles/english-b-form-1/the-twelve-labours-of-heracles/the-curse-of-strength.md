# Lesson 31 — The Strongest Man Alive, and the Promise He Broke
### Form 1 English B · Greek Myths · Unit 3 · *The Twelve Labours of Heracles* (part 1)

---

## The story so far

Perseus ended his unit by showing you the ideal hero's shape: brave, clever, helped by the
gods — and still unable to escape his fate.

Now the course introduces its most famous hero of all — a man whose name you already know from
the word **"Herculean."** His story is Unit 3's warning shot: it asks what happens when the
strongest person in the world has a weakness he refuses to believe in.

The answer, as you will see, is ruin — and then a very long bill.

---

## 1. Heracles or Hercules?

First, the name. You have probably heard "**Hercules**." That is the **Roman** form. The course
(and the Greek world) uses **Heracles**. Same hero, two languages.

His birth comes with a feud already loaded. Heracles is the son of **Zeus** — born to a mother,
**Alcmene**, and this infuriates **Hera**, Zeus's wife and the goddess of marriage. The insult
goes a step further: the baby's birth name is **Alcides**, but his mother names him **Heracles**
— which literally means **'Glory of Hera'** — as a deliberate slap in the goddess's face.

Hera never forgives him. She "spent a lot of energy to make Heracles have a horrible life."
Keep that fact — it explains two different tellings of the tragedy to come.

---

## 2. The baby who strangled snakes

Heracles's strength arrives absurdly early. With him still in the cradle:

> When huge snakes slithered into his crib to strangle him, he knotted and plaited them as if
> they were pieces of string, and threw them out again.

That is the unit's founding image of the hero: born powerful beyond human scale. And the text
immediately adds the crucial qualifier —

> For Heracles was strong—fantastically strong—stronger than you and me and a hundred others put
> together. Fortunately, he was also **gentle and kind**, so that his friends had nothing to fear
> from him.

Strength, kindness. Strong and kind — a fair start for a hero.

---

## 3. The promise about drink

Enter the schoolmaster — the minor character who sets the whole tragedy in motion:

> "If you were ever to get drunk, Heracles," the schoolmaster said, "who knows what terrible
> thing you might do with that great strength of yours!"

Heracles promises: **never to touch alcoholic drink**.

Read the warning as the myth's engine. The danger was never the drink itself — it was the
*dangerous thing a giant of a man could do* while drunk. Strength makes a single bad choice
infinitely worse.

---

## 4. The broken promise — the family on the floor

Heracles intends to keep his word. And then:

> His friends all drank at parties, his family always had wine with their meals: it seemed
> foolish for Heracles to ask for fruit juice or water.

So he is tempted. One glass. Then another. And another. The text is pitiless:

> Soon he was roaring drunk, throwing punches in all directions. When the wine's work was done,
> **Heracles' own family lay dead on the floor**, and Heracles was an outcast hated by everyone
> and most of all by himself.

Pause there. The strongest man alive could not keep the one promise that mattered — and the
price was the people he loved most. Every labour in the next two lessons is a **consequence of
this night**, not a reward for it.

---

## 5. Two tellings of how it happened

The course is honest that the story comes down in two versions:

- **Version A (the core story text — teach this one):** it happens through **drunkenness**, exactly
  as above. Heracles breaks the promise, gets drunk, and destroys his family. This matches the
  course outline: "breaking his promise on alcohol, getting drunk, and killing his family."
- **Version B (the slides):** **Hera** sends him into a fit of madness in which he kills his
  wife and sons. In this telling he goes to the Oracle at Delphi, where Hera's influence
  dictates his punishment.

Both versions converge on the same sentence of redemption: **he must complete twelve labours to
atone.**

---

## 6. The penance — seven years and King Eurystheus

For his crime, Heracles is condemned to serve King **Eurystheus** — his own cousin, and a
"mean, spiteful man" — as a slave for **seven years**.

Eurystheus is not a noble master. His kingdom is overrun with problems, and he does what any
bully would: he sets Heracles the most dangerous tasks he can think of, hoping the demigod dies.

The outline names this idea's idiom already — **"a Herculean task"**: an extremely difficult job
requiring immense effort and strength. The whole next two lessons are a parade of them. But now
you know what sits behind the idiom: **each task is another few years of the bill for one
broken promise.**

---

## 7. The image to keep in your pocket

Remember this: **a giant of a man kneeling among his own dead family, asking what he must do to
pay for it — and being handed twelve impossible tasks.**

Heracles is the strongest hero in the Greek world. And his story's first lesson is that the
strongest people can still wreck everything — when they are too proud to fear their own
weakness.

---

### Key terms for this lesson
- **Heracles / Hercules** — the same hero; Greek name (Heracles) vs. Roman name (Hercules).
- **Fatal flaw** — a weakness inside a hero that drives their downfall; Heracles's is pride plus
  a broken promise about drink.
- **Penance / atonement** — the payment for a wrong; Heracles's twelve labours.
- **Eurystheus** — Heracles's cousin-king; the spiteful master who sets the twelve labours.
- **Demigod** — half-god, half-mortal (Heracles is a son of Zeus).
- **Hera** — Zeus's wife, goddess of marriage; Heracles's lifelong enemy.

### Things to remember
- Hercules = Roman name; Heracles = Greek name; the course uses Heracles.
- Baby Heracles strangles the snakes in his crib — strength from birth.
- The schoolmaster's warning: never drink — "who knows what terrible thing you might do."
- Heracles breaks the promise, gets drunk, and kills his own family.
- Two versions: drunkenness (core text) or Hera's madness (slides); both end in the 12 labours.
- Penalty: seven years as slave to spiteful King Eurystheus.
