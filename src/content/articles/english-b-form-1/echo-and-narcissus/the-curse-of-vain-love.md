# Lesson 18 — The Curse of Vain Love
### Form 1 English B · Greek Myths · Unit 2 · *Echo and Narcissus* (part 3)

---

## The story so far

Echo has faded to a voice, her "wicked, wordless wish" made: that Narcissus should one day love
as she loved and suffer as she suffered. Narcissus is still striding through the world, too
vain to love anyone. Somewhere on Olympus, Hera is beginning to regret her temper.

One of those three threads is about to snap.

---

## 1. The pool that becomes a mirror

One hot day, Narcissus goes to drink. At the centre of the forest there is a **pool** — clear,
still water, "as bright as silver."

He leans over the edge to drink... and freezes.

There, looking up at him, is the most beautiful face he has ever seen.

It is his own reflection.

Narcissus does not know this — or will not accept it. He falls in love with the face in the
water **exactly as Echo fell in love with him**: instantly, completely, hopelessly. The more
he looks, the more he likes what he sees.

---

## 2. The love that can never be returned

Now watch the tragedy assemble itself, piece by piece:

- Narcissus longs to kiss the lips in the water. He leans forward and **kisses the water** —
  and the ripples shatter the face.
- He plunges his hand in — and the reflection dissolves again.
- He waits for the water to still, until the face floats back — and then he cannot stop
  looking.

He is in love with a picture that vanishes the moment he touches it. He cannot reach it, cannot
keep it, cannot feed or marry or ease it.

This is the "love as I loved, suffer as I suffered" that Echo wished for — and it is being
served to Narcissus exactly as ordered.

---

## 3. Taking root — the transformation

Narcissus does not drown. He does not run away. He does something stranger and more
permanent:

> He **took root where he sat** and pined away in hopeless love.

Day after day he gazes at his own face in the water, wasting away with longing, until at
last his body is gone — and in its place stands a single slender flower, leaning out over the
pool's edge.

The flower is the **narcissus** — pale yellow and white — and it has one final trick from the
story's design: it bends toward the water's surface, **as if admiring its own reflection**,
just as the boy did.

A boy who loved his own image has become a flower that does the same thing forever.

---

## 4. Hera's regret

Back on Olympus, Hera is not triumphant. The story says plainly:

> Hera regretted her temper — and sent her handmaidens to look for Echo and forgive her.

The handmaidens search the woods, calling "Echo! Echo!" through the trees. And the only
answer is the breeze returning their own words to them.

The myth is closing its circle: the curse that made Echo a voice has made sure that even her
forgiveness can never reach her in words of her own. All that is left of the chatty nymph is
the echo — and in a moment that would be unbearably sad, the story lets the wind whisper one
more name instead: **"Narcissus! Narcissus!"**

The flower is named for the boy who never loved anyone — and the echo is named for the girl who
loved him far too much. Neither name is a coincidence.

---

## 5. The two lessons that complete the myth

The course collects this myth's morals into two clear statements — one for each victim:

> **Don't act hastily — you might regret it later.** (Hera's curse, from Lesson 16.)

> **Vanity can lead to destruction.** (Narcissus's end, this lesson.)

Notice how precisely the two halves fit the two characters:

| Character | Flaw | The cost |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Hera** | Hasty temper | Regret she can never undo |
| **Echo** | Last-word habit | Cursed to repeat, then fades to a voice |
| **Narcissus** | Vanity | Loves his reflection, wastes away, becomes a flower |

Three people, three flaws, three costs — and one shared truth: **everyone in this myth lost,
because nobody stopped to think before they acted or loved.**

---

## 6. Love that cannot be returned — the deep lesson

Underneath the magic, the myth is working on a very human fear: **what it means to love
someone who will never love you back.**

- Echo loves Narcissus → rejected, fades.
- Narcissus loves his reflection → unrequited, pines, becomes a flower.

The two ruined lovers end the story exactly alike: **one a voice, one a flower.** The symmetry
is the point. The Greeks are not just telling you a spooky transformation — they are telling
you that a life spent loving the wrong thing, or loving the right thing at the wrong distance,
unravels you in the end.

### Key terms for this lesson
- **Reflection** — the image in the water that Narcissus mistakes for a real person.
- **Pine away** — waste away from longing (Echo and Narcissus both do this).
- **Transformation** — the body becoming something new (Narcissus becomes a flower).
- **Narcissus (flower)** — the pale yellow-and-white flower that leans over the water, named
  for the boy.
- **Symmetry** — the story's design: Echo becomes a voice, Narcissus becomes a flower.

### Things to remember
- Narcissus falls in love with his own reflection — the moment he touches it, it shatters.
- He takes root where he sat and wastes away — he becomes the narcissus flower.
- The flower leans over the water "as if admiring its own reflection" — the myth never lets the
  vanity go.
- Hera regrets her hasty curse — but cannot undo it. "Don't act hastily."
- Vanity leads to destruction — the myth's second half.
- Echo ends as a voice; Narcissus ends as a flower: love that cannot be returned unravels you.
