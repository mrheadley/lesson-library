# Lesson 01 — What is a Myth?
### Form 1 English B · Greek Myths · Unit 1

---

## Open with a question

Every culture on Earth has asked the same four questions. Where did the world come from? Why is
nature the way it is? Why do bad things happen to good people? What happens after we die?

Two thousand five hundred years ago, a person on a Greek hillside could not open Google — so she asked her grandfather, who had heard the story from *his* grandfather. What he told her was a **myth**: a story that explains something mysterious by turning it into a drama full of gods, monsters, heroes, and lessons.

A myth is not "a lie." For the people who told it, a myth was the closest thing they had to a
textbook — and a moral compass — and a movie franchise — rolled into one.

---

## 1. What exactly is a myth?

Here is a working definition you can underline in your notes:

> **A myth is a traditional story that expresses a culture's beliefs and values. It explains
> things the culture cannot yet explain by science — the origins of the world, the nature of
> good and evil, life and death, and the doings of the gods.**

Three job descriptions for a myth:

| Job | What it does | Greek example |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Explainer** | Answers "why does X happen?" with a story | Why is there winter? → Persephone's six pomegranate seeds |
| **Teacher** | Shows approved and forbidden behaviour | Don't boast → Arachne's spider curse |
| **Keeper** | Passes the culture's beliefs and values to the next generation | The gods are powerful but flawed → be careful, be humble |

A famous summary the class uses: mythology is *the collection of stories which deal with the
human condition, good versus evil, human origins, life and death, the afterlife, and the gods.*
The word "****mythology****" means "the study of myths," and a single one of these stories is a
single "myth."

---

## 2. Mount Olympus: the gods' house with very thin walls

The Greek gods did not live in a faraway heaven. They lived on **Mount Olympus** — a real mountain in Greece — in "halls of sunlight and cloud." That closeness matters: the gods could see everything humans did, visit earth whenever they liked, and meddle in human lives for
very human reasons.

**King of the gods: Zeus.** He ruled the sky and threw lightning. But here is the twist the
Greeks took very seriously: **the gods were not perfect.** They were immensely powerful — but
they were also vain, jealous, impulsive, bored, and often ridiculous. They argued. They cheated. They took offence. They were, in other words, *very much like people, with god-sized power and god-sized consequences.*

This is why the myths work as lessons at all. A perfect god would be a poor teacher for
humans; a flawed god makes a mirror you can see yourself in.

**Olympus in your world today:** the word "Olympics" comes straight from Mount Olympus, and the
first Olympic games were held in honour of Zeus over 2,000 years ago. (More footprints in
Lesson 02.)

---

## 3. Fate: the one boss even the gods obey

For all their power, the Greek gods did not control everything. Above them stood **Fate** — three sister-goddesses born to Zeus and Themis who controlled every mortal's life:

- One **spun the thread of life** and set the hour of birth.
- One **measured the thread** and spun out the events of the life.
- One **cut the thread** at the moment of death.

Nobody could change what Fate had spun. This idea — that your future is decided ahead of you,
whether you like it or not — loops through the whole term. It will come back fiercely in the
Perseus story (a king who tries to escape his fate and cannot).

---

## 4. A worked example: how to read a myth

Take one tiny beat from Pandora's story (you will read it in Lessons 07–09). Zeus is annoyed that humans exist. His reaction is *not* to fix the problem calmly — it is to craft a beautiful trap, hand it to a human as a "gift," and grin. Read that in three layers:

1. **Literal layer** — what happens? A box is delivered with a warning not to open it.
2. **Explanation layer** — what does this explain? Why evil and trouble exist in the world.
3. **Lesson layer** — what is the belief or value? Be careful of gifts from enemies; curiosity can have dangerous consequences; never lose hope.

**This three-layer reading is the skill of the whole unit.** Every story we meet, from
Pandora to the Wooden Horse, rewards reading at all three levels.

---

## 5. A final thought (keep this idea in your pocket)

The Greeks were not stupid people who believed obvious nonsense. They were brilliant people who lacked two things we have: science and mass media. Their myths were how they did **both jobs at once** — understanding their world and teaching their children. When you study Greek myth in an
English class, you are not studying ancient history. You are studying the most successful
storytelling franchise of all time — and it is still running today.

---

### Key terms for this lesson
- **Myth** — a traditional story that explains the world and teaches a culture's values.
- **Mount Olympus** — the mountain home of the Greek gods.
- **Zeus** — king of the gods, ruler of the sky.
- **Fate** — the three sisters who control every mortal's life, birth to death.
- **Greek gods** — powerful but flawed, vain, jealous, and sometimes silly.

### Things to remember
- A myth is an explanation + a lesson, not a lie.
- The gods are powerful and imperfect — that is the point.
- Every myth can be read at three levels: what happens, what it explains, what it teaches.
