# Lesson 23 — The Tapestry Battle
### Form 1 English B · Greek Myths · Unit 2 · *Arachne* (part 2)

---

## The story so far

Arachne boasted she could out-weave any god. The old woman she insulted threw off her disguise
and revealed herself as **Athene**, goddess of wisdom and household crafts — and the challenge
was accepted on the spot. A mortal's loom against a goddess's. The whole fair is watching.

Now they weave.

---

## 1. Two tapestries, two weapons

The contest is not just a measure of skill. Each weaver makes a *choice of subject* — and
that choice is a weapon.

**Athene's tapestry** — the safe one, the flattering one:

- She weaves the **courts of Olympus** — the gods and goddesses at their finest.
- She frames their greatest deeds, their honour, their glory.
- She finishes the border in threads of **gold**.

It is beautiful, respectful… and endlessly, deliberately safe. Notice what Athene has done: she
has chosen a subject that flatters the audience she is weaving for. She is not showing off —
she is paying homage.

**Arachne's tapestry** — the dangerous one, the satire:

- She weaves the gods' **worst moments** — their shame, their crimes, the stories they would
  rather forget.
- The subjects are mocking sketches of Olympus's most humiliating scenes.
- She finishes with a glorious, **golden border**, as if adding a frame of swagger to an insult.

Arachne has not chosen a subject. She has chosen a **groin-kick wrapped in gold thread**. Her
weaving says: *not only can I out-weave you — here is what I think of you, woven in silk.*

---

## 2. Satire — the art of the pointed attack

Here is the concept the contest stands on, so give it a proper definition:

> **Satire** is art that mocks or attacks something — a person, an idea, a habit — by pointing
> out its flaws, often with humour.

Arachne's tapestry is satire. She does not fight with fists or words; she fights with pictures:
the gods caught in their embarrassing moments, stitched in perfect thread, framed in gold.

Satire is the strongest weapon a powerless artist has — and the most dangerous one to use
against the powerful. Arachne has just done to the gods of Greece what no army could do.

---

## 3. The verdict

The looms stop. The two tapestries hang side by side. Even the crowd, braced for the worst,
has to admit both are staggering.

And now the story makes its single sharpest point. Athene looks at Arachne's work — and
judges it honestly:

> Athene had to admit that **Arachne's hand was the more skilful** — but her **subject-matter
> was an outrage.**

Read that carefully. The verdict is **split**:

- **Skill:** Arachne wins. Her hand is better. The goddess says so herself.
- **Subject:** Arachne loses. Her *content* is an outrage — an insult to heaven, whatever the
  stitches say.

The contest is not a clean win. It is a knife right down the middle: genius with the needle,
foolishness with the meaning. The myth is refusing to let you have a simple answer. Arachne is
the best — and she is also in monstrous trouble.

---

## 4. Why the verdict matters

This is the moment to stop and think like a mythology student. Why does the story split the
verdict like this? Because the ancients wanted you to see **two different things at once**:

1. **Talent is real.** Arachne genuinely is the finest weaver alive. The story will not let
   pride erase her gift — that is a warning about arrogance, not about skill.
2. **What you do with talent is a choice.** Arachne chose to use her gift as a weapon against
   the gods. Brilliant craftsmanship did not make the attack wise. Skill without humility is
   still dangerous — to the skilled.

The verdict gives the story its engine: this is not a fable about "the bad weaver who was
actually bad." It is a fable about "the best weaver who chose the worst thing to weave."

---

## 5. A word the myth gives to science

Before the curtain (Lesson 24), collect the story's second gift to the modern world — biology
this time instead of psychology:

> **Arachnid** — the class of animals that includes spiders — is named after **Arachne**.

The ancient weaver's name is baked into Latin scientific vocabulary. Every time a scientist
writes about a spider's eight legs, they are writing about Arachne. The girl who wove better
than a goddess is, in a strange way, still weaving — now on every spider's web in the world.

Remember the pair of name-loans Unit 2 has given you:

- **Narcissus** → narcissism (psychology)
- **Arachne** → arachnid (biology)

The myths keep turning their heroes into living vocabulary.

---

## 6. The pause before the storm

The contest is over. The verdict has been spoken. The crowd is silent — because everyone can
feel what is coming.

Arachne has just won the weaving *and* lost her life in the same sentence. The gods do not lose
arguments gracefully, and they do not forgive artists who stitch their shame in gold thread.

Lesson 24 asks the question the judge has just placed in everyone's mind: **what happens to a
girl who wins a contest against a goddess — but wins it the wrong way?**

---

### Key terms for this lesson
- **Tapestry** — a woven picture-hanging; the story's art-form.
- **Satire** — art that mocks or attacks something, often with humour.
- **Flattery** — praise aimed to please, often more than is deserved (Athene's tapestry).
- **Verdict** — the judge's decision: skill to Arachne, outrage to her subject.
- **Arachnid** — the class of spiders, named after Arachne.

### Things to remember
- Athene weaves Olympus at its best — flattery, framed in gold.
- Arachne weaves the gods' worst moments — satire, framed in gold.
- The verdict splits: Arachne's hand is more skilful, but her subject is an outrage.
- Talent is real; what you do with talent is a choice.
- "Arachnid" — spiders — is named after Arachne. The myth gifts biology a word.
- The verdict sets up the punishment (Lesson 24).
